# Unit 18 — The Transaction Lifecycle, Trust Funds & Career Launch

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Walk** a transaction from first contact through closing, identifying each party's role.
2. **Define** trust funds and **apply** the three-business-day deposit rule.
3. **Distinguish** commingling from conversion and state the consequences of each.
4. **Describe** trust account recordkeeping: the control record, beneficiary ledgers, and monthly reconciliation.
5. **Calculate** closing prorations and complete debit and credit entries for buyer and seller.
6. **Explain** record retention requirements and how to prepare for a DRE audit.
7. **Evaluate** brokerages against criteria that matter for a new licensee.
8. **Synthesize** the course: apply concepts from all eighteen units to an integrated transaction.

## 1. The Transaction, End to End

Everything in this course converges here. A residential sale moves through predictable stages, and your job is to know what happens at each and who is responsible.

**Stage 1 — Pre-listing / pre-approach.** Establish agency in writing (Unit 6): agency disclosure, then listing agreement or buyer representation agreement (Unit 8). For sellers, gather disclosures early (Unit 9) and order the preliminary report so title problems surface before you have a buyer, not after (Unit 10). For buyers, get a real preapproval — not a prequalification (Unit 12).

**Stage 2 — Marketing and showing.** Advertising must be compliant (Units 1 and 15) and truthful. Every showing carries the visual inspection duty (Unit 6). Every interaction is governed by fair housing (Unit 15).

**Stage 3 — Offer and negotiation.** Offer, counteroffer, acceptance, and the rule that a counteroffer terminates the original offer (Unit 7). Confirm agency in writing in the contract. Deliver the deposit into escrow or the trust account within **three business days** (Section 2 below).

**Stage 4 — Escrow opened.** Escrow instructions signed, preliminary report ordered, demands requested from lienholders, disclosures delivered and receipted (Unit 9), inspections scheduled.

**Stage 5 — Contingency period.** Investigation, appraisal, loan, title, and HOA document review. Written contingency removals, with the seller's remedy being a notice to perform rather than automatic cancellation (Unit 8). This is the highest-liability window in the transaction: **calendar every deadline the day of acceptance.**

**Stage 6 — Loan and appraisal.** Underwriting, appraisal (Unit 13), and the Closing Disclosure delivered at least three business days before consummation (Unit 12).

**Stage 7 — Closing.** Final walk-through, signing, funding, **recording** — and title passes at recording, not at signing (Unit 10). Prorations settle taxes, rents, and HOA dues.

**Stage 8 — Post-closing.** Deliver files, follow up on the supplemental tax bill your buyer will receive (Unit 16), retain records, and — practically — begin the relationship that produces your next three transactions.

**The cast:** listing broker, buyer's broker, escrow holder (neutral, dual agent before closing), title insurer, lender, appraiser, home inspector, pest inspector, transaction coordinator, and — behind all of it — the **responsible broker** who supervises and is accountable for the licensed activity of everyone in the brokerage.

## 2. Trust Funds — the Rules That End Careers

More California licenses are lost over trust fund violations than almost anything else, and the violations are usually sloppiness rather than theft.

**Definition.** **Trust funds** are money or things of value received by a broker or salesperson **on behalf of another** in a transaction: earnest money deposits, rents, security deposits, and funds for future costs. They belong to someone else. They never belong to you.

**The three-business-day rule.** Trust funds must be placed into **escrow**, a **trust account**, or the **hands of the principal** entitled to them, **not later than three business days** after receipt by the broker or the broker's salesperson. <!-- VERIFY: current regulation text and business-day counting -->

**The uncashed-check exception.** When a buyer's offer instructs that the deposit check be held uncashed pending acceptance, the broker may hold it — but **the seller must be informed** of that instruction, and once the offer is accepted, the three-business-day clock runs from **acceptance**.

**Non-trust funds.** A broker's own commission earned and payable is not a trust fund, but the moment it sits in a trust account beyond a limited period it creates a commingling problem. A broker may keep a small amount of personal funds in a trust account to cover bank service charges — historically **$200** — and that limited allowance is the only intentional mixing permitted. <!-- VERIFY: current permitted amount -->

### Commingling vs. conversion

These two words appear on every California exam, and students mix them up.

**Commingling** is **mixing** trust funds with the broker's own funds. Examples: depositing an earnest money check into the brokerage operating account; leaving earned commissions in the trust account beyond the permitted period; paying business expenses from the trust account.

> Commingling is a violation **even if no client loses a penny** and **even if the funds are restored the same day**. The offense is the mixing itself.

**Conversion** is **using** trust funds — actually spending client money on something else. Conversion is far more serious: it supports license revocation and criminal charges.

Every conversion involves commingling; not every commingling involves conversion. A broker who parks a deposit in the operating account over a weekend has commingled. A broker who uses it to make payroll has converted.

### Recordkeeping

A broker holding trust funds must maintain:

- **A control record** (columnar record) of all trust funds received and disbursed, in chronological order.
- **A separate record (ledger) for each beneficiary or transaction**, showing every receipt and disbursement affecting that beneficiary's balance.
- **Monthly reconciliation** of the control record, the sum of all beneficiary ledgers, and the bank statement. All three must agree. The reconciliation must be in writing and retained.

Additional rules: the account must be designated a **trust account** in the broker's name as trustee, at a California bank, **non-interest-bearing** unless specific conditions are met and the interest passes to the beneficiary; the broker must authorize signatories in writing, and an unlicensed employee may be a signatory only if bonded; and **no trust account may ever go negative for any beneficiary** — using one client's money to cover another's shortfall is conversion.

**Record retention: three years** for transaction records generally, running from closing or from the listing date if no closing occurred. <!-- VERIFY: current retention period and whether it differs by record type; CLAUDE.md §3.2 assumes at least 5 years for school records, a separate requirement -->

### The DRE audit

The DRE audits brokerages, sometimes routinely and sometimes on complaint. Auditors ask for the control record, the beneficiary ledgers, monthly reconciliations, bank statements, and the transaction files. The common findings are mundane: reconciliations not performed, ledgers not maintained per beneficiary, deposits made late, personal funds left in the account, and missing signed receipts.

**The practical protection is boring and effective:** deposit immediately, reconcile monthly in writing, keep a ledger per beneficiary, and never let the account balance drop below the total owed to beneficiaries.

## 3. Closing Math and Settlement Statements

At closing, escrow prepares a settlement statement with **debits** (charges) and **credits** (amounts in your favor) for each party.

**The logic:**

- A **debit** to a party is money that party **owes or pays**.
- A **credit** to a party is money that party **receives or has already paid**.
- The buyer's debits include the purchase price; the buyer's credits include the deposit and loan proceeds.
- The seller's credits include the purchase price; the seller's debits include the loan payoff, commissions, and costs.

**Prorations** divide ongoing items as of closing. The convention matters — a **360-day year with 30-day months** is common unless the contract says otherwise, and **who owns the day of closing** must be stated.

| Item | Situation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Property taxes | Seller **prepaid** | **Credit seller / debit buyer** |
| Property taxes | **Unpaid** and accrued | **Debit seller / credit buyer** |
| Rents | Seller **collected** for the month | **Debit seller / credit buyer** for buyer's days |
| Security deposits | Held by seller | **Debit seller / credit buyer** (full amount) |
| HOA dues | Prepaid | **Credit seller / debit buyer** |
| Interest on assumed loan | Accrued, unpaid | **Debit seller / credit buyer** |

Security deposits transfer **in full** — they are the tenants' money and are never prorated.

## 4. Choosing a Brokerage

Your first brokerage decision shapes your first two years more than any other choice.

**What actually matters:**

**Training and mentorship.** For a new licensee this outranks commission split by a wide margin. A 60/40 split with a broker who reviews your contracts and takes your calls is worth far more than 90/10 with no one to ask. Ask specifically: who reviews my first contract? Who do I call on a Sunday when a deal is falling apart?

**Supervision quality.** The responsible broker is legally accountable for your licensed activity (Unit 1). A broker who takes that seriously protects you.

**Commission structure.** Understand the whole picture: split, cap, desk fees, transaction fees, technology fees, E&O insurance cost, and franchise fees. Model it honestly against your realistic first-year transaction count — not your optimistic one.

**Lead generation.** Does the brokerage provide leads, or is it entirely self-generated? Neither is wrong, but know which you are signing up for.

**Culture and specialization.** A brokerage strong in luxury coastal listings may not serve you well if you plan to build a first-time-buyer business in the Inland Empire.

**Realistic economics for year one.** Budget for: license and application fees, association and MLS dues, E&O insurance, lockbox access, signs and marketing, transaction management software, vehicle and fuel, and — critically — **six to twelve months of living expenses.** Commissions arrive at closing, and your first closing may be three or four months out. The single most common reason new agents fail is running out of money before the pipeline matures, not lack of ability.

**Build the business on systems from day one:** a CRM you actually use, a database of everyone you know, a consistent follow-up cadence, and a standardized process applied identically to every client — which is also your best defense against implicit bias (Unit 15).

## 5. Capstone: An Integrated Transaction

A Covina transaction, touching most of the course.

**The setup.** Maria lists her late mother's home, held in a family trust with Maria as successor trustee. She has a buyer's agent friend and asks you to represent her.

**Unit 4 & 10 — Vesting.** Title is vested in the trustee. Escrow will require a **certification of trust** evidencing Maria's authority. Confirm this before marketing.

**Unit 9 — Disclosures.** The sale may be **exempt from the TDS** as a fiduciary transfer — but Maria lived in the house for six years and **knows the north wall leaks.** She must disclose the known material fact regardless of the form exemption.

**Unit 5 & 10 — Preliminary report.** It shows a 1953 CC&R containing a racially restrictive covenant (void and unenforceable — Unit 5 and 15, with a statutory process to strike it), a utility easement in gross along the rear, and an abstract of judgment against a person with a similar name. The judgment is likely a **name variance** resolved by a statement of information — handle it in week one, not week four.

**Unit 8 — Offers.** Two offers arrive. Maria counters both using a **multiple counteroffer** so she does not accidentally bind to two buyers. The accepted buyer's **$25,000 deposit** reaches your brokerage Friday at 4 p.m. with instructions to hold pending acceptance; acceptance comes Monday. **Your three-business-day clock runs from Monday**, and you inform the seller of the hold instruction.

**Unit 15 — Fair housing.** Maria mentions she'd prefer "a nice family, not investors." Preferring owner-occupants is lawful; preferring a "family" implicates familial status. You address it plainly and document that offers were presented without regard to protected characteristics.

**Unit 13 — Appraisal.** The appraisal comes in **$18,000 below** contract. The buyer's appraisal contingency is live. Options: renegotiate, buyer covers the gap, split it, or cancel. You present market data; you do not contact the appraiser to request a number (Unit 13).

**Unit 12 & 11 — Financing.** The buyer's conventional loan is at 88% LTV, so **PMI applies**. The existing loan on the property is a purchase-money loan being paid off at closing — no assumption, so the full price is subject to **documentary transfer tax**.

**Unit 16 — Taxes.** The property reassesses to the sale price; the buyer will receive a **supplemental bill**. Because the transfer is from a trust following death and the buyer is unrelated, no reassessment exclusion applies. Maria should see a **CPA** about the estate's basis — you flag it and refer.

**Unit 10 — Closing.** Sale price **$845,000**, closing **June 20**, buyer owns the day of closing. Annual taxes of **$9,180** were prepaid by the estate for the fiscal year beginning July 1.

**Proration walkthrough:**
1. Daily: $9,180 ÷ 360 = **$25.50**
2. Seller owned July 1 – June 19 = 11 months + 19 days = 330 + 19 = **349 days**
3. Seller's share: 349 × $25.50 = **$8,899.50**
4. Prepaid balance benefiting the buyer: $9,180 − $8,899.50 = **$280.50**
5. **Credit seller / debit buyer $280.50**

**Transfer tax:** $845,000 ÷ 500 = 1,690 × $0.55 = **$929.50**

**Commission:** at 5% total, $845,000 × 0.05 = **$42,250**, split per agreement — paid by escrow to the **brokerages**, then to the salespersons (Unit 1).

**Unit 10 — Title passes on recording**, not on signing. Escrow disburses after recording confirms.

That single transaction touched fifteen of the eighteen units. That is what this course was building toward.

## 6. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Trust funds** | Money or things of value received on behalf of another in a transaction — deposits, rents, security deposits. |
| **Three-business-day rule** | Trust funds must be placed in escrow, a trust account, or the principal's hands within three business days of receipt. |
| **Uncashed-check exception** | Permission to hold a deposit check pending acceptance when the offer so instructs and the seller is informed; the clock then runs from acceptance. |
| **Commingling** | Mixing trust funds with the broker's own funds; a violation even without loss or with same-day restoration. |
| **Conversion** | Actually using trust funds for another purpose; grounds for revocation and criminal charges. |
| **Control record** | The chronological columnar record of all trust funds received and disbursed. |
| **Beneficiary ledger** | A separate record for each beneficiary or transaction showing all receipts, disbursements, and the running balance. |
| **Monthly reconciliation** | The written monthly agreement of the control record, the sum of beneficiary ledgers, and the bank statement. |
| **Record retention** | The period a broker must keep transaction records — three years for brokerage transaction records. |
| **Debit / credit** | An amount a party owes or pays / an amount a party receives or has already paid. |
| **Proration** | Division of ongoing expenses and income between buyer and seller as of closing. |
| **Security deposit transfer** | Tenant deposits pass to the buyer in full at closing and are never prorated. |
| **Settlement statement** | The escrow accounting showing each party's debits and credits at closing. |
| **Responsible broker** | The broker accountable for supervising licensed activity and for trust funds handled by anyone in the brokerage. |
| **E&O insurance** | Errors and omissions coverage protecting licensees against claims arising from professional services. |

## 7. Exam Tips

- **Three business days** for trust funds into escrow, a trust account, or the principal's hands. When a check is properly held uncashed pending acceptance, the clock runs **from acceptance**.
- **Commingling = mixing; conversion = using.** Commingling is a violation even with no loss and immediate restoration. This distinction appears on essentially every exam.
- **The trust account may never go negative for any beneficiary.** Covering one client's shortfall with another's funds is conversion.
- **Monthly written reconciliation of three things:** control record, sum of beneficiary ledgers, bank statement.
- **Three-year record retention** for brokerage transaction records.
- **Proration direction:** prepaid by seller → credit seller, debit buyer. Accrued and unpaid → debit seller, credit buyer. **Security deposits transfer in full**, never prorated.
- **Title passes at recording**, not at signing or funding.
- **A salesperson is paid only by their employing broker** — the rule from Unit 1 that closes the loop here.
- **A form exemption is never a knowledge exemption.** Known material facts get disclosed regardless.

## 8. Course Conclusion

You have covered California license law, property classification, estates, ownership, encumbrances, agency, contracts, disclosures, escrow and title, finance and foreclosure, appraisal, land use, fair housing, taxation, landlord-tenant law, and the transaction lifecycle.

Three things worth carrying forward:

**Competence is a compliance strategy.** Nearly every disciplinary case in this course traces to an agent who did not know a rule, not one who chose to break it. The material you just learned is the protection.

**Disclosure is almost always the answer.** When you are unsure whether something must be disclosed, disclose it. No licensee has been disciplined for telling a buyer too much about a property's condition.

**The standard is the same for everyone.** Consistency in how you treat clients is simultaneously the fair housing requirement, the defense against a bias claim, and the foundation of a referral business.
