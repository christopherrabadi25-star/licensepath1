# Unit 8 — Real Estate Contracts in Practice

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** the three listing types and explain when each is used.
2. **Identify** the required elements of a valid listing agreement, including the definite termination date.
3. **Explain** safety (protection) clauses and the concept of procuring cause.
4. **Describe** buyer representation agreements and their compensation provisions.
5. **Outline** the structure of a California residential purchase agreement and identify its critical date-driven terms.
6. **Explain** how contingencies function, how they are removed, and the consequences of each approach.
7. **Analyze** counteroffers, multiple counteroffers, and backup offers.
8. **Calculate** commission splits, net-to-seller figures, and contingency deadline dates.

## 1. Listing Agreements: the Broker's Employment Contract

A **listing agreement** is a contract employing a broker to find a buyer. It is the broker's employment agreement, and under the statute of frauds it must be **written and signed** for the broker to sue for a commission.

### The three types

**Exclusive right to sell.** One broker is entitled to the commission **no matter who sells the property**, including the owner. This is the dominant residential listing, and for good reason: because the broker's compensation is secure, the broker invests fully in marketing. Nearly every MLS listing you write will be this type.

**Exclusive agency.** One broker is the exclusive agent, **but the owner may sell it themselves without owing a commission**. The broker takes the marketing risk of being cut out by an owner who finds their own buyer, which creates obvious friction — did the broker's sign generate that "owner's" buyer?

**Open listing.** A **non-exclusive**, unilateral contract given to any number of brokers. Only the broker who is the **procuring cause** of the sale earns a commission, and the owner selling directly owes nothing. Open listings are rare in residential practice and common in some commercial and land contexts.

A fourth arrangement, the **net listing**, is not a separate type of exclusivity but a compensation structure: the seller sets a net amount and the broker keeps everything above it. Net listings invite abuse — the broker's interest runs directly against the client's interest in a higher price — and they are heavily restricted; the broker must disclose the amount of compensation before the seller becomes obligated. Treat them as a trap to avoid rather than a tool to use. <!-- VERIFY: current California restrictions on net listings -->

### Required elements

Every listing needs the contract elements from Unit 7 plus practical essentials: identification of the parties, an adequate property description, the price and terms sought, the compensation and how it is earned, the broker's authority and duties, required statutory notices, signatures, and — critically — a **definite termination date**.

That last item is a license-law requirement, not a formality. **An exclusive listing with no definite termination date is grounds for disciplinary action.** So is an automatic-renewal provision that extends the listing indefinitely. When you write a listing, the end date goes in before anything else.

### Safety clauses and procuring cause

A **safety clause** (protection period, or extender clause) entitles the broker to a commission if, within a stated number of days after expiration, the owner sells to someone the broker introduced during the listing. It protects the broker from the seller who waits out the listing to avoid paying. Its enforceability normally depends on the broker delivering a **written list of protected prospects** to the seller within the time the contract specifies.

**Procuring cause** is the doctrine determining which broker earned the commission when more than one was involved. It asks who set in motion the **uninterrupted chain of events** leading to the sale. Merely opening a door or sending a link rarely wins; sustained involvement culminating in the contract usually does. Procuring cause disputes between brokerages are typically resolved through association arbitration rather than court.

## 2. Buyer Representation Agreements

A **buyer representation agreement** is the mirror image of a listing: it employs a broker to represent the buyer. It states the term, the geographic and property scope, the broker's duties, and how the broker is compensated — including what happens if the seller's side contributes less than the agreed amount, in which case the buyer typically owes the difference.

Written buyer agreements have become standard practice, and the direction of travel across the industry is toward requiring them before a buyer tours property. Because compensation practices are actively changing, verify current requirements and form language rather than relying on any summary. <!-- VERIFY: current requirements for written buyer agreements and compensation disclosure -->

The exam principle is stable even as practice shifts: **a broker's right to compensation comes from a written agreement with the party who agreed to pay**, and **who pays does not determine who is represented** (Unit 6).

## 3. The Residential Purchase Agreement

The California residential purchase agreement is a long form, but it is organized around a small number of decisions. Learn the architecture and the details become manageable.

**Parties and property.** Full legal names, the property address and legal description, and the items included and excluded (the fixtures fight from Unit 2 gets settled here).

**Price and financing terms.** Purchase price, initial deposit, any increased deposit, loan amount(s), and the balance of the down payment. The form tracks where every dollar comes from.

**Escrow and closing.** Escrow holder, escrow period, and the closing date. Possession is separately addressed — usually at close of escrow, sometimes by a rent-back agreement letting the seller remain briefly after closing.

**Contingencies and their timeframes.** Loan, appraisal, investigation/inspection, title review, HOA document review, and sale-of-buyer's-property, each with its own deadline.

**Disclosures and reports.** Which disclosures the seller will deliver and when (Unit 9), who pays for what reports and services, and the buyer's right to investigate.

**Default and dispute resolution.** Liquidated damages (separately initialed, capped at 3% for owner-occupied 1–4 units), mediation, and arbitration provisions the parties must separately initial to be bound.

**Time is of the essence.** This clause makes the stated deadlines strict, so late performance is a material breach — the reason a four-day slip can become a default (Unit 7, Example 5).

### The deposit

The buyer's **earnest money deposit** demonstrates good faith and funds potential liquidated damages. Three rules matter to you as an agent:

1. Deposits are **trust funds**. A broker who receives one must place it in escrow, a trust account, or the principal's hands **within three business days**, unless the offer directs the check be held uncashed pending acceptance — in which case the clock runs from acceptance, and the holding instruction must be disclosed to the seller.
2. The deposit is **not** what makes the contract binding; the exchanged promises are (Unit 7).
3. Never let a client hand you cash. Ever.

## 4. Contingencies

A **contingency** is a condition that must be satisfied or waived before a party is obligated to proceed. Contingencies are the buyer's structured escape hatches, and managing their deadlines is the single most common source of agent liability in residential practice.

**Common contingencies:**

- **Loan contingency** — the buyer's obligation depends on obtaining specified financing. Prequalification is not preapproval, and neither is a loan commitment (Unit 12).
- **Appraisal contingency** — protects the buyer if the property appraises below the contract price. Frequently the pressure point in competitive markets.
- **Investigation/inspection contingency** — the buyer's right to inspect and to approve or disapprove the property's condition, usually the broadest contingency in the contract.
- **Title contingency** — review and approval of the preliminary report and related documents (Unit 10).
- **HOA document contingency** — review of CC&Rs, budgets, reserves, minutes, and pending litigation in a common interest development.
- **Sale of buyer's property** — the buyer must sell an existing home first. Sellers resist these, and a **backup offer** or kick-out clause often accompanies them.

**Two removal regimes, and the difference is everything.** Under **active removal**, contingencies remain in effect until the buyer removes them **in writing**; the deadline passing does not by itself remove them, but it does entitle the seller to serve a **notice to buyer to perform**, after which the seller may cancel if the buyer still does not act. Under **passive removal**, a contingency is deemed waived if the buyer says nothing by the deadline. California residential practice generally uses **active removal** with a notice mechanism. <!-- VERIFY: current standard-form removal mechanics and notice periods -->

The practical consequence: **the seller cannot simply cancel the moment a deadline passes** — a proper written notice must be delivered first, and the buyer gets a short cure period. Agents who assume automatic cancellation cost their clients deals and invite lawsuits.

## 5. Counteroffers, Multiple Counteroffers, and Backups

From Unit 7 you know a **counteroffer rejects and terminates the original offer**. Practice adds refinements.

**Multiple counteroffers.** A seller may counter several buyers at once, provided the form makes clear that no contract forms until the seller signs a final acceptance of one buyer's response. This preserves the seller's ability to choose without accidentally binding to two buyers — a catastrophe that happens when agents use ordinary counteroffer forms for multiple parties.

**Backup offers.** A backup offer takes effect automatically if the primary contract falls through. It must state its position (first backup, second backup) and typically allows the backup buyer to cancel while waiting. For sellers accepting weak primary offers, a signed backup is real leverage.

**Revocation timing.** An offer or counteroffer may be revoked any time before the other party's acceptance is **communicated back**. In a hot market this is a genuine race, which is why you deliver written revocations rather than relying on a phone call.

## 6. Options and Other Instruments

An **option** gives the optionee the right, but not the obligation, to buy on stated terms within a set period, supported by consideration. It is unilateral: the optionor is bound, the optionee is not. Options appear in commercial deals, lease-option arrangements, and land assemblage.

A **land contract** (installment sale contract, or contract for deed) is a financing device where the seller retains legal title while the buyer takes possession and pays over time, receiving title only after final payment. They carry significant risk for buyers and are heavily regulated. Route clients considering one to an attorney.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Which listing type, and what it costs (Glendora)

A Glendora seller says: "I'll list with you, but my cousin might buy it. If he does, I'm not paying a commission."

**Analysis.** The seller is describing an **exclusive agency** listing. You have three honest options: take the exclusive agency and accept the risk, negotiate an **exclusive right to sell** that carves out the named cousin by written exclusion (common, clean, and specific), or decline. What you must not do is take an exclusive right to sell while letting the seller believe he can sell to the cousin commission-free — that is a misrepresentation of your own contract.

The named-exclusion approach is the professional solution: it protects the seller's actual intent, protects you against every *other* buyer, and puts the understanding in writing where it belongs.

### Example 2 — Safety clause after expiration (Upland)

You show an Upland home to a buyer in March. Your listing expires June 30 with a 90-day safety clause. On July 12 you deliver the seller a written list of protected prospects including that buyer. On August 20, the seller sells to that same buyer directly.

**Analysis.** If the contract required the prospect list within a stated period after expiration and you delivered it within that period, the safety clause is enforceable and the commission is owed — the sale occurred within 90 days to a prospect you introduced and properly identified. **The whole case turns on the list.** Miss the delivery deadline and you likely have nothing, no matter how clearly you introduced the buyer. Calendar it the day the listing expires.

### Example 3 — Contingency deadline arithmetic (Rancho Cucamonga)

An offer is accepted **Tuesday, September 8**. The contract gives the buyer **17 days** for the investigation contingency and **21 days** for the loan and appraisal contingencies, counted from acceptance.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Day 1 is the day *after* acceptance under the typical counting convention: **September 9**. <!-- VERIFY: confirm counting convention against current form -->
2. Investigation contingency: 17 days from September 9 → September 9 + 16 = **September 25**.
3. Loan and appraisal contingencies: 21 days → September 9 + 20 = **September 29**.
4. If the buyer has not delivered written contingency removals by those dates, the seller may deliver a **notice to buyer to perform**, and only after the notice period expires may the seller cancel.

Two habits separate professionals from amateurs here: calendar every deadline the day the contract is accepted, and never assume a passed deadline means automatic cancellation.

### Example 4 — Net-to-seller math (Covina)

A Covina seller says: "I need to walk away with **$500,000**." Closing costs will run about **1.5%** of the sale price, the payoff is **$180,000**, and total commission is **5%**.

**Walkthrough — solve for the price, don't guess it.**

Let P be the sale price. The seller nets: P − 0.05P − 0.015P − 180,000 = 500,000

1. Combine the percentage costs: P(1 − 0.065) = P × 0.935
2. So: 0.935P − 180,000 = 500,000
3. 0.935P = **680,000**
4. P = 680,000 ÷ 0.935 = **$727,272.73**, round to **$727,300**

**Check:** $727,300 × 0.065 = $47,274.50 in costs; $727,300 − $47,274.50 − $180,000 = **$500,025.50** ✓

The classic error is multiplying $680,000 by 1.065 ($724,200), which under-delivers by about $3,000. When solving for a price *before* percentage deductions, you **divide by (1 − rate)**, never multiply by (1 + rate). Expect this on the exam.

### Example 5 — Commission split through the chain (Pasadena)

A Pasadena home closes at **$1,240,000**. Total commission is **4.5%**, split **55% to the listing side / 45% to the buyer side**. The listing salesperson's broker agreement gives her **75%** of her side after a **$395** transaction fee is deducted from her share.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Total commission: $1,240,000 × 0.045 = **$55,800**
2. Listing side: $55,800 × 0.55 = **$30,690**
3. Salesperson's gross share: $30,690 × 0.75 = **$23,017.50**
4. Less transaction fee: $23,017.50 − $395 = **$22,622.50 net to the salesperson**
5. Broker retains: $30,690 − $23,017.50 + $395 = **$8,067.50**

Escrow pays the *brokerages*, never the salespersons — the split happens inside the brokerage afterward (Unit 1).

## 8. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Listing agreement** | The written contract employing a broker to find a buyer; required in writing for the broker to sue for compensation. |
| **Exclusive right to sell** | A listing entitling the broker to compensation regardless of who sells the property, including the owner. |
| **Exclusive agency listing** | A listing making one broker the exclusive agent, but allowing the owner to sell without owing a commission. |
| **Open listing** | A non-exclusive unilateral listing; only the procuring cause broker earns compensation. |
| **Net listing** | A compensation structure where the broker keeps everything above the seller's stated net; heavily restricted and conflict-prone. |
| **Definite termination date** | The required end date in an exclusive listing; omitting it is grounds for disciplinary action. |
| **Safety clause (protection period)** | A provision entitling the broker to compensation if the owner sells, within a stated period after expiration, to a prospect the broker introduced and identified in writing. |
| **Procuring cause** | The doctrine identifying which broker set in motion the uninterrupted chain of events resulting in the sale. |
| **Buyer representation agreement** | The written contract employing a broker to represent a buyer, stating term, scope, duties, and compensation. |
| **Earnest money deposit** | The buyer's good-faith deposit; a trust fund that must be placed in escrow, a trust account, or the principal's hands within three business days. |
| **Contingency** | A condition that must be satisfied or waived before a party is obligated to proceed. |
| **Active removal** | The regime under which contingencies remain in effect until removed in writing, with the seller's remedy being a notice to perform followed by possible cancellation. |
| **Notice to buyer to perform** | The written notice a seller delivers when a buyer misses a contingency deadline, starting a cure period before cancellation is permitted. |
| **Rent-back** | An agreement permitting the seller to remain in possession for a period after closing. |
| **Backup offer** | An offer that automatically takes effect if the primary contract falls through; states its numbered position. |
| **Multiple counteroffer** | A counteroffer made to several buyers at once, structured so no contract forms until the seller signs a final acceptance of one response. |
| **Time is of the essence** | A clause making contract deadlines strict, so late performance becomes a material breach. |
| **Land contract (installment sale contract)** | A seller-financing device where the seller retains legal title while the buyer possesses and pays, receiving title after final payment. |

## 9. Exam Tips

- **Exclusive right to sell = broker paid no matter who sells.** Exclusive agency = owner escapes. Open = only the procuring cause broker, and the owner escapes. Know all three cold.
- **No definite termination date in an exclusive listing = disciplinary violation.** This is a guaranteed question.
- **Safety clauses require the written prospect list.** If a fact pattern mentions expiration and a later sale, look for whether the list was delivered on time.
- **Deposits go to escrow, a trust account, or the principal within three business days.** When a check is properly held uncashed pending acceptance, the clock runs from acceptance.
- **Passed deadlines do not auto-cancel.** Under active removal, the seller must serve a notice to perform and let the cure period run.
- **Net-to-seller problems: divide by (1 − rate).** Multiplying by (1 + rate) is the wrong answer sitting in the option list.
- **Follow commission money in order:** total → side → salesperson's split → fees. Every intermediate figure will appear as a distractor.
- **A counteroffer terminates the original offer** — the single most tested contract-practice rule.
