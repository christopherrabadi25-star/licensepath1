# Unit 10 — Escrow & Title Insurance

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Define** escrow and **list** the requirements for a valid escrow.
2. **Describe** the escrow holder's role as a neutral depository and dual agent of both parties.
3. **Outline** the escrow lifecycle from opening to closing and recording.
4. **Explain** valid delivery of a deed and when title actually passes.
5. **Distinguish** the chain of title, abstract, preliminary report, and title policy.
6. **Compare** CLTA standard and ALTA extended coverage, and identify what each excludes.
7. **Explain** constructive vs. actual notice and how recording establishes priority.
8. **Calculate** closing prorations, transfer taxes, and buyer/seller debit and credit entries.

## 1. What Escrow Is

**Escrow** is a neutral third-party arrangement in which a disinterested party holds funds and documents and delivers them only when all conditions of the parties' mutual instructions are met. It exists because buyers and sellers do not trust each other enough to hand over money and a deed simultaneously across a kitchen table — and they shouldn't have to.

California requires two elements for a valid escrow:

1. **A binding contract** between the parties — the purchase agreement, exchange agreement, or loan agreement.
2. **Conditional delivery of transfer instruments and funds** to a neutral third party.

Miss either and there is no escrow. A seller who hands a deed directly to a buyer with "hold this until you pay me" has created a mess, not an escrow.

### The escrow holder's peculiar role

The escrow holder is a **neutral depository** and — this is the tested part — a **dual agent of both parties** for the limited purpose of carrying out the escrow instructions. Once the transaction closes, the escrow holder becomes the separate agent of each party for delivering what each is entitled to receive.

What follows from neutrality:

- The escrow holder **may not give advice** to either party or advocate for either side.
- The escrow holder **may not disclose** one party's confidential information to the other without authorization.
- The escrow holder acts **only on written mutual instructions**. Verbal changes are not instructions, and one party alone cannot amend escrow.
- If the parties deadlock, the escrow holder may file an **interpleader** action, depositing the disputed funds with a court and letting the parties fight it out.

That last point matters practically: when a deal falls apart and both sides claim the deposit, escrow does not decide who is right. It holds the money until both sides sign mutual cancellation instructions or a court orders release. Agents who promise clients "escrow will just return your deposit" set up an unpleasant conversation.

### Who may act as escrow holder

Independent escrow companies are licensed and regulated by the **Department of Financial Protection and Innovation**. Certain others may perform escrow without that license when acting in the course of their own business — including attorneys for their clients, banks and title insurers, and **real estate brokers** in transactions in which the broker is performing licensed acts. <!-- VERIFY: current broker escrow exemption conditions and advertising restrictions -->

A broker acting as escrow holder faces strict limits: the exemption is transaction-specific, the broker may not advertise general escrow services, and the funds are trust funds subject to full trust-fund accounting (Unit 18). In Northern California, escrow is commonly handled by title companies; in Southern California, independent escrow companies are more common. Neither pattern is legally required.

## 2. The Escrow Lifecycle

**Opening.** An agent delivers the fully executed purchase agreement and the buyer's deposit to the escrow holder, who assigns an escrow number and prepares **escrow instructions** reflecting the contract terms. Both parties sign the instructions. Where instructions and the purchase agreement conflict, the parties should resolve the conflict expressly rather than leaving two inconsistent documents.

**Processing.** Escrow orders the preliminary report, requests demands (payoff statements) from existing lienholders, obtains beneficiary statements where a loan will be assumed, collects the parties' documents, coordinates with the new lender, and clears title requirements — releases, statements of information, trust certifications.

**Prorations and settlement statements.** Escrow prepares the accounting: prorating taxes, interest, rents, and HOA dues; computing transfer taxes; allocating fees per the contract.

**Closing.** When all conditions are satisfied and funds are in, escrow **records** the deed and any new security instruments with the county recorder, then disburses funds.

**Close of escrow is recording.** Title passes on recording, and this is why the sequence matters at year-end and month-end deadlines.

### Termination

Escrow terminates by **full performance** (closing), by **mutual written cancellation instructions**, or by court order. Note the middle one: cancellation requires **both** parties' written agreement. A buyer who walks away cannot unilaterally instruct escrow to return the deposit, and a seller cannot unilaterally instruct escrow to release it to him.

## 3. Deeds and Valid Delivery

Escrow's core function is the conditional delivery of a **deed**, so you need the deed rules.

**Requirements for a valid deed:**

- **In writing**, with a competent grantor;
- an identifiable, **living grantee** (a deed to a dead person or a nonexistent entity is void);
- an adequate **property description**;
- **granting clause** words of conveyance;
- **signature of the grantor** (the grantee does not sign);
- **delivery and acceptance** during the grantor's lifetime.

Notice what is **not** required for validity between the parties: **recording**, **acknowledgment** (notarization), **consideration recited**, or the grantee's signature. Recording and acknowledgment matter for a different purpose — protection against third parties — which we cover in Section 5.

**Types of deeds:**

- **Grant deed** — California's standard, carrying two implied warranties: the grantor has not already conveyed the property to another, and the grantor has disclosed known encumbrances placed by the grantor. It also conveys after-acquired title.
- **Quitclaim deed** — conveys whatever interest the grantor has, if any, with **no warranties**. Used to clear clouds, release easements, and transfer between spouses.
- **Warranty deed** — broad express warranties; common in other states, rare in California because title insurance does the work.
- **Trustee's deed** — delivered to the purchaser at a foreclosure trustee's sale.
- **Sheriff's deed**, **tax deed**, **gift deed** — situational instruments.

**Delivery is about intent, not handing over paper.** A deed is delivered when the grantor **intends to pass title presently and irrevocably**. Two classic exam scenarios follow: a deed signed and locked in the grantor's safe deposit box with instructions to deliver it at death is **not** validly delivered (no present intent to pass title) and is void as a conveyance; a deed placed into escrow with irrevocable instructions **is** conditionally delivered, and on closing, delivery relates back to the date it was deposited.

## 4. Title Search, Abstract, and the Preliminary Report

Before insuring anything, the title company examines the public record.

**Chain of title** is the recorded history of successive owners of a parcel, traced through the grantor-grantee indexes. Breaks in the chain — a missing conveyance, a forged deed, an unprobated estate — create **clouds on title**.

An **abstract of title** is a summarized written history of everything recorded affecting the property. **Marketable title** means title reasonably free from doubt and defects such that a prudent buyer would accept it — not perfect title, which barely exists.

A **preliminary report** ("prelim") is the title company's report of the current condition of title: how title is vested, and the liens, easements, restrictions, and other exceptions the company would list if it issued a policy. Two things students must understand:

1. The prelim is **an offer to issue a policy on stated terms**, not a representation about the condition of title and not a warranty. Its legal significance is narrower than agents assume.
2. It is nonetheless **the single most useful document you will read** in any transaction. Every encumbrance from Unit 5 shows up here.

Buyers should review the prelim *and the underlying documents* — the CC&Rs, the easement grants, the reciprocal parking agreement — during the title contingency, not skim the summary.

A **statement of information** is a confidential form the parties complete listing names, aliases, marriages, and residence history. Its purpose is to let the title company distinguish your seller from the fourteen other people in the county with the same name who have judgments and tax liens recorded against them. Buyers sometimes resist it as intrusive; the honest explanation — "this is how we keep someone else's judgment off your title" — usually resolves that.

## 5. Recording, Notice, and Priority

**Recording** places a document in the county's public records. Recording is not required to make a deed valid between grantor and grantee, but it is essential to protect against third parties.

To be recorded, a document generally must be **acknowledged** — signed before a notary, who verifies identity and voluntariness.

Recording provides **constructive notice**: the law charges everyone with knowledge of what is properly recorded, whether or not they actually looked. **Actual notice** is knowledge a person really has. And **possession itself gives constructive notice** — someone living in the property puts the world on inquiry notice of their claim, which is why a careful buyer asks who is occupying the property and on what terms.

California's recording statute protects a **subsequent bona fide purchaser (BFP)** — one who pays value, takes **without notice** of the prior interest, and **records first**. Combine those requirements and you get the rule the exam tests: between an earlier buyer who failed to record and a later good-faith buyer who recorded, **the later buyer wins**.

Note the notice element carefully. A second buyer who *knew* about the first sale is not a bona fide purchaser and cannot win by racing to the recorder. And if the first buyer is living in the house, the second buyer has inquiry notice from the possession and cannot claim ignorance.

## 6. Title Insurance

Title insurance protects against **defects existing at the time the policy is issued** — it looks backward, unlike other insurance that covers future events. It is paid by a **single premium at closing** and lasts as long as the insured holds an interest.

**CLTA standard coverage** typically insures against matters of public record and certain off-record risks such as forgery, incompetence of a grantor, and improperly delivered deeds. What it typically **excludes**: matters a **survey** would reveal (encroachments, boundary disputes), rights of parties in possession, unrecorded easements and liens, mining claims, water rights, and zoning and other government regulation.

**ALTA extended coverage** adds protection for many of those off-record risks — survey matters, parties in possession, unrecorded easements — and typically requires a **survey or inspection**. Lenders generally require an ALTA lender's policy, since the lender never visits the property.

Two important structural points:

1. A **lender's policy insures the lender only**, up to the loan amount, and decreases as the loan is paid. It does nothing for the buyer's equity. A buyer who takes only the lender's policy is uninsured.
2. An **owner's policy** insures the owner for the purchase price. **Who pays for which policy is negotiable and varies by county custom** in California; the contract controls.

Neither policy covers **government regulation** (zoning, building codes), **eminent domain**, defects the insured **created or knew about** and did not disclose, or, generally, **environmental conditions**.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Prorating property taxes at closing (Upland)

An Upland sale closes **October 16**, with the **buyer owning the day of closing**. Annual property taxes are **$8,760**, and the seller has paid the full fiscal year in advance. California's property tax fiscal year runs **July 1 through June 30**. Use a 360-day year, 30-day months.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Daily tax: $8,760 ÷ 360 = **$24.33/day**
2. Seller's period of ownership this fiscal year: July 1 through October 15 = 3 full months (July, August, September) + 15 days = **105 days**
3. Seller's share: 105 × $24.33 = **$2,554.65**
4. Total prepaid by seller: **$8,760**
5. **Credit to seller / debit to buyer** for the unused portion: $8,760 − $2,554.65 = **$6,205.35**

The seller prepaid taxes covering a period the buyer will own, so the buyer reimburses the seller. Get the direction right: a prepaid expense produces a **seller credit**; an unpaid accrued expense produces a **seller debit and buyer credit**.

### Example 2 — Documentary transfer tax (Claremont)

A Claremont home sells for **$918,000**. The county documentary transfer tax rate is **$0.55 per $500** of consideration. There is an existing **$300,000 loan the buyer is assuming**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Transfer tax applies to consideration **net of any assumed loan** that remains on the property: $918,000 − $300,000 = **$618,000**
2. Increments of $500: $618,000 ÷ 500 = **1,236**
3. Tax: 1,236 × $0.55 = **$679.80**

Two traps in one problem. First, if the buyer had obtained *new* financing rather than assuming the existing loan, the tax would be computed on the full $918,000 ($1,009.80). Second, some cities impose their own transfer tax on top of the county's, which can multiply the figure substantially — always check the city.

### Example 3 — The unrecorded deed loses (Rialto)

A buyer closes on a Rialto home and, distracted by a move, never records her grant deed and never takes occupancy. Three months later the seller fraudulently deeds the same property to a second buyer, who pays full value, knows nothing of the first sale, and records immediately.

**Analysis.** The second buyer is a **bona fide purchaser**: paid value, no notice, recorded first. Under California's recording statute, the second buyer takes title, and the first buyer is left with a fraud claim against a seller who is probably judgment-proof.

**Change one fact and the outcome flips.** Had the first buyer been *living in the house*, her possession would give the second buyer **constructive (inquiry) notice**, destroying his bona fide purchaser status. This is why title companies and careful buyers always ask who is in possession.

### Example 4 — CLTA vs. ALTA in practice (Chino Hills)

A buyer purchases a Chino Hills home with a standard CLTA owner's policy. After closing, a survey for a planned pool reveals the neighbor's block wall sits **four feet inside** the buyer's lot, and that a portion of the buyer's driveway crosses an **unrecorded** easement area.

**Analysis.** Standard CLTA coverage typically **excludes** matters a survey would disclose and unrecorded interests — so the encroaching wall and the unrecorded easement likely fall outside the policy. **ALTA extended coverage**, with its survey requirement, would generally address exactly these risks.

The practical counseling point for buyers, especially on larger lots, rural parcels, or properties where fences look "off": the extended policy costs more and is often worth it. Your role is to raise the question during the title contingency and refer the buyer to the title company for coverage options — not to opine on what a policy covers.

### Example 5 — Delivery by safe deposit box (Monrovia)

An elderly Monrovia owner signs a grant deed to her nephew, notarizes it, and locks it in her safe deposit box with a note: "Give this to David when I die." She dies two years later; the nephew finds the deed and records it.

**Analysis.** There was **no valid delivery**. Delivery requires the grantor's intent to pass title **presently and irrevocably** during her lifetime; retaining the deed with instructions to deliver at death shows the opposite intent. The deed is ineffective as a conveyance, and recording it after her death does not cure the defect — recording never validates an invalid deed. The property passes through her estate under her will or by intestate succession.

The lesson for your clients: this well-meaning arrangement fails constantly. Estate planning belongs with an attorney, whether through a trust, a properly delivered deed, or a revocable transfer on death deed if appropriate. <!-- VERIFY: current status and requirements of the California revocable transfer on death deed -->

## 9. Exam Tips

- **Two requirements for valid escrow:** a binding contract plus conditional delivery to a neutral third party. Both, every time.
- **Escrow is a dual agent before closing** and a separate agent of each party after. It never advises, never advocates, and acts only on **mutual written** instructions.
- **Cancellation requires both parties' written agreement.** No unilateral deposit release.
- **Recording is NOT required for a valid deed** — delivery and acceptance are. Recording protects against third parties.
- **Delivery = present intent to pass title.** The safe-deposit-box deed fails. Recording never cures an invalid deed.
- **BFP = pays value + no notice + records first.** Possession defeats the "no notice" element, so a fact pattern mentioning someone living in the property is telling you the later buyer loses.
- **CLTA excludes what a survey would show; ALTA includes it.** Encroachment and boundary questions point to ALTA.
- **A lender's policy protects only the lender.** Any answer implying it protects the buyer's equity is wrong.
- **Proration direction:** prepaid by seller → credit seller, debit buyer. Accrued and unpaid → debit seller, credit buyer.
- **Transfer tax is computed net of an assumed loan**, and cities may add their own tax on top of the county rate.
