# Unit 7 — Working with Buyers

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Conduct** a buyer consultation that establishes criteria, financing readiness, and expectations.
2. **Establish** buyer agency in writing at the correct time and explain compensation.
3. **Distinguish** prequalification, preapproval, and a loan commitment, and explain why the difference matters.
4. **Plan and conduct** showings that satisfy the visual inspection duty and respect occupied property.
5. **Apply** fiduciary duties in the moments where buyers commonly ask you to breach them.
6. **Advise** on property condition without practicing inspection, law, or lending.
7. **Manage** buyer expectations in competitive and slow markets without misrepresenting either.

---

## 1. The Buyer Consultation

Most new agents skip this and start showing houses. That is backwards, and it is why they spend Saturdays driving strangers around.

The **buyer consultation** is a scheduled meeting before any showing. Its jobs:

**1. Establish the relationship in writing.** Deliver the agency disclosure, explain the three possible relationships, elect yours, and — where you and the buyer agree to proceed — execute a **buyer representation agreement** before touring property. Written buyer agreements have become standard practice, and the direction of travel across the industry is toward requiring them before showings. <!-- VERIFY: current requirements for written buyer agreements before showing property -->

**2. Establish financing reality.** More on this in Section 3, but the short version: a buyer without a real preapproval is not yet a buyer. Getting this wrong wastes months.

**3. Let the buyer define criteria.** Ask what they want; never infer it. This is both better service and your fair housing protection (Unit 3). Cover price ceiling versus comfort payment, location parameters *they* set, must-haves versus preferences, timeline, and what would make them walk away from a house they otherwise loved.

**4. Set expectations about process.** How offers work, what contingencies do, what inspections cost, how long escrow takes, and what closing costs run. A buyer surprised by a $12,000 closing-cost figure three days before closing is an avoidable crisis.

**5. Explain compensation clearly.** How you are paid, what the buyer's obligation is, and what happens if the seller's side contributes less than the agreed amount — typically the buyer owes the difference (Principles Unit 8). Because compensation practices have shifted materially, be precise and current rather than repeating what you heard. <!-- VERIFY: current compensation disclosure requirements -->

## 2. Buyer Agency and Its Duties

Once you represent a buyer, the full fiduciary package applies — **OLD CAR** (Principles Unit 6): obedience to lawful instructions, loyalty, disclosure, confidentiality, accounting, and reasonable care.

Where buyers commonly ask you to breach them:

**"What's the least they'll take?"** You may share public information and your market analysis. You may not share the seller's confidential motivation if you somehow learned it — and if you represent the buyer, you should be *seeking* that information for them, not protecting it.

**"Can you write the offer without telling them I'm an investor?"** Concealment of a material fact from the other party is fraud. Investor status is not protected, and misrepresenting the buyer's identity or intent is not a negotiating tactic.

**"Just tell them we'll waive the inspection."** You may write what the buyer instructs. You should also document, in writing, that you advised an inspection and that the buyer declined — because when a defect surfaces after closing, that email is the whole case.

**Confidentiality runs to your buyer.** Do not tell the listing agent your buyer's maximum, timeline pressure, or how much they love the house. New agents leak this constantly in friendly conversation with the other side.

## 3. Financing Readiness: Three Different Things

Buyers and agents use these interchangeably. They are not the same, and the gap is where deals die.

**Prequalification** — an informal estimate based on **stated, unverified** information. Fast, free, and nearly worthless as evidence of ability to close. A prequalification letter tells a seller almost nothing.

**Preapproval** — the lender has **verified** income, assets, and credit, and issued a conditional commitment subject to property, appraisal, and final underwriting. This is what a serious offer carries.

**Loan commitment / clear to close** — final underwriting approval. This comes near the end of escrow, not the beginning.

**What to do with this:** get buyers to a real preapproval before showing property. Read the letter — many say "prequalification" in the body while being titled "preapproval." Confirm the lender actually pulled credit and reviewed documents.

**A note on lender referrals:** you may recommend lenders. You may **not** accept anything of value for the referral — that is a RESPA violation regardless of what it is called (Principles Unit 12, Unit 2 of this course). Give buyers two or three names and let them choose.

## 4. Showings

**Before you go:**

- Confirm showing instructions and lockbox access, and follow them exactly.
- **Respect occupied property.** Tenants have possession rights and entry generally requires reasonable written notice, with 24 hours presumed reasonable (Principles Unit 17). "The seller said it's fine" does not override the tenant's rights, and an antagonized tenant can make a listing unshowable.
- Verify the property is what the MLS says — square footage source, permit status of additions, HOA dues.

**During the showing, your visual inspection duty is live.** You owe a reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of accessible areas in 1–4 unit residential property, and must disclose material facts it reveals (Principles Unit 6). Practically: look at ceilings for stains, look under sinks, notice fresh paint in one patch, notice grading that slopes toward the house, notice a bedroom with no window.

**Observe, disclose, refer.** You are not an inspector. You do not diagnose cause or estimate cost. You say what you saw, in writing, and recommend a qualified inspection.

**What not to do:**

- Do not characterize neighborhoods or residents (Unit 3).
- Do not answer "is this a good school district?" with an opinion — point to published ratings and let them evaluate.
- Do not speculate about permits, boundaries, or what "could" be built next door. Refer them to the city.
- Do not discuss your buyer's enthusiasm or budget where the seller's cameras can hear you. Assume every occupied property has audio recording — many do.

**Safety.** Meet new clients at the office first, share your showing schedule with someone, and trust your instincts. This is standard practice, not paranoia.

## 5. Advising Without Overstepping

The recurring test in buyer work is knowing where your expertise ends.

| Buyer asks | You may | You may not |
|---|---|---|
| "Is this a good price?" | Present comparable sales and your analysis | Guarantee value or appreciation |
| "Will this foundation crack get worse?" | Note what you observed, recommend a structural engineer | Diagnose or estimate repair cost |
| "Can I add a second unit?" | Point them to the city planning department | Opine on zoning or permitting outcomes |
| "Should I hold title in a trust?" | Explain that options exist | Recommend a vesting — attorney/CPA question |
| "What are the tax consequences?" | Note the provisions exist | Calculate liability — CPA question |
| "Is the neighborhood safe?" | Provide published crime data sources | Characterize the area or its residents |

The professional posture is the same each time: **give them the information and the right expert, not your opinion dressed as expertise.**

## 6. Managing Expectations in Both Markets

**In a competitive market**, buyers lose houses. Prepare them for it at the consultation, not after the third loss. Explain what actually makes offers competitive — price, yes, but also proof of funds, contingency structure, timeline flexibility, and a clean, complete, correctly written offer. And be candid about what waiving contingencies actually costs: a buyer waiving inspection is accepting unknown condition risk with no recourse, and they should decide that consciously.

**In a slow market**, buyers hesitate and try to time the bottom. Your job is not to push. It is to present what the data shows — inventory, days on market, sale-to-list ratios (Unit 5) — and let them decide. An agent who pressures a hesitant buyer into a purchase they regret has bought one commission and lost a career of referrals.

**In both:** never predict the market. "Prices will keep going up" is a forecast you cannot support and a buyer will remember.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — The prequalification that wasn't (Rancho Cucamonga)

A buyer brings a "preapproval letter" and writes on a $685,000 home. You skim the letter and submit. On day 19, the lender discovers the buyer's income is largely 1099 self-employment with only 14 months of history, and the file dies. The buyer loses the house and, because the loan contingency had already been removed, faces a liquidated damages claim against their $20,000 deposit.

**Analysis.** The letter said "preapproval" in the header and "based on information provided by the borrower" in the body — a **prequalification**. Reading it and asking two questions ("Did they pull credit? Did they review tax returns?") would have surfaced the self-employment issue before the offer.

**What the agent should have done:** verified the letter's substance at consultation, had the lender confirm document review in writing, and — critically — **not advised removing the loan contingency** until the lender confirmed underwriting approval, not merely that things "looked good."

### Example 2 — The stain and the buyer who didn't care (Whittier)

Showing a 1952 home, you notice a brown ring on the dining room ceiling under an upstairs bathroom, freshly painted around it. The seller's disclosure mentions no leaks. Your buyer says, "It's fine, I love this house, let's not make it complicated."

**Analysis.** Your visual inspection duty is not waived by the buyer's enthusiasm. You must **disclose what you observed in writing** and recommend inspection of that area. If the buyer still declines further investigation, document that too.

The email you send that afternoon — "During today's showing I observed staining on the dining room ceiling below the upstairs bath, with what appears to be recent paint. I recommend a qualified inspection of this area before removing your investigation contingency." — is a five-minute task that is the entire difference between a defensible file and a lawsuit.

### Example 3 — The buyer's confidential information (Arcadia)

At an open house, the listing agent chats with you: "Your buyers seem to really love it. What's their timeline?" You mention they need to be in before the school year and are approved up to $1.3M on a $1.19M listing.

**Analysis.** You just handed the seller's agent two pieces of leverage — urgency and a $110,000 ceiling above asking. That is a **breach of confidentiality** to your own client, and if it costs them money in negotiation, it is actionable.

Friendly conversation with the other side is normal and useful. It is also where new agents leak. Decide in advance what you will say: "They're interested — I'll let you know if we write." Nothing more.

### Example 4 — The tenant-occupied showing (Ontario)

Your buyer wants to see a duplex. The listing says "tenant occupied, 24-hour notice." A unit becomes available for a same-day showing because "the seller said just go."

**Analysis.** The seller cannot waive the **tenant's** entry rights. Entering without proper notice violates the tenant's right of possession, exposes the seller and both brokerages to liability, and — practically — creates a hostile tenant who will make every future showing difficult and who your buyer will inherit as a landlord.

Wait for proper notice. If the buyer is frustrated, explain that they are about to become this tenant's landlord and the relationship starts now.

## 8. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Buyer consultation** | The scheduled meeting before showings that establishes agency, financing readiness, criteria, and expectations. |
| **Buyer representation agreement** | The written contract employing a broker to represent a buyer, stating term, scope, duties, and compensation. |
| **Prequalification** | An informal estimate based on stated, unverified information; weak evidence of ability to close. |
| **Preapproval** | A conditional commitment after the lender verifies income, assets, and credit. |
| **Loan commitment** | Final underwriting approval, issued near the end of escrow. |
| **Visual inspection duty** | The agent's duty to conduct a reasonably competent, diligent visual inspection of accessible areas of 1–4 unit residential property and disclose material facts revealed. |
| **Observe, disclose, refer** | The professional posture for property condition: state what you saw, in writing, and recommend a qualified expert. |
| **Proof of funds** | Documentation of a buyer's cash available for down payment and closing costs. |

## 9. Practice Tips

- **Consult before you show.** Agency in writing, financing verified, criteria set by the buyer.
- **Read the preapproval letter.** Many say "prequalification" in the body. Ask whether credit was pulled and documents reviewed.
- **Never advise removing a loan contingency** until the lender confirms underwriting approval in writing.
- **Disclose what you observe, in writing, the same day** — even when the buyer says it doesn't matter.
- **Protect your buyer's confidential information** in casual conversation with the other side.
- **Respect tenant entry rights.** The seller cannot waive them, and your buyer inherits the relationship.
- **Never predict the market**, and never accept anything of value for a lender referral.
