# Unit 16 — Real Estate Taxation

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Explain** ad valorem property taxation and **calculate** annual property taxes under Proposition 13.
2. **Describe** the property tax calendar, delinquency dates, and the memory aids for each.
3. **Explain** reassessment on change of ownership, supplemental assessments, and the major exclusions.
4. **Identify** the homeowners' and veterans' exemptions and Mello-Roos special assessments.
5. **Calculate** documentary transfer tax, including the assumed-loan adjustment.
6. **Explain** the principal residence capital gain exclusion and how gain is computed.
7. **Describe** 1031 exchanges, boot, and the identification and closing deadlines.
8. **Explain** depreciation on investment property and the withholding rules under FIRPTA and California law.

## 1. Property Taxes and Proposition 13

California property taxes are **ad valorem** — "according to value." They are a **specific, involuntary lien** attaching to the property, and as you learned in Unit 5, they hold **first priority over all other liens** regardless of recording date.

**Proposition 13**, adopted in 1978, restructured California property taxation and still governs it. Its core rules:

- The **basic tax rate is capped at 1%** of assessed value.
- The **assessed value at acquisition** is the purchase price (the "base year value").
- Assessed value may increase by **no more than 2% per year** thereafter, regardless of how fast market value rises.
- The property is **reassessed to current market value on a change of ownership** or on new construction.

The practical consequence dominates California real estate: two identical houses side by side in Arcadia can carry wildly different tax bills. The neighbor who bought in 1994 may pay a third of what your buyer will pay, because your buyer's base year value resets at today's price. **Always run the buyer's taxes off the purchase price, never off the seller's current bill.** Agents who quote the seller's tax figure create a nasty surprise at the first tax bill and a legitimate complaint.

**Total tax rate** exceeds 1%. On top of the base rate, the bill includes **voter-approved bonded indebtedness** (school and infrastructure bonds) and **direct assessments** (lighting, sewer, vector control, Mello-Roos). A realistic effective rate in much of Southern California runs roughly **1.1% to 1.3%**, and materially higher in Mello-Roos districts. <!-- VERIFY: current typical rate ranges by county -->

## 2. The Property Tax Calendar

The fiscal year runs **July 1 through June 30**. Taxes are billed in two installments, and the dates are pure memorization:

| Event | Date |
|---|---|
| Fiscal year begins | July 1 |
| **Lien date** (taxes attach) | **January 1** (preceding the fiscal year) |
| First installment due | November 1 |
| **First installment delinquent** | **December 10** |
| Second installment due | February 1 |
| **Second installment delinquent** | **April 10** |
| Fiscal year ends | June 30 |

Two memory aids that have carried generations of students:

- **"No Darn Fooling Around"** — **N**ovember 1, **D**ecember 10, **F**ebruary 1, **A**pril 10.
- The delinquency dates are the **10th of the month after a holiday month** — December 10 (after Thanksgiving), April 10 (after taxes/spring).

A **10% penalty** applies to a late installment, with additional costs after the second delinquency. Property remaining delinquent becomes **tax-defaulted**, and after a statutory redemption period — generally **five years** for residential property — the county may sell it at a **tax sale**. <!-- VERIFY: current redemption period and tax sale procedure -->

**Supplemental assessments.** When a property changes ownership or new construction is completed, the assessor issues a **supplemental tax bill** for the difference between the old and new assessed values, prorated for the remainder of the fiscal year. This is the bill your buyers forget about — it arrives months after closing, is **not** covered by their impound account, and is frequently the first angry call a new agent receives. Warn buyers at contract, not at closing.

## 3. Exclusions from Reassessment

Because reassessment is so consequential, the exclusions matter enormously to clients.

**Transfers that do NOT trigger reassessment** generally include:

- **Interspousal transfers** — between spouses, including on divorce or death.
- **Parent–child and grandparent–grandchild transfers**, now sharply narrowed. Proposition 19 (effective 2021) limits this to a **principal residence that the transferee also uses as a principal residence**, with a value cap, and eliminated the former broad exclusion for other property. <!-- VERIFY: current Prop 19 value cap and filing deadlines -->
- **Transfers into a revocable trust** where the trustor remains the beneficial owner.
- **Refinancing** — no change of ownership occurs.
- Adding or removing a co-owner where beneficial ownership is unchanged (proportional interest transfers).

**Base year value transfers.** Proposition 19 also **expanded** portability: homeowners who are **55 or older**, severely disabled, or victims of a wildfire or natural disaster may transfer their base year value to a replacement principal residence **anywhere in California**, with adjustments if the replacement costs more. <!-- VERIFY: current transfer limits and number of permitted transfers -->

This is genuinely valuable to clients and a real source of business: a 68-year-old seller in Arcadia who has been afraid to downsize because of taxes may be able to carry her low assessed value to a new home. **Explain that the benefit exists and refer her to a CPA or the county assessor** — do not calculate it for her.

## 4. Exemptions and Special Assessments

**Homeowners' exemption** — a modest reduction in assessed value for an owner-occupied residence, historically **$7,000** of assessed value (worth roughly $70/year at the 1% rate). It requires a filing with the assessor. <!-- VERIFY: current exemption amount -->

**Veterans' and disabled veterans' exemptions** — larger reductions for qualifying veterans, with the disabled veterans' exemption substantially more valuable. <!-- VERIFY: current amounts and eligibility -->

**Special assessments** fund local improvements benefiting the property (Unit 5):

- **Mello-Roos (Community Facilities District)** — funds infrastructure in newer developments. Common across the Inland Empire and newer Santa Clarita and Riverside County tracts. Can add **$100–$400+ per month**, must be disclosed (Unit 9), and typically has a fixed term of years.
- **1915 Act improvement bonds** — assessments for streets, sewers, and similar improvements.

**Teach buyers to evaluate the total monthly obligation**, not the sale price. A $650,000 home with $340/month of Mello-Roos carries a higher effective payment than a $690,000 home without it — a comparison most buyers never make on their own.

## 5. Documentary Transfer Tax

A tax on transferring real property, imposed at the county level and, in some cities, additionally at the city level.

- The common **county rate is $0.55 per $500** of consideration (equivalently $1.10 per $1,000).
- The tax is computed on consideration **net of any existing loan the buyer assumes** or takes subject to.
- Many cities impose their own transfer tax on top, and some are substantial — several California cities have adopted high-value transfer taxes on expensive properties. <!-- VERIFY: current city transfer tax rates, which change by ballot measure -->
- **Who pays is negotiable** and set by contract and local custom.

Certain transfers are exempt, including gifts, transfers between spouses, and transfers where no consideration passes.

## 6. Income Taxation for Homeowners

**Basis** is your investment in the property for tax purposes:

> **Adjusted basis = purchase price + capital improvements − depreciation taken**

**Capital improvements** add to basis (a new roof, an addition, a remodel). **Repairs** do not (fixing a leak, repainting). The distinction rewards owners who keep records — teach your clients to keep receipts from day one.

**Gain on sale:**

> **Gain = amount realized (sale price − selling costs) − adjusted basis**

**The principal residence exclusion (IRC §121)** is the single most valuable tax provision most of your clients will ever use:

- Exclude up to **$250,000** of gain if single, **$500,000** if married filing jointly.
- Must have **owned and used** the home as a principal residence for at least **2 of the last 5 years**.
- Generally usable **once every two years**.
- Partial exclusions may apply for qualifying circumstances such as a job relocation, health, or unforeseen events. <!-- VERIFY: current partial exclusion rules -->

**Mortgage interest and property tax deductions** exist for itemizing homeowners, subject to federal caps on both mortgage principal and state-and-local tax deductions that have changed and may change again. <!-- VERIFY: current deduction limits before stating figures to students -->

**Your line here is bright:** you explain that these provisions *exist* and that clients should discuss them with a CPA. You never calculate a client's tax liability or advise on a tax strategy. That is not caution for its own sake — it is the difference between a helpful agent and an unlicensed tax practitioner (Unit 1).

## 7. Investment Property: Depreciation, Exchanges, and Withholding

**Depreciation (cost recovery)** lets an investor deduct the cost of *improvements* over a set recovery period. **Land is never depreciated.** Residential rental property and commercial property use different recovery periods under federal law. <!-- VERIFY: current recovery periods --> Depreciation reduces basis, which increases gain on sale — and that recaptured depreciation is taxed, often at a different rate.

**1031 exchanges (like-kind exchanges).** An investor may defer gain by exchanging investment or business real property for other like-kind real property. Since 2018 §1031 applies to **real property only** — personal property exchanges were eliminated.

The mechanics you must recognize:

- **45 days** from closing the relinquished property to **identify** replacement property in writing.
- **180 days** from that closing to **complete** the acquisition (or the tax return due date, if earlier).
- A **qualified intermediary** must hold the proceeds. **If the seller touches the money, the exchange fails.**
- **Boot** is non-like-kind value received — cash, debt relief, or personal property — and is **taxable to the extent of gain**.
- To fully defer, the replacement generally must be of **equal or greater value** with **equal or greater debt**, and all proceeds reinvested.
- Both properties must be held for **investment or business use** — a personal residence does not qualify.

The two deadlines run **concurrently**, not sequentially, and they are **not extendable** for ordinary hardship. Miss day 45 and the exchange is dead.

**Withholding on sale:**

- **FIRPTA** (federal) requires withholding on sales by foreign persons, at a percentage of the amount realized, with exemptions for certain lower-priced residences the buyer will occupy. <!-- VERIFY: current FIRPTA rates and thresholds -->
- **California withholding** requires escrow to withhold a percentage of the sale price on certain transactions, including sales by out-of-state sellers, with exemptions including a principal residence and sales below a dollar threshold. <!-- VERIFY: current California withholding rate and exemption threshold -->

Escrow handles the mechanics, but **you** need to spot the issue early — an out-of-state or foreign seller who learns about withholding three days before closing is an unhappy seller, and the exemption certifications take time.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — The tax bill surprise (Arcadia)

Your buyer purchases an Arcadia home for **$1,180,000**. The sellers bought in 1996 and their current annual tax bill is **$4,900**. The buyer asks what taxes will be.

**Walkthrough.**
1. The sellers' bill reflects a 1996 base year value grown at 2%/year — **irrelevant to your buyer.**
2. The buyer's base year value resets to the purchase price: **$1,180,000**
3. Base 1% tax: $1,180,000 × 0.01 = **$11,800**
4. Add bonds and direct assessments; at a realistic **1.18%** effective rate: $1,180,000 × 0.0118 = **$13,924**
5. Monthly: $13,924 ÷ 12 = **$1,160/month**

The buyer's taxes are roughly **$9,000/year higher** than the sellers'. Quoting $4,900 would have understated the monthly payment by about $750 — enough to break the buyer's qualification.

**Also warn about the supplemental bill.** The county will reassess after closing and send a supplemental bill covering the difference for the remainder of the fiscal year. It arrives separately, often months later, and is not in the impound account.

### Example 2 — Property tax proration (Ontario)

Closing is **October 16**, the buyer owns the day of closing, and the seller has prepaid the full fiscal year of **$8,760**. (This mirrors Unit 10; taxes are the most commonly prorated item.)

**Walkthrough.**
1. Fiscal year began **July 1**.
2. Daily rate (360-day year): $8,760 ÷ 360 = **$24.33**
3. Seller's ownership: July 1 – October 15 = 3 months + 15 days = **105 days**
4. Seller's share: 105 × $24.33 = **$2,554.65**
5. Prepaid balance benefiting the buyer: $8,760 − $2,554.65 = **$6,205.35**
6. **Credit seller / debit buyer $6,205.35**

### Example 3 — Documentary transfer tax with an assumption (Claremont)

A home sells for **$918,000**; the buyer **assumes** an existing **$300,000** loan. County rate: **$0.55 per $500**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Taxable consideration: $918,000 − $300,000 = **$618,000**
2. Increments: $618,000 ÷ 500 = **1,236**
3. Tax: 1,236 × $0.55 = **$679.80**

Had the buyer obtained **new** financing, the full $918,000 would be taxed: $918,000 ÷ 500 = 1,836 × $0.55 = **$1,009.80**. And if the city imposed its own tax, add it separately.

### Example 4 — Principal residence gain exclusion (Whittier)

A married couple bought a Whittier home in 2009 for **$385,000**. They added a **$62,000** addition and a **$28,000** kitchen remodel. They sell for **$1,090,000** with **6.5%** in selling costs. They have lived there continuously.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Adjusted basis: $385,000 + $62,000 + $28,000 = **$475,000**
2. Selling costs: $1,090,000 × 0.065 = **$70,850**
3. Amount realized: $1,090,000 − $70,850 = **$1,019,150**
4. Gain: $1,019,150 − $475,000 = **$544,150**
5. §121 exclusion (married filing jointly): **$500,000**
6. **Taxable gain: $544,150 − $500,000 = $44,150**

Two teaching points. First, **the improvements saved them real money** — without the $90,000 in capital improvements, taxable gain would have been $134,150 instead of $44,150. That is the argument for keeping receipts. Second, notice how ordinary this is: a couple who bought a modest house 17 years ago now has gain exceeding the exclusion. This is common in California, and it is exactly the moment to say: **"You should talk to your CPA before you list."**

### Example 5 — 1031 exchange with boot (Inland Empire investor)

An investor sells a Fontana rental for **$920,000** with a **$310,000** loan payoff and **$58,000** in costs, then buys a Riverside replacement for **$840,000** with a **$260,000** new loan. Her adjusted basis in the relinquished property was **$430,000**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Net proceeds to the qualified intermediary: $920,000 − $310,000 − $58,000 = **$552,000**
2. Cash needed for the replacement: $840,000 − $260,000 = **$580,000** — she reinvests all $552,000 and adds $28,000 of her own. **No cash boot.**
3. **But check the debt.** Old debt $310,000, new debt $260,000 → **debt relief of $50,000 = mortgage boot.**
4. Realized gain: ($920,000 − $58,000) − $430,000 = **$432,000**
5. **Recognized (taxable) gain = lesser of boot or realized gain = $50,000.** The remaining $382,000 is deferred.

**How she could have deferred it all:** buy a replacement of **equal or greater value with equal or greater debt** — trading down in either price or debt creates boot.

**Deadlines:** 45 days to identify in writing, 180 days to close, both from the relinquished closing, running concurrently, and the **qualified intermediary must hold every dollar.** If she had taken the $552,000 into her own account for even a day, the entire exchange would collapse and all $432,000 would be taxable.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Ad valorem** | "According to value" — the basis of property taxation. |
| **Proposition 13** | The 1978 measure capping the basic rate at 1%, limiting annual assessment increases to 2%, and triggering reassessment on change of ownership. |
| **Base year value** | The assessed value established at acquisition, growing no more than 2% annually until a change of ownership. |
| **Lien date** | January 1, when property taxes attach as a lien for the following fiscal year. |
| **No Darn Fooling Around** | Memory aid for the tax calendar: November 1, December 10, February 1, April 10. |
| **Supplemental assessment** | A separate tax bill for the difference between old and new assessed values after a change of ownership or new construction. |
| **Tax-defaulted property** | Property remaining delinquent, subject to a redemption period before a county tax sale. |
| **Proposition 19** | The measure narrowing the parent–child reassessment exclusion and expanding base year value portability for those 55+, severely disabled, or disaster victims. |
| **Homeowners' exemption** | A modest assessed value reduction for an owner-occupied residence, requiring a filing with the assessor. |
| **Mello-Roos (CFD)** | A special assessment funding infrastructure in newer developments; must be disclosed and can add hundreds monthly. |
| **Documentary transfer tax** | A tax on transfers, commonly $0.55 per $500 at the county level, computed net of an assumed loan, with possible city taxes added. |
| **Basis / adjusted basis** | Investment in property for tax purposes: purchase price plus capital improvements minus depreciation taken. |
| **Capital improvement vs. repair** | An improvement adds to basis; a repair does not. |
| **Amount realized** | Sale price less selling costs. |
| **Principal residence exclusion (§121)** | Exclusion of up to $250,000 of gain (single) or $500,000 (married filing jointly), requiring ownership and use for 2 of the last 5 years. |
| **Depreciation (cost recovery)** | Deduction of improvement cost over a recovery period; land is never depreciated, and depreciation reduces basis. |
| **1031 exchange** | A deferral of gain by exchanging like-kind investment or business real property; real property only since 2018. |
| **Qualified intermediary** | The party who must hold exchange proceeds; the exchange fails if the taxpayer receives the funds. |
| **45-day / 180-day rules** | Deadlines to identify replacement property in writing and to complete acquisition, running concurrently from the relinquished closing. |
| **Boot** | Non-like-kind value received in an exchange — cash or debt relief — taxable to the extent of gain. |
| **FIRPTA** | Federal withholding on sales by foreign persons. |
| **California withholding** | State withholding on certain sales, including by out-of-state sellers, with principal residence and threshold exemptions. |

## 10. Exam Tips

- **Prop 13: 1% base rate, 2% annual cap, reassessment on change of ownership.** All three, every time.
- **Never quote the seller's tax bill to a buyer.** Compute from the purchase price, and warn about the supplemental bill.
- **"No Darn Fooling Around"** — November 1, December 10, February 1, April 10. Lien date is **January 1** and the fiscal year is **July 1 – June 30**.
- **Transfer tax is computed net of an assumed loan**, at $0.55 per $500 at the county level, with city taxes possibly added.
- **§121: $250,000 single / $500,000 married, 2 of the last 5 years.** Both numbers and the time test get tested.
- **Land is never depreciated** — true in both appraisal (Unit 13) and taxation.
- **1031: 45 days to identify, 180 days to close, concurrent, qualified intermediary mandatory.** Touching the money kills the exchange.
- **Boot = cash or debt relief**, taxable to the extent of gain. Trading down in price *or* debt creates boot.
- **Property tax liens are first in priority** regardless of recording date.
- **You explain that tax provisions exist; a CPA applies them.** On any exam question asking what the agent should advise, the answer involves referral.
