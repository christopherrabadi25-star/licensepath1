# Unit 4 — Ownership & How Title Is Held

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** ownership in severalty from concurrent (co-)ownership.
2. **List** the four unities required for joint tenancy and **explain** how each can be broken.
3. **Compare** joint tenancy and tenancy in common on survivorship, shares, inheritance, and probate.
4. **Explain** California community property, including community property with right of survivorship and separate property.
5. **Calculate** ownership shares and sale proceeds for co-owners holding unequal interests.
6. **Describe** how title is held by trusts, partnerships, LLCs, and corporations.
7. **Recognize** when a vesting question requires referral to an attorney or CPA rather than agent advice.

---

## 1. Severalty and Concurrent Ownership

Start with the simplest case. **Ownership in severalty** means one person or one entity owns the entire estate alone. The word trips students up because "several" sounds like "many" — it is the opposite. The term comes from the owner's interest being *severed* from everyone else's. A single woman who buys a Covina condo by herself, or a corporation that owns a warehouse, holds title in severalty.

Everything else is **concurrent ownership** (co-ownership): two or more people holding title at the same time. California recognizes several forms, and the differences are not academic — they determine what happens when a co-owner dies, whether probate is required, what each owner may sell without permission, and how the property is taxed. This is why the vesting line on a deed is one of the highest-consequence sentences in a transaction, and why you must never choose it for your client.

Every form of concurrent ownership shares one feature: **unity of possession**. Each co-owner has the right to possess and use the *whole* property, not a physically divided piece. Two people owning a Duarte duplex 50/50 do not each own one unit; they each own an **undivided** one-half interest in all of it. If they want to own separate units, they must legally divide the property — a condominium conversion or subdivision, not a handshake.

## 2. Joint Tenancy and the Four Unities

**Joint tenancy** is co-ownership with the **right of survivorship**: when one joint tenant dies, that interest passes automatically to the surviving joint tenant(s), outside of probate and regardless of what the decedent's will says. That last point surprises people constantly — a will cannot defeat survivorship, because the interest simply evaporates at death and the survivors' shares expand to fill it.

Creating a joint tenancy requires the **four unities**, remembered as **T-TIP**:

- **T — Time.** All joint tenants must acquire their interests at the same moment.
- **T — Title.** All must take title through the same document (one deed).
- **I — Interest.** All must hold equal shares. Three joint tenants each own one-third; you cannot have a 50/30/20 joint tenancy.
- **P — Possession.** All have an equal, undivided right to possess the whole.

California also requires the intent to create a joint tenancy to be **expressly stated in the deed** — typically "as joint tenants" or "as joint tenants with right of survivorship." Silence produces a tenancy in common, not a joint tenancy. <!-- VERIFY: exact statutory language requirements -->

### Severance: how a joint tenancy breaks

Break any unity and the joint tenancy is **severed** as to that interest. The most common severance is a **conveyance**: a joint tenant may sell or gift their interest without the others' consent, and the buyer takes as a **tenant in common** — because the buyer acquired at a different time, through a different deed, breaking unities of time and title.

Work through the arithmetic, because this is a guaranteed exam question. Three siblings own a Riverside parcel as joint tenants, each with one-third. One sells her interest to an outside buyer:

- The buyer holds **one-third as a tenant in common**.
- The two remaining siblings still hold **two-thirds as joint tenants between themselves**, with survivorship still running as to that two-thirds.
- If one of those two siblings then dies, the survivor takes the full two-thirds, and the outside buyer still holds one-third as a tenant in common.

Other severance events include a joint tenant conveying to a trust, certain judgment executions and foreclosure sales, and — in California — a recorded unilateral severance, which the law regulates to prevent secret severances timed around a death. <!-- VERIFY: current recording requirements for unilateral severance -->

**A key non-event:** merely recording a deed of trust (taking out a loan) generally does *not* sever a California joint tenancy, because California follows the lien theory — the lender takes a lien, not title. Students trained on other states' materials get this wrong.

## 3. Tenancy in Common

**Tenancy in common (TIC)** is California's default co-ownership form. When two or more people take title without specifying otherwise, they are tenants in common. Its features:

- **No right of survivorship.** A deceased co-tenant's share passes to their heirs or by will, through probate.
- **Unequal shares allowed.** One owner may hold 60%, another 25%, another 15%. Only unity of possession is required.
- **Freely transferable.** Each co-tenant may sell, gift, mortgage, or devise their share without the others' consent.
- **Shared expenses and income** generally follow ownership percentages, absent agreement.

When co-owners cannot agree — one wants to sell, another refuses — the remedy is a **partition action**: a lawsuit asking the court to divide the property physically (partition in kind) or, far more commonly with a single house, order it sold and divide the proceeds (partition by sale). Partition is expensive, slow, and relationship-destroying, which is why written co-ownership agreements matter.

## 4. Community Property and Marital Vesting

California is a **community property** state, and this shapes an enormous share of your transactions.

**Community property** is property acquired by either spouse during marriage through the labor or earnings of either. Each spouse owns an equal, undivided one-half interest. The presumption is strong: property acquired during marriage is presumed community unless proven otherwise.

**Separate property** is property (a) owned before marriage, (b) acquired during marriage by gift or inheritance, or (c) acquired with the proceeds or rents of separate property. A house a spouse inherited from a parent during the marriage is separate property — until it gets commingled, refinanced, or improved with community funds, at which point tracing becomes an attorney's problem, not yours.

Three practical rules to internalize:

1. **Both spouses must join in a conveyance or encumbrance of community real property.** One spouse cannot sell or mortgage the community home alone. When you take a listing, confirm marital status and how title is vested — a missing spousal signature will stop a closing cold.
2. **Community property may be devised by will as to the decedent's half only.** Without survivorship vesting, that half goes through probate.
3. **Community property with right of survivorship** combines the tax advantages of community property with automatic survivorship — the surviving spouse takes the whole without probate. Many California couples now vest this way. Its potential income-tax advantage on the "step-up in basis" at death is real and significant, but it is a **CPA and attorney question**, and you must present it as such rather than advising. <!-- VERIFY: current basis treatment before any consumer-facing description -->

California also recognizes registered domestic partners with community property rights, and specialized vestings exist for particular circumstances. When a client asks "how should we hold title?", your correct and complete answer is: *"Here are the options and what each generally means; the right choice depends on your estate plan and taxes, so let's get your attorney or CPA to confirm before escrow prepares the deed."* Choosing for them is practicing law.

## 5. Entity Ownership: Trusts, Partnerships, LLCs, Corporations

Title is often held by an entity rather than an individual, and each form changes who signs and what documentation escrow demands.

**Living (revocable) trusts** are ubiquitous in California. Title vests in the trustee: "Maria Chen, Trustee of the Chen Family Trust dated March 3, 2015." The trustee signs, and escrow will require evidence of the trustee's authority — commonly a certification of trust rather than the entire trust document, which keeps the beneficiaries' private terms private. Property held in trust avoids probate, which is the main reason clients use them.

**General partnerships** hold title in the partnership name; partners generally have authority to bind the partnership, and each general partner carries unlimited personal liability. **Limited partnerships** add limited partners who are passive investors with liability capped at their investment — a common structure for syndicated apartment deals in the Inland Empire.

**Limited liability companies (LLCs)** combine liability protection with pass-through taxation and have become the default vehicle for small investment property. Title vests in the LLC; the operating agreement determines who may sign, and escrow will ask for it or for a resolution.

**Corporations** hold title in the corporate name, with authority flowing from board resolutions. A corporation owns in **severalty** — it is one legal person, no matter how many shareholders it has. That is a favorite exam point.

Two related structures round out the picture. **Condominiums** give each owner fee title to a unit of airspace plus an undivided interest in common areas (Unit 2). **Stock cooperatives** are different: the corporation owns the building, and each resident owns *shares* plus a proprietary lease — personal property, not real property, which is why co-op financing works differently. **Community apartment projects** and **planned developments (PUDs)** are further variations, all governed as common interest developments under the Davis-Stirling Act (covered in depth in Legal Aspects Unit 13).

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Survivorship defeats the will (San Gabriel)

Two brothers own a San Gabriel rental "as joint tenants." The older brother's will leaves "all my real property to my daughter." He dies. The daughter demands her father's half.

**Analysis.** She gets nothing from this property. In joint tenancy, the decedent's interest passes automatically to the surviving joint tenant at the moment of death — there is nothing left in the estate for the will to distribute. The surviving brother now owns 100% in severalty, and clears title by recording an affidavit of death of joint tenant with a certified death certificate. If the father wanted his daughter to inherit, the vesting had to change during his lifetime (by severing to a tenancy in common), not the will.

### Example 2 — Unequal TIC shares and a sale (Fontana)

Three investors buy a Fontana rental as tenants in common: Ana 50%, Ben 30%, Carla 20%. They later sell for **$690,000** with **7%** total closing costs and commissions, and pay off a **$310,000** loan they took together.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Gross sale price: **$690,000**
2. Closing costs and commissions: $690,000 × 0.07 = **$48,300**
3. Loan payoff: **$310,000**
4. Net proceeds: $690,000 − $48,300 − $310,000 = **$331,700**
5. Distribution by ownership share:
   - Ana: $331,700 × 0.50 = **$165,850**
   - Ben: $331,700 × 0.30 = **$99,510**
   - Carla: $331,700 × 0.20 = **$66,340**
   - Check: 165,850 + 99,510 + 66,340 = $331,700 ✓

Note the order of operations the exam tests: costs and the loan come out *before* splitting, not after. Splitting the gross price and then subtracting each owner's "share" of costs happens to produce the same result here, but only because the split percentages are applied uniformly — change any assumption (say, one owner contributed the down payment) and the co-ownership agreement, not the deed, controls. Which is exactly why investors need one.

### Example 3 — The missing spouse (West Covina)

You take a listing from a seller who tells you he is "separated but not divorced" and that the West Covina house is "his." Title shows "Luis R., a married man, as his sole and separate property," with a quitclaim deed from his wife recorded in 2019.

**Analysis.** The 2019 quitclaim is evidence that the wife transmuted her community interest to Luis's separate property — but "evidence" is not the same as "settled." Transmutations must meet statutory requirements, pending dissolution proceedings can restrict transfers, and a title company may require the spouse's signature or a court order anyway. Your job is not to resolve this. It is to (1) spot it before you market the property, (2) get the title company's requirements in writing early, and (3) tell the seller to consult his family law attorney. A listing that cannot close is worse than no listing.

### Example 4 — Severance arithmetic (Riverside)

Four cousins own Riverside land as joint tenants, each one-quarter. Cousin A deeds her interest to a friend. Cousin B then dies, leaving a will giving everything to his son.

**Walkthrough.**
1. A's conveyance severs her quarter: the **friend holds 1/4 as a tenant in common**.
2. Cousins B, C, and D still hold **3/4 as joint tenants** among themselves.
3. B dies. Survivorship operates *within* that joint tenancy: B's interest passes to **C and D**, not to B's son. The will cannot reach it.
4. Final ownership: **C = 3/8, D = 3/8** (splitting the 3/4 between them), **friend = 1/4** as a tenant in common.
5. Check: 3/8 + 3/8 + 2/8 = 8/8 ✓

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Severalty** | Ownership by one person or entity alone; the interest is severed from all others. |
| **Concurrent ownership** | Co-ownership by two or more persons at the same time. |
| **Undivided interest** | A fractional interest in the whole property carrying the right to possess all of it, not a physically divided portion. |
| **Joint tenancy** | Co-ownership with right of survivorship, requiring the four unities and an express statement in the deed. |
| **T-TIP (four unities)** | Time, Title, Interest, Possession — all four required to create and maintain a joint tenancy. |
| **Right of survivorship** | The automatic passing of a deceased co-owner's interest to the surviving co-owner(s), outside probate and unaffected by a will. |
| **Severance** | Breaking a unity so a joint tenancy interest converts to a tenancy in common. |
| **Tenancy in common** | California's default co-ownership: no survivorship, shares may be unequal, each share freely transferable and inheritable. |
| **Partition action** | A lawsuit to divide co-owned property in kind or order it sold and divide proceeds. |
| **Community property** | Property acquired by either spouse during marriage through labor or earnings; each spouse owns an equal one-half interest. |
| **Separate property** | Property owned before marriage or acquired during marriage by gift, inheritance, or with separate-property funds. |
| **Community property with right of survivorship** | A California vesting combining community property treatment with automatic survivorship, avoiding probate on the first death. |
| **Transmutation** | A change in the character of marital property (community to separate or vice versa), subject to statutory requirements. |
| **Affidavit of death of joint tenant** | The recorded document, with a certified death certificate, that clears a deceased joint tenant from title. |
| **Certification of trust** | An abbreviated document evidencing a trustee's authority without disclosing the trust's full private terms. |
| **Limited partnership** | A partnership with general partners who manage and bear unlimited liability, and limited partners who are passive with capped liability. |
| **Limited liability company (LLC)** | An entity combining liability protection with pass-through taxation; authority to sign flows from the operating agreement. |
| **Stock cooperative** | A structure where a corporation owns the building and residents own shares plus a proprietary lease — personal property, not real property. |
| **Common interest development (CID)** | Condominiums, PUDs, co-ops, and community apartment projects, governed in California by the Davis-Stirling Act. |

## 8. Exam Tips

- **Severalty means ONE owner.** Every exam includes at least one question betting you will read "several" as "multiple." A corporation owns in severalty no matter how many shareholders it has.
- **T-TIP creates joint tenancy; breaking any unity severs it.** The buyer of a joint tenant's interest always becomes a *tenant in common*, while the remaining joint tenants stay joint tenants with each other. Practice the fraction arithmetic until it is automatic.
- **Survivorship beats a will, every time.** If a question puts a will against joint tenancy vesting, the will loses.
- **Silence defaults to tenancy in common.** Joint tenancy and community property with right of survivorship must be expressly stated.
- **Both spouses sign.** Any question where one spouse alone conveys or encumbers community real property is testing the requirement that both join.
- **In California, a loan does not sever a joint tenancy** — lien theory, not title theory. Watch for out-of-state logic in the distractors.
- **"Which vesting should I choose?" is not your question to answer.** The professionally correct response — explain generally, refer to attorney/CPA — is often itself the right exam answer.
