# Unit 17 — Landlord–Tenant & Property Management

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** lease types by rent structure and identify the essential elements of a valid lease.
2. **Explain** the implied warranty of habitability and the landlord's repair obligations.
3. **Apply** California's security deposit rules, including limits, permitted deductions, and return deadlines.
4. **Describe** required notices to terminate tenancies and the unlawful detainer process.
5. **Explain** just cause eviction and rent cap requirements under the Tenant Protection Act.
6. **Distinguish** assignment from sublease and explain each party's continuing liability.
7. **Describe** the property manager's role, the management agreement, and trust fund duties.
8. **Calculate** prorated rent, security deposit accounting, and management fees.

---

## 1. Leases and Their Essentials

A **lease** creates a leasehold estate (Unit 3) and is simultaneously a **conveyance** of possession and a **contract**. The landlord is the **lessor**, the tenant the **lessee**.

**Essential elements:** capable parties, mutual consent, lawful object, consideration (rent), an adequate description of the premises, a statement of the term, and — under the statute of frauds — **a writing if the term exceeds one year** (Unit 7).

**Lease types by rent structure:**

- **Gross lease** — the tenant pays a fixed rent; the landlord pays operating expenses (taxes, insurance, maintenance). Most residential leases.
- **Net lease** — the tenant pays rent plus some expenses. Escalating variants: **net, net-net, and triple net (NNN)**, where triple net has the tenant paying taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Standard in commercial and retail.
- **Percentage lease** — rent includes a base amount plus a percentage of the tenant's gross sales. Standard in shopping centers, aligning landlord and tenant interests.
- **Ground lease** — a long-term lease of land on which the tenant builds; common for large commercial projects.
- **Graduated lease** — rent increases at scheduled intervals.
- **Index lease** — rent adjusts by reference to a published index such as CPI.

**Termination.** An **estate for years** ends automatically on its stated date with no notice (Unit 3). A **periodic tenancy** continues until proper notice. A lease generally **survives the sale of the property** — the buyer takes subject to existing leases, which is why you always ask for estoppel certificates and copies of leases when listing occupied income property.

## 2. Habitability and Repairs

California implies a **warranty of habitability** in every residential lease. It **cannot be waived**, and a lease clause purporting to waive it is void.

The landlord must maintain, among other things: effective waterproofing and weather protection; working plumbing, gas, heating, and electrical systems; hot and cold running water; sanitary and safe common areas; adequate trash receptacles; floors, stairways, and railings in good repair; and functioning smoke and carbon monoxide detectors. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory habitability checklist -->

**Tenant remedies** for a landlord's failure to repair after reasonable notice include:

- **Repair and deduct** — the tenant makes the repair and deducts the cost from rent, limited in amount and frequency by statute. <!-- VERIFY: current dollar/frequency limits -->
- **Rent withholding** in appropriate circumstances.
- **Abandonment** of the premises where conditions are severe.
- **Constructive eviction** — where the landlord's breach makes the premises unusable, the tenant may vacate and end the lease without further liability (Unit 3).
- Affirmative suits for damages and, in serious cases, statutory penalties.

**Retaliatory eviction is prohibited.** A landlord may not evict, raise rent, or reduce services because a tenant complained to a code enforcement agency, exercised repair rights, or organized with other tenants. California presumes retaliation when adverse action follows protected activity within a statutory window. <!-- VERIFY: current retaliation presumption period -->

**Landlord's right of entry.** A landlord may enter for repairs, inspections, showings, and emergencies, generally with **reasonable written notice — 24 hours is presumed reasonable** — during normal business hours. Emergencies and tenant abandonment are exceptions. Showing a property is not a license to walk in; agents who ignore entry rules expose their broker to liability and their sellers to a tenant who becomes uncooperative at exactly the wrong moment. <!-- VERIFY: current notice requirements -->

## 3. Security Deposits

California's security deposit rules are strict, heavily litigated, and reliably tested.

**Limits.** California recently reduced the maximum security deposit for most residential tenancies to **one month's rent**, whether furnished or unfurnished, with a limited exception for certain small landlords. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory cap, exceptions, and effective date --> Historically the caps were two months (unfurnished) and three months (furnished), and older materials still teach those figures — a good reason to verify before publishing.

**All residential security deposits are refundable.** California does not permit non-refundable cleaning or administrative fees in residential tenancies. Calling a deposit "non-refundable" does not make it so.

**Permitted deductions** are limited to:

1. **Unpaid rent**
2. **Cleaning** to return the unit to the level of cleanliness at the start of the tenancy
3. **Repair of damage** beyond **ordinary wear and tear**
4. **Restoration or replacement of personal property** (furnishings) where the lease allows

**Not deductible:** ordinary wear and tear. Faded paint, carpet worn from normal use, small nail holes, and minor scuffs are the cost of doing business. A landlord who charges a departing tenant for repainting after a four-year tenancy will generally lose.

**The return deadline is 21 days** after the tenant vacates. Within that period the landlord must return the deposit and, if any amount is withheld, provide an **itemized statement** with **receipts or invoices** for the deductions. <!-- VERIFY: current documentation thresholds -->

**Pre-move-out inspection.** The tenant has a right to request an inspection before moving out, with the landlord giving notice of the right and an opportunity to cure identified problems — a provision that prevents most disputes when actually used.

**Bad faith retention** can expose a landlord to statutory damages beyond the deposit itself. <!-- VERIFY: current penalty multiple -->

## 4. Terminating Tenancies and Unlawful Detainer

**Notices to terminate a periodic tenancy:**

- Landlord ending a month-to-month: generally **30 days** if the tenant has resided under a year, **60 days** if a year or more.
- Tenant ending a month-to-month: generally **30 days**.
- **Three-day notice to pay rent or quit** for nonpayment.
- **Three-day notice to perform covenant or quit** for a curable lease violation.
- **Three-day notice to quit** for incurable conduct such as serious nuisance or illegal activity.

Notices must be properly served — personal service, substituted service, or "nail and mail" as the statutes permit — and defective service is the most common reason an eviction fails. <!-- VERIFY: current notice periods and service requirements -->

**Unlawful detainer** is California's summary court action to recover possession. The sequence: proper notice expires → complaint filed → tenant served → tenant's response window (short) → trial → judgment → **writ of possession** → the **sheriff** performs the lockout.

Three points you must internalize:

1. **Only the sheriff removes a tenant.** Ever.
2. **Self-help is illegal and expensive.** Changing locks, removing doors, shutting off utilities, or removing a tenant's belongings exposes the landlord to statutory damages, actual damages, and attorney's fees.
3. The process takes **weeks at minimum**, longer if contested. Landlords who expect to "have them out by the weekend" need a realistic timeline from you at the outset.

## 5. Rent Control and the Tenant Protection Act

The **Tenant Protection Act of 2019 (AB 1482)** applies statewide to covered residential rental property and does two things:

**Rent caps.** Annual increases are limited to a formula tied to inflation with a hard ceiling — commonly stated as **5% plus local CPI, capped at 10%**, whichever is lower. <!-- VERIFY: current formula and cap -->

**Just cause eviction.** After the tenant has occupied for **12 months**, the landlord must state a **just cause** to terminate. Just causes divide into two categories:

- **At-fault** — nonpayment of rent, breach of a material lease term, nuisance, criminal activity, refusal to allow lawful entry, failure to sign a similar renewal lease.
- **No-fault** — owner or qualifying relative move-in, withdrawal from the rental market (Ellis Act), demolition or substantial remodel, government order. **No-fault terminations require relocation assistance**, typically one month's rent or a rent waiver. <!-- VERIFY: current relocation assistance amount -->

**Exemptions** from AB 1482 generally include newer construction (a rolling ~15-year window), single-family homes and condos when the owner is not a corporate entity **and** proper written notice of exemption was given, owner-occupied duplexes, and certain affordable housing. The notice requirement is where landlords fail — the exemption is lost without the required lease language. <!-- VERIFY: current exemption criteria and notice language -->

**Local ordinances** may be stricter, and several California cities have their own rent stabilization and just cause ordinances that predate and exceed AB 1482. **Always check the city.** Advising a client on rent increases without checking the local ordinance is malpractice waiting to happen.

## 6. Assignment, Sublease, and Transfers

**Assignment** transfers the tenant's **entire remaining interest** to a new tenant. The assignee comes into direct relationship with the landlord, but — as in Unit 7 — the **original tenant remains secondarily liable** unless the landlord grants a novation.

**Sublease** transfers **less than the entire interest** — part of the term or part of the premises. The subtenant's relationship is with the **original tenant**, not the landlord. The original tenant remains fully liable and becomes a sublessor.

Most leases require the landlord's **written consent** to either, often with a standard that consent will not be unreasonably withheld.

**Sale of the property.** A buyer takes **subject to existing leases**. Estoppel certificates — signed tenant statements confirming rent, deposit, term, and any claims — are how a buyer verifies what they are inheriting. On any occupied income property listing, gather leases, rent roll, deposit accounting, and estoppels early.

## 7. Property Management

Property management is a licensed activity (Unit 1): leasing, collecting rents, and negotiating for others for compensation requires a license, subject to the resident manager exemption.

**The management agreement** — the contract between owner and broker — should specify the term, the scope of authority, the fee structure, the maintenance spending limit requiring owner approval, reporting frequency, trust account handling, insurance and indemnification, and termination rights. The property manager is a **general agent** of the owner (Unit 6), with ongoing authority over a range of matters — a broader agency than the special agency of a listing broker.

**Compensation** is typically a percentage of collected rents (commonly 4–10% residential depending on portfolio size), often with leasing fees for placing new tenants and sometimes project management fees for capital work.

**Trust fund duties are the heart of the job and the leading source of discipline.** Rents, security deposits, and reserves are **trust funds** belonging to others (Unit 18 covers the rules in full):

- Deposit into a **trust account** — never a personal or general business account.
- **Commingling is a violation**, even briefly and even without loss.
- Maintain a **control record** and **separate ledgers for each beneficiary**, reconciled monthly.
- The broker is responsible for trust funds handled by anyone in the brokerage.

**Operational responsibilities:** marketing and leasing, tenant screening applied uniformly (fair housing — Unit 15 governs every step), rent collection and enforcement, maintenance and vendor management, habitability compliance, deposit accounting, owner reporting and distributions, and record retention.

**Fair housing exposure in management is constant.** Tenant screening is where discrimination claims concentrate: apply the same criteria, in the same order, to every applicant, document the reason for every denial, and remember that source of income is protected in California.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Security deposit accounting (Ontario)

A tenant vacates a $2,700/month Ontario rental after two years, having paid a **$2,700** security deposit. The landlord wants to deduct:

| Item | Amount | Deductible? |
|---|---|---|
| Unpaid final month's rent | $2,700 | **Yes** — unpaid rent |
| Professional cleaning to original condition | $310 | **Yes** — cleaning to original level |
| Repair a large hole in the drywall | $480 | **Yes** — damage beyond wear and tear |
| Repaint entire unit (normal wear, 2-year tenancy) | $1,850 | **No** — ordinary wear and tear |
| Replace carpet worn from normal use | $2,400 | **No** — ordinary wear and tear |

**Walkthrough.**
1. Permitted deductions: $2,700 + $310 + $480 = **$3,490**
2. Deposit held: **$2,700**
3. Deductions exceed the deposit, so the entire deposit is consumed and the landlord may pursue the **$790** shortfall separately.
4. The landlord must still deliver an **itemized statement with receipts within 21 days**.

Had the landlord improperly included the paint and carpet, he would have claimed $6,850 against a $2,700 deposit, returned nothing, and exposed himself to a bad faith claim. **Ordinary wear and tear is the line**, and two years of normal living is squarely on the non-deductible side.

### Example 2 — Prorated rent at move-in and move-out (Rancho Cucamonga)

A tenant moves in **March 18** at **$2,700/month** and later gives notice, vacating **September 9**. Rent is due on the 1st; use a 30-day month.

**Walkthrough — move-in.**
1. Daily rent: $2,700 ÷ 30 = **$90**
2. Days occupied in March, counting the move-in day: March 18–31 = **14 days**
3. Prorated rent due: 14 × $90 = **$1,260**

**Move-out.**
4. Days occupied in September through the 9th = **9 days**
5. Rent owed for September: 9 × $90 = **$810**
6. If the tenant already paid the full $2,700 on September 1, the landlord owes a **$1,890 refund**, accounted for separately from the security deposit.

Keep those two buckets distinct. Mixing a rent refund into deposit accounting is how landlords end up unable to explain their own math to a judge.

### Example 3 — AB 1482 rent increase (Pomona)

An owner of a 1985 twelve-unit Pomona building wants to raise a long-term tenant's rent from **$1,850** to **$2,200**. Local CPI is **3.2%**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. The building is a 1985 multifamily property, so it is **not** exempt as new construction and **not** a single-family exemption. AB 1482 applies.
2. Permitted increase: **5% + 3.2% CPI = 8.2%**, below the 10% ceiling → cap is **8.2%**.
3. Maximum new rent: $1,850 × 1.082 = **$2,001.70**
4. The proposed $2,200 is an **18.9% increase — unlawful.**
5. **Check Pomona's local ordinance**, which may be stricter still.

Also relevant: because the tenant has occupied more than 12 months, any termination requires **just cause**, and a no-fault termination would require **relocation assistance**.

### Example 4 — Assignment vs. sublease (Claremont)

A commercial tenant with three years remaining on a Claremont lease wants out. Two options:

**(a) Assign** the entire remaining term to a new business. The assignee deals directly with the landlord — but the original tenant **remains secondarily liable** if the assignee defaults, unless the landlord signs a **novation** releasing her.

**(b) Sublease** the space for two of the three remaining years. The subtenant's relationship is with **her**, not the landlord. She remains **fully liable** on the original lease and now also acts as a landlord to her subtenant.

**Counsel:** if she wants a clean exit, she needs an assignment **plus a written release**. An assignment alone leaves her exposed for three more years on a business she no longer operates. Most leases require the landlord's written consent to either route.

### Example 5 — Management fee and owner distribution (Inland Empire portfolio)

You manage an eight-unit building. Monthly scheduled rent is **$2,150 per unit**; one unit is vacant. Your agreement provides an **7% management fee on collected rents**. This month's expenses: **$1,340** in maintenance and **$620** in utilities.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Collected rent: 7 occupied × $2,150 = **$15,050**
2. Management fee: $15,050 × 0.07 = **$1,053.50**
3. Total disbursements: $1,053.50 + $1,340 + $620 = **$3,013.50**
4. Owner distribution: $15,050 − $3,013.50 = **$12,036.50**

Two professional notes. The fee is on **collected** rent, not scheduled rent — the vacancy costs you too, which is the alignment the structure intends. And every dollar above moved through the **trust account** with a ledger entry for this owner; the management fee becomes yours only when properly disbursed out of trust.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Lessor / lessee** | The landlord who conveys possession / the tenant who receives it. |
| **Gross lease** | A lease in which the tenant pays fixed rent and the landlord pays operating expenses. |
| **Net lease / triple net (NNN)** | A lease in which the tenant pays rent plus some expenses / taxes, insurance, and maintenance. |
| **Percentage lease** | A lease with base rent plus a percentage of the tenant's gross sales; common in retail. |
| **Ground lease** | A long-term lease of land on which the tenant constructs improvements. |
| **Warranty of habitability** | The non-waivable implied promise that residential premises are fit for human occupancy. |
| **Repair and deduct** | A tenant remedy allowing repair of a defect and deduction from rent, subject to statutory limits. |
| **Constructive eviction** | A landlord breach making premises unusable, permitting the tenant to vacate and end the lease. |
| **Retaliatory eviction** | Prohibited adverse action taken because a tenant exercised legal rights; presumed within a statutory window. |
| **Right of entry** | The landlord's limited right to enter with reasonable written notice, with 24 hours presumed reasonable. |
| **Security deposit** | A refundable deposit capped by statute; California permits no non-refundable residential deposits. |
| **Ordinary wear and tear** | Normal deterioration from ordinary use, which may never be deducted from a security deposit. |
| **21-day rule** | The deadline to return the deposit with an itemized statement and receipts after the tenant vacates. |
| **Pre-move-out inspection** | The tenant's right to an inspection before vacating, with an opportunity to cure identified issues. |
| **Three-day notice** | Notice to pay rent or quit, to perform a covenant or quit, or to quit for incurable conduct. |
| **Unlawful detainer** | California's summary action to recover possession; only the sheriff may remove a tenant. |
| **Writ of possession** | The court order authorizing the sheriff to restore possession to the landlord. |
| **Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482)** | The statewide law imposing rent caps and just cause eviction requirements on covered property. |
| **Just cause** | The required basis for terminating a covered tenancy after 12 months; at-fault or no-fault. |
| **Relocation assistance** | Payment or rent waiver required for a no-fault termination under the Tenant Protection Act. |
| **Ellis Act** | The law permitting a landlord to withdraw rental property from the market. |
| **Assignment** | Transfer of the tenant's entire remaining interest; the assignor stays secondarily liable absent novation. |
| **Sublease** | Transfer of less than the entire interest; the subtenant's relationship is with the original tenant. |
| **Estoppel certificate** | A signed tenant statement confirming lease terms, rent, deposit, and any claims, used by buyers and lenders. |
| **Management agreement** | The contract between owner and broker establishing a general agency and defining scope, fees, and authority. |
| **Trust account** | The account holding rents and deposits belonging to others; commingling is a disciplinary violation. |

## 10. Exam Tips

- **The warranty of habitability cannot be waived.** Any lease clause purporting to waive it is void, and any answer permitting waiver is wrong.
- **Ordinary wear and tear is never deductible** from a security deposit. Paint and carpet worn from normal use are the classic fact pattern.
- **21 days to return the deposit with an itemized statement.** Memorize the number.
- **Verify the current deposit cap** — California recently moved toward one month's rent, while older materials teach two and three months.
- **Only the sheriff removes a tenant**, after a writ of possession. Self-help lockouts, utility shutoffs, and door removal are always the wrong answer.
- **Three-day notice for nonpayment; 30 or 60 days to end a month-to-month** depending on length of residency.
- **AB 1482: rent cap of 5% + CPI, maximum 10%, and just cause after 12 months.** No-fault terminations require relocation assistance, and local ordinances may be stricter.
- **Assignment = entire interest, assignor still secondarily liable. Sublease = partial, subtenant answers to the original tenant.**
- **A property manager is a GENERAL agent**; a listing broker is a special agent.
- **Rents and deposits are trust funds.** Commingling violates the law even without loss.
