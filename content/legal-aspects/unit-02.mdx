# Unit 2 — Law of Agency: Duties, Disclosure & Conflict

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

1. **Explain** how agency relationships are created and why conduct can matter as much as forms.
2. **Distinguish** fiduciary duties owed to a principal from duties owed to all parties.
3. **Identify** actual authority, apparent authority, ratification, and agency by estoppel.
4. **Apply** agency disclosure and confidentiality principles to buyer, seller, and dual-agency situations.
5. **Recognize** conflicts of interest and the limits of informed consent.
6. **Use** a disciplined communication process when representation is uncertain.
7. **Escalate** agency disputes and high-risk facts before acting.

---

## 1. Agency Is a Legal Relationship, Not a Sales Label

Agency exists when one person, the agent, is authorized to act on behalf of another, the principal, in dealings with third parties. In real estate, a broker commonly acts as agent for a seller, buyer, landlord, tenant, or property owner. A salesperson acts on behalf of the broker and within broker supervision. Agency changes the legal and ethical stakes of ordinary conversations because the agent may owe loyalty, confidentiality, disclosure, obedience to lawful instructions, reasonable care, and accounting to the principal.

Do not assume that agency begins only when a commission agreement is signed, or that it cannot arise without one. Written agreements and disclosures are the safest and expected professional way to clarify representation, but conduct and communications can create expectations and legal consequences. If an agent gives individualized advice, negotiates for someone, receives confidential bargaining information, or says “I represent you,” the relationship may be more complicated than an open-house visitor relationship.

The practical rule is simple: clarify representation early, use approved written disclosures and agreements, do not overpromise, and bring the broker in immediately when the relationship is uncertain. A client cannot give informed consent to an agency relationship they do not understand.

### Worked Example 1 — The open-house conversation

At an open house, a visitor tells the listing agent, “I will pay up to $1.2 million, but do not tell anyone.” The agent should not casually promise buyer representation or repeat the number to the seller without analyzing the actual relationship and duties. The agent should explain whom they represent, provide required agency information, avoid inducing reliance, and consult the broker if the visitor expects confidentiality. A five-minute conversation can create a problem if the agent acts as though every visitor is a client only when convenient.

## 2. Fiduciary Duties and Duties to Everyone

An agent’s fiduciary duties run to the principal. While terminology and specific duties may be applied to particular facts by courts, students should understand the core concepts:

- **Loyalty:** place the principal’s interests ahead of the agent’s undisclosed personal interest.
- **Obedience:** follow lawful principal instructions within the scope of authority.
- **Confidentiality:** protect nonpublic information that could harm the principal’s negotiating position or privacy.
- **Disclosure:** reveal material information the principal should know, including conflicts and material facts learned within the agency role.
- **Reasonable care and diligence:** perform licensed work competently and attentively.
- **Accounting:** properly handle money, property, and records belonging to others.

Fiduciary duty does not authorize dishonesty toward others. A real estate licensee still owes honesty, fair dealing, and required disclosures to people who are not the principal. An agent cannot conceal a known material fact, make a false statement, or use discriminatory practices because the principal tells them to “protect the deal.” Loyalty means lawful advocacy, not blind obedience.

### Confidential information is not a bargaining tool

Examples of confidential information can include a seller’s willingness to accept less, a buyer’s maximum price, a party’s urgency, family or medical information, financial documents, or an unannounced motivation for moving. Do not disclose it without authority merely because it would help the other side negotiate. A public listing price, recorded easement, or disclosed repair invoice may be information; it is not necessarily confidential client information.

### Worked Example 2 — The desperate seller

A listing client privately says they will accept $40,000 below list price because they must relocate. A buyer’s agent calls asking why the seller is moving and whether the price is flexible. The listing agent should not reveal the seller’s bottom line or personal circumstances without authorization. The agent can communicate the seller’s authorized position and present offers. If the seller wants a particular fact shared, document that instruction.

## 3. Authority: What the Agent May Actually Do

**Actual authority** is authority the principal intentionally grants to the agent, expressly or by implication. A signed listing agreement may expressly authorize marketing and offer presentation. Authority can also be implied when an action is reasonably necessary to carry out an authorized task.

**Apparent authority** concerns a third party’s reasonable belief that the agent has authority based on the principal’s words or conduct. It can create risk when a broker allows someone to appear authorized beyond their actual role. **Ratification** occurs when a principal, with knowledge of material facts, accepts or affirms an unauthorized act. **Agency by estoppel** can arise when a principal’s conduct leads another to reasonably rely on an appearance of agency and suffer harm.

Students should not use these labels to decide a dispute in the field. Use them to recognize why clear authority and careful communication matter. Do not sign for a client, alter a contract, promise a repair, release funds, or agree to a commission change unless you have actual authority and broker approval. “The client will probably agree” is not authority.

### Worked Example 3 — The unauthorized repair promise

During inspections, a buyer’s agent tells the listing agent that the seller “will surely credit $8,000 for the roof.” The seller has not approved a credit. The statement can create reliance and negotiation confusion. The correct approach is to present the buyer’s request accurately, avoid speaking for the seller, and wait for authorized written response. A good agent is persuasive without pretending to have power they do not have.

## 4. Disclosure and Informed Consent

California requires agency disclosures in real estate transactions in prescribed circumstances, and broker policy may require earlier or broader documentation. The student’s goal is not to memorize a form name in isolation. The goal is to make the consumer understand: Who represents whom? What duties does that representative owe? Is there a potential dual agency? What confidential information will be protected? What are the client’s alternatives?

Disclosure should occur early enough to be meaningful. Handing a consumer a form at the final signing without explanation may satisfy neither ethical service nor the purpose of informed consent. Use approved forms, provide required notices, answer factual questions within your role, and refer legal questions. The client should have time to choose whether and how to proceed.

## 5. Dual Agency and Conflicts

Dual agency exists when the same broker represents both buyer and seller in the same transaction. It can also be implicated through affiliated licensees under the same broker depending on facts and applicable law. Dual agency is not “two separate agents so there is no conflict.” The relationship requires careful disclosure and informed consent, and it limits what confidential bargaining information may be used or disclosed.

The practical tension is obvious: a seller wants the highest price and strongest terms; a buyer wants the lowest price and protective terms. An agent in dual agency cannot secretly give either side the other’s confidential bottom line. Both clients need transparent disclosure and an opportunity to make an informed choice. Follow the broker’s procedures; do not invent a workaround through informal “team” labels or private messages.

Other conflicts include buying a client’s property yourself, referring a client to a business you own, representing relatives with adverse interests, accepting undisclosed compensation, or recommending a transaction because it benefits the agent. A conflict is not always forbidden, but it is never solved by silence. Identify it early, disclose it through the authorized process, obtain any required informed consent, and step away or seek legal/broker direction when consent is not enough.

### Worked Example 4 — The investor offer

Your investor friend wants to buy your listing at a discount. Before discussing price, notify the responsible broker. The seller must receive clear, authorized disclosure of your interest and have the opportunity for informed decision-making. Do not use seller urgency, confidential information, or your control of marketing to benefit your friend. If the broker determines the conflict cannot be managed, do not proceed.

## 6. Agency Terminology in Real Conversations

Avoid vague phrases such as “I am kind of helping everyone.” Use plain, accurate language:

- “I represent the seller in this transaction; I can provide property information, but I cannot give you confidential seller information.”
- “Before I give individualized advice or negotiate for you, let’s discuss representation and complete the broker-approved process.”
- “I cannot share your maximum price or private financial information without your authorization.”
- “This situation may create a conflict. I need to involve my broker before we take the next step.”

These statements do not weaken rapport. They make the relationship honest. Consumers can make better decisions when the agent does not blur loyalty, confidentiality, and marketing.

## 7. The Agency File

The file should allow a later reviewer to understand the relationship from first contact through closing or cancellation. Preserve agency disclosures, agreements, representation communications, client instructions, consent documents, material referrals, conflict disclosures, offers, and broker consultations. Keep personal texts or private notes from becoming the only record of an important instruction.

When a client changes an instruction, confirm the updated direction in the approved system. When you give a referral, document whether you disclosed a relationship or compensation as required. When representation ends, follow brokerage procedure for notices, file retention, and confidential information. Confidentiality can continue after a transaction; it is not automatically erased by a closing.

## 8. Agency Problems Often Begin With Small Shortcuts

Many agency disputes start with language that sounded harmless at the time. “Do not worry; I am on your side.” “I can tell you what the seller will take.” “We do this all the time.” “Send me your documents and I will handle it.” These statements can create expectations about representation, authority, confidentiality, and expertise. A professional agent does not avoid being helpful; they make help accurate. State your role, obtain proper authorization, and separate factual information from individualized advice.

The same caution applies to teams. A team name, assistant relationship, shared marketing account, or office friendship does not determine agency. Ask which broker is involved, which licensees represent whom, which agreements are in place, and how confidential information is being controlled. Do not assume that a colleague may see or use a client’s private information merely because the two of you work under the same brand.

### Worked Example 5 — The team chat

A buyer’s agent posts in a team chat that their client “will go way above asking price if necessary.” A listing-side teammate later works with the seller. The original message may have spread confidential bargaining information beyond people who needed it. The right practice is to limit access, use approved transaction records, clarify agency relationships, and involve the broker before internal communications create a conflict. “Internal” does not mean “free to share.”

### Consent must be informed, voluntary, and documented

Consent is not a magic word. A consumer should receive enough timely information to understand the relationship, the material conflict, the alternatives, and the foreseeable consequences. They should have a real opportunity to ask questions and choose. A hurried signature after a conflict has already shaped negotiations is weaker than early, plain-language disclosure. The broker’s required forms and processes are designed to create this clarity; use them faithfully.

Informed consent also has limits. Some conflicts may be so serious that disclosure alone is not enough, or a client may choose not to consent. Do not pressure a client by saying that “everyone does dual agency” or that they will lose a property unless they agree. Bring the issue to the broker and follow the authorized path, which may include changing the representation structure or declining the transaction.

### The conflict checklist

Before you accept a new client, referral, investment opportunity, fee, or special instruction, ask:

1. Could my personal interest or another client’s interest affect my judgment?
2. Do I possess confidential information that could be relevant to the new matter?
3. Does the broker need to approve, disclose, or supervise this arrangement?
4. Can the client make a voluntary informed decision after appropriate disclosure?
5. Would I be comfortable explaining the arrangement in a complete transaction file?

If the answer raises doubt, stop before taking the next step. A lost commission is recoverable; a loyalty breach can damage a client and career.

Good agency practice is visible in small decisions: ask before sharing a financial document, confirm a changed instruction in writing, say when you represent only one side, and use the broker before an attractive opportunity becomes a hidden conflict. Those actions may feel slower at first. They become faster once they are built into the agent’s normal workflow, and they preserve the trust that gives agency its value.

Consistent, transparent agency conduct protects clients, reduces disputes, and lets every party know whose interests the licensee is authorized to advance.

That clarity is the foundation for lawful negotiation, meaningful confidentiality, and the client’s informed decisions throughout the transaction.

Keep it visible, documented, and current from first contact through post-closing follow-up.

Never substitute assumption for authority.

Always verify.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Principal** | Person who authorizes an agent to act on their behalf. |
| **Fiduciary duty** | Heightened duty of loyalty, care, confidentiality, disclosure, and accounting owed by an agent to a principal. |
| **Actual authority** | Authority intentionally granted by the principal. |
| **Apparent authority** | Authority a third party reasonably believes exists because of the principal’s conduct. |
| **Ratification** | Principal’s later approval of an unauthorized act with material knowledge. |
| **Dual agency** | Representation of both buyer and seller in the same transaction by the same broker. |
| **Conflict of interest** | Personal or competing interest that can impair an agent’s loyalty or judgment. |
| **Informed consent** | Agreement made after adequate disclosure of material information. |

## 10. Exam and Practice Tips

- Agency can involve conduct and reliance; clarify it early in writing.
- Fiduciary loyalty does not permit dishonesty or concealment.
- Never disclose a client’s bottom line or private motivation without authority.
- Do not promise a client will accept a term unless you have actual authority.
- Dual agency requires transparency and careful protection of confidential information.
- Conflicts must be identified and managed through the broker; silence is not consent.
- Preserve disclosures, instructions, consent, and broker consultation in the transaction file.
