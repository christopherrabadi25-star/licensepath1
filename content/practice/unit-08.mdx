# Unit 8 — Writing & Negotiating Offers

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Complete** a residential purchase agreement accurately, identifying the terms that most affect outcome.
2. **Advise** on offer strategy beyond price — terms, timing, and contingency structure.
3. **Explain** the real cost of waiving each contingency.
4. **Present** offers to a seller in a way that supports a sound decision.
5. **Handle** multiple-offer situations without creating a binding conflict or a fair housing problem.
6. **Apply** the rules governing counteroffers, backup offers, and revocation.
7. **Calculate** deposit amounts, credits, and net comparisons between competing offers.

---

## 1. What Actually Makes an Offer Strong

New agents believe price wins. Price matters most, but a seller comparing two offers is really comparing **certainty of closing** at a given number.

**The levers, roughly in order of weight:**

1. **Price** — but net price, not gross (Section 6).
2. **Financing type and strength** — cash, then conventional with large down payment, then FHA/VA, roughly. What actually matters is the underwriting risk each carries.
3. **Deposit size** — a larger deposit signals commitment and increases the seller's remedy on default.
4. **Contingency structure** — shorter periods and fewer contingencies reduce the seller's risk.
5. **Close date and possession flexibility** — a seller who needs 45 days and a rent-back cares enormously.
6. **Cleanliness of the offer** — complete, correctly written, all disclosures acknowledged, preapproval attached. Sloppy offers signal a sloppy escrow.

**Where new agents lose deals they should win:** submitting an incomplete offer with a generic prequalification letter and no cover explanation. The listing agent is presenting to a seller who will ask "will this close?" Make that answer easy.

## 2. Completing the Purchase Agreement

The architecture is in Principles Unit 8. In practice, the terms that most often go wrong:

**Buyer names.** Exactly as they will take title. "And/or assignee" has real consequences and some sellers refuse it.

**Deposit.** State the amount and when it is delivered. Remember the deposit is a **trust fund** — into escrow, a trust account, or the principal's hands within **three business days**, with the clock running from acceptance when the offer directs the check be held uncashed pending acceptance (Principles Unit 18).

**Financing terms.** Loan type, amount, and whether the offer is contingent on it. If the buyer is putting 20% down, say so — it is a selling point.

**Contingency periods.** Investigation, appraisal, loan, title, HOA documents. **Calendar every one the day of acceptance.**

**Inclusions and exclusions.** Settle fixtures in writing (Principles Unit 2).

**Closing and possession.** These are separate. If the seller needs a rent-back, address it now rather than three days before closing.

**Liquidated damages and arbitration.** Both require **separate signatures or initials** to be effective (Principles Unit 8). Explain what each means before the buyer initials — arbitration in particular waives a jury trial, and buyers should know that.

**Time is of the essence.** Every deadline is material once this clause applies.

## 3. Contingencies: What Waiving Actually Costs

In competitive markets buyers waive contingencies. Your job is to make sure they understand what they are giving up, in writing.

**Investigation contingency** — the broadest protection. Waiving it means accepting the property's condition sight-unseen in terms of recourse. A buyer who later finds a $40,000 sewer problem has no contractual exit. **Alternative:** a shortened period, or inspecting *before* offering.

**Appraisal contingency** — protects against paying above appraised value. Waiving means the buyer covers any gap in cash. **Quantify it:** "If it appraises $30,000 low, you need $30,000 more cash at closing. Do you have it?" Many buyers say yes without doing the arithmetic.

**Loan contingency** — the most dangerous to waive. If financing fails after removal, the buyer is in default and the seller may pursue liquidated damages up to 3% of the price on owner-occupied 1–4 units (Principles Unit 7). On an $800,000 home that is **$24,000**.

**A rule worth stating plainly to buyers:** never remove the loan contingency because the lender says things "look good." Remove it when underwriting has approved the file in writing.

**Sale-of-buyer's-property contingency** — sellers resist these. Options include a shorter period, a kick-out clause, or bridge financing.

**Document the advice.** When a buyer waives against your recommendation, email them the same day confirming what you advised and that they elected otherwise. That email is your file.

## 4. Presenting Offers

**Present every offer.** You may not screen offers by any protected characteristic (Unit 3), and you generally must present all offers received unless the seller has instructed otherwise in writing and lawfully.

**Present them usefully.** A seller staring at four offers needs a comparison, not a stack of PDFs. Build a **net comparison** (Section 6) showing each offer's net proceeds, contingency exposure, financing strength, and timeline.

**Do not editorialize about the buyers.** Terms, financing, and timeline — never characteristics, and never anything that could function as a proxy for them. "The Nguyens seem like a lovely family" is a fair housing problem, not a compliment.

**Buyer love letters** deserve their own warning. Letters describing a buyer's family, religion, or background create real fair housing exposure for the seller and the agents, because they inject protected characteristics into the decision. Some brokerages prohibit them outright. The defensible practice is to decline to transmit them and tell the buyer why. <!-- VERIFY: current California law and brokerage-policy landscape on buyer letters -->

## 5. Multiple Offers

**The mechanical risk:** binding to two buyers at once. Use a **multiple counteroffer** form that expressly provides no contract forms until the seller signs a final acceptance of one buyer's response (Principles Unit 8). Using ordinary counteroffers with several buyers is how an agent creates two contracts on one house.

**The seller's options** with multiple offers:
- Accept the best one outright.
- Counter one.
- Counter several via multiple counteroffer.
- Ask all parties for highest and best.
- Accept one and take a **backup offer** in first position.

**"Highest and best"** is common but not free — some strong buyers walk rather than bid against themselves. Advise the seller on that trade-off.

**Escalation clauses** (automatically increasing the offer above competing bids up to a cap) are used in some markets and disfavored in others; they reveal the buyer's ceiling and can create disputes about proof of the competing offer. Check your brokerage's position before writing one.

**What you must not do:**
- Disclose the terms of one buyer's offer to another without the seller's authorization.
- Misrepresent that competing offers exist when they do not — that is fraud, and it is a common informal practice that agents rationalize.
- Screen or rank offers by protected characteristics.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Net comparison of competing offers (Covina)

A seller receives three offers on a home listed at $845,000. Total commission 5%; other closing costs $9,800.

| | Offer A | Offer B | Offer C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | $862,000 | $850,000 | $845,000 |
| Financing | FHA, 3.5% down | Conventional, 25% down | Cash |
| Seller credit requested | $12,000 | $0 | $0 |
| Contingencies | All, 17/21 days | Investigation only, 10 days | None |
| Close | 45 days | 30 days | 14 days |

**Net walkthrough.**

*Offer A:* $862,000 − $43,100 (5%) − $9,800 − $12,000 credit = **$797,100**
*Offer B:* $850,000 − $42,500 − $9,800 = **$797,700**
*Offer C:* $845,000 − $42,250 − $9,800 = **$792,950**

**The headline price ranks A > B > C. The net ranks B > A > C**, and B leads by only $600 over A while carrying dramatically less risk: one contingency instead of four, ten days instead of twenty-one, and 25% down instead of 3.5%.

Offer C nets $4,750 less than B — but closes in 14 days with no contingencies and no financing risk at all. Whether that certainty is worth $4,750 is **the seller's decision**, and your job is to present the comparison clearly, not to make it for them.

The lesson: **the highest offer was not the best offer**, and a seller handed three PDFs would probably have taken A.

### Example 2 — The appraisal gap, quantified (Arcadia)

Your buyer wants to offer $1,240,000 on a home listed at $1,175,000 and asks whether to waive the appraisal contingency.

**The conversation to have.**
1. Recent comparables support roughly $1,180,000–$1,205,000.
2. If it appraises at $1,190,000, the gap is $1,240,000 − $1,190,000 = **$50,000**.
3. The lender lends on the **lesser of price or appraised value**. At 80% of $1,190,000, the loan is $952,000 — meaning the buyer needs $1,240,000 − $952,000 = **$288,000 in cash**, versus the $248,000 they planned on 20% of the purchase price.
4. **Question:** "Do you have an additional $40,000 available beyond your down payment, and are you willing to spend it on this house?"

If the answer is yes, waiving is a legitimate strategic choice. If it is "probably," the answer is no — and a partial waiver (covering a gap up to a stated cap) is often the better structure.

### Example 3 — The multiple counteroffer that bound twice (Ontario)

A seller receives two offers and the agent sends each buyer a standard counteroffer at $872,000. Both buyers sign and return within hours.

**Analysis.** The seller may now be bound to **two contracts on one property** — a genuine crisis with no clean resolution. One buyer will likely sue for specific performance (available in real estate because land is unique — Principles Unit 7), and the agent's errors and omissions carrier is about to be involved.

**The prevention** was a single form: a **multiple counteroffer** providing that no contract forms until the seller signs a final acceptance of one buyer's response. This is exactly why that form exists, and using ordinary counteroffers with multiple buyers is a known, avoidable error.

### Example 4 — The love letter (Pasadena)

Your buyers write a heartfelt letter describing their young family, their church community, and their hope to raise their children in the home. They include a photo. They ask you to submit it with the offer.

**Analysis.** The letter discloses **familial status, religion, and — via the photo — race or national origin**. If the seller chooses this buyer, or rejects another, the letter is evidence that protected characteristics entered the decision. That exposes the seller, both brokerages, and you.

**What to do:** explain kindly and specifically. "I can't submit this, and I want to tell you why rather than just refusing. It describes your family and your faith, and fair housing law keeps those out of the seller's decision entirely. If it went in and another buyer later claimed they were rejected for who they are, that letter becomes the evidence. What we *can* do is strengthen the offer where it actually counts — deposit, timeline, and contingency structure."

Then do that. Buyers who wanted to write a letter usually want to win, and there are lawful ways to help them.

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Net comparison** | A side-by-side analysis of competing offers by net proceeds, contingency exposure, financing strength, and timeline. |
| **Multiple counteroffer** | A counteroffer to several buyers structured so no contract forms until the seller signs a final acceptance of one response. |
| **Backup offer** | An offer that automatically takes effect if the primary contract falls through; states its numbered position. |
| **Highest and best** | A request that all offerors submit their strongest terms by a deadline. |
| **Escalation clause** | A provision automatically raising an offer above competing bids up to a stated cap; reveals the buyer's ceiling. |
| **Kick-out clause** | A provision allowing a seller with a contingent contract to continue marketing and require the buyer to perform or release. |
| **Appraisal gap** | The difference between contract price and appraised value, which the buyer must cover in cash. |
| **Buyer letter** | A personal letter from buyer to seller; creates fair housing exposure by injecting protected characteristics into the decision. |
| **Proof of funds** | Documentation that a buyer holds the cash required for the down payment and closing costs. |

## 8. Practice Tips

- **Submit clean, complete offers with a real preapproval.** Sloppy offers lose to equal offers.
- **Calendar every contingency deadline the day of acceptance.**
- **Quantify what waiving costs** before a buyer waives — especially the appraisal gap in dollars.
- **Never remove the loan contingency on "it looks good."** Wait for written underwriting approval.
- **Document contrary advice the same day**, in writing.
- **Use a multiple counteroffer form** whenever countering more than one buyer. Never ordinary counteroffers.
- **Present net comparisons, not stacks of PDFs.** The highest price is frequently not the best offer.
- **Decline buyer letters, and explain why.** Then strengthen the offer where it lawfully counts.
