# Unit 7 — Contract Law Fundamentals

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **List** the four essential elements of a valid contract and **apply** them to real estate scenarios.
2. **Distinguish** void, voidable, unenforceable, and valid contracts.
3. **Explain** capacity rules in California, including the special treatment of minors and real property.
4. **Identify** the defects in mutual consent: fraud, mistake, duress, menace, and undue influence.
5. **Apply** the statute of frauds to determine which real estate agreements must be written.
6. **Classify** contracts as bilateral or unilateral, executory or executed, express or implied.
7. **Describe** discharge by performance and the remedies available for breach.
8. **Calculate** liquidated damages within California's residential deposit limits.

---

## 1. The Four Essential Elements

A **contract** is an agreement the law will enforce. Strip away the vocabulary and every contract question on the exam reduces to a checklist. California requires four elements:

1. **Capable parties** — the parties must have legal capacity to contract.
2. **Mutual consent** — a meeting of the minds, shown by offer and acceptance, given freely.
3. **Lawful object** — the purpose must be legal and not against public policy.
4. **Consideration** — something of value exchanged by each side.

Miss any one and the agreement is defective. Which *kind* of defective depends on which element failed, and that is the second half of this unit.

Notice what is *not* on the list: a writing. Most contracts are enforceable whether written or oral. Real estate is the great exception — the statute of frauds pulls most real estate agreements into writing, which we cover in Section 5.

## 2. Capacity

**Capable parties** means parties the law permits to bind themselves.

**Minors.** California treats a minor's contract concerning real property as **void** — not merely voidable. This is stricter than the general rule for minors' contracts elsewhere and stricter than most students expect. A 17-year-old cannot make an enforceable contract to buy or sell real property, and a later signature at 18 cannot rescue the original agreement; the parties must make a new contract. The exception is an **emancipated minor**, who may contract as an adult.

**Persons of unsound mind.** The law distinguishes two states. A person who has been **judicially declared** incompetent contracts **void** — the adjudication removes the power to contract entirely. A person of unsound mind who has *not* been adjudicated may make a **voidable** contract, rescindable by that person or their representative. That void/voidable split by adjudication status is a favorite exam distinction.

**Entities.** Corporations, LLCs, and partnerships contract through authorized representatives, and authority is provable — board resolutions, operating agreements, partnership agreements. Escrow will demand that proof.

**Others.** Convicted felons serving sentences have historically faced contracting limits, and people acting under a valid power of attorney contract for their principals within the authority granted. When capacity is genuinely in question — an elderly seller with apparent cognitive decline, for instance — your role is to stop and route the family to counsel, not to evaluate competence yourself.

## 3. Mutual Consent

**Mutual consent** means both parties agreed to the same thing, and did so freely. It has two halves: the mechanics of offer and acceptance, and the absence of defects.

### Offer and acceptance

An **offer** requires contractual intent, reasonably definite terms, and communication to the offeree. An **acceptance** must be unqualified and communicated in the manner the offer requires. Some rules that produce exam questions:

- A **counteroffer rejects and terminates the original offer**. Once a seller counters, the buyer's original offer is dead; the buyer may accept the counter, counter back, or walk. The seller cannot later "change her mind" and accept the original offer unless the buyer renews it.
- An offer may be **revoked any time before acceptance is communicated**, even if the offeror promised to keep it open — unless the promise was supported by consideration, which makes it an **option**.
- An offer terminates by revocation, rejection, counteroffer, lapse of the stated time (or a reasonable time), death or incapacity of either party, or destruction of the subject matter.
- Acceptance must be **communicated**. A seller who signs an acceptance and leaves it in a drawer has not created a contract.

### The five defects in consent

Even with a signed document, consent can be defective. Each defect makes the contract **voidable** by the injured party:

**Fraud** — an intentional misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact, relied upon to the victim's detriment. **Actual fraud** is deliberate. **Constructive fraud** is a breach of duty that gains an advantage without deliberate dishonesty — the doctrine that catches fiduciaries who did not intend to lie but who breached their position of trust.

**Mistake.** A **mutual mistake** of material fact — both parties wrong about the same thing — allows rescission. A **unilateral mistake** by one party usually does not, unless the other side knew or caused it. Mistakes of *value* ("I thought it was worth more") never justify rescission; that is just being wrong about the market.

**Duress** — unlawful force or confinement compelling consent.

**Menace** — a threat of duress, of unlawful injury to person or property, or of injury to character.

**Undue influence** — taking unfair advantage of another's weakness of mind, distress, or a relationship of trust. This is the doctrine most likely to touch your practice: an adult child pressuring an ailing parent to deed the family home, a caretaker steering an elderly owner toward a below-market sale. Where you see it, you stop and refer to an attorney.

## 4. Lawful Object and Consideration

**Lawful object.** The purpose must be legal at the time of formation. A contract to violate fair housing law, to pay an unlicensed person for licensed activity, or to defraud a lender is illegal. Illegal contracts are **void**, and courts generally leave the parties where they found them — no recovery for either side.

**Consideration** is something of legal value each party gives: money, a promise, an act, or forbearance (giving up a right you had). Two clarifications the exam probes:

- Consideration need not be *adequate*, only legally sufficient. Selling a Pasadena house to your sister for $1 is a valid contract as to consideration; the price is the parties' business. (Whether it triggers gift-tax consequences is a CPA question, and whether it defrauds creditors is an attorney question — but the contract itself is not void for cheapness.)
- **Past consideration is not consideration.** A promise to pay for something already done, with nothing new exchanged, generally lacks consideration.

In a purchase agreement, each side's *promise* is the consideration — the buyer's promise to pay and the seller's promise to convey. The earnest money deposit is evidence of good faith and a source of damages, not the consideration that makes the contract binding. Students often get this backwards.

## 5. The Statute of Frauds

The **statute of frauds** (Civil Code §1624) requires certain contracts to be **in writing and signed by the party to be charged** to be enforceable. The real estate categories:

- Contracts for the **sale of real property** or an interest in it.
- **Leases longer than one year.**
- An agreement **authorizing an agent to buy or sell real property** for compensation — this is why a broker cannot sue for a commission without a signed written employment agreement.
- Contracts that **cannot be performed within one year** of their making.
- A promise to answer for the debt of another.

Understand precisely what the statute does. An oral agreement to sell a house is not void — it is **unenforceable**. If both sides voluntarily perform, the transaction stands. But if one side refuses, a court will not compel performance. The doctrine of **part performance** can sometimes take an oral agreement out of the statute where a buyer has taken possession, paid, and made improvements in reliance — but that is an expensive lawsuit, not a plan.

**Your practical rule:** put it in writing, every time, including amendments and extensions. An oral modification of a written real estate contract is generally unenforceable, so the "we agreed on the phone to move the closing date" conversation must become a signed addendum the same day.

## 6. Classifying Contracts

Four classification pairs, all testable:

**Valid / void / voidable / unenforceable.**
- **Valid** — meets all four elements and binds both parties.
- **Void** — no legal effect from the start; a minor's real property contract, an illegal contract, a contract by an adjudicated incompetent. Nothing can revive it.
- **Voidable** — valid until the injured party elects to rescind; contracts induced by fraud, duress, menace, undue influence, or made by an unadjudicated incompetent.
- **Unenforceable** — otherwise valid, but a court will not enforce it; an oral real estate sale agreement, or a claim barred by the statute of limitations.

**Bilateral / unilateral.** A **bilateral** contract exchanges a promise for a promise — the standard purchase agreement, where both sides are bound. A **unilateral** contract exchanges a promise for an *act*, binding only the offeror until the act is performed. An **open listing** is the classic unilateral contract: the seller promises to pay whoever produces a buyer, but no broker promises to do anything. An **option** is also unilateral — the optionor is bound to sell, the optionee is not bound to buy.

**Executory / executed.** **Executory** means something remains to be performed; a purchase agreement is executory from acceptance until closing. **Executed** means fully performed. Careful: "executed" is also used loosely to mean "signed," and the exam exploits the ambiguity.

**Express / implied.** **Express** contracts state their terms in words, written or spoken. **Implied** contracts arise from conduct.

## 7. Performance, Discharge, and Breach

Most contracts end by **full performance** — everyone does what they promised and the obligations discharge. Contracts also end by mutual **rescission**, by **novation** (substituting a new contract or party with intent to extinguish the old obligation), by **release**, by impossibility, or by operation of law.

Two related terms worth separating. **Assignment** transfers your *rights* under a contract to someone else; the assignor usually remains secondarily liable. **Novation** substitutes a new party *and releases* the original — the difference matters enormously to a seller whose buyer assigns a loan assumption. Assignment leaves you on the hook; novation gets you off it.

When a party fails to perform without legal excuse, that is a **breach**. The non-breaching party's remedies:

- **Damages** — money to compensate the actual loss.
- **Liquidated damages** — an amount the parties agreed in advance would serve as damages, enforceable if reasonable under the circumstances.
- **Specific performance** — a court order compelling performance. Available in real estate precisely because **land is unique**, so money damages are considered inadequate. A buyer denied a specific Claremont house cannot simply buy an identical one.
- **Rescission** — cancel the contract and restore the parties to their prior positions, returning consideration.
- **Unilateral rescission** by the injured party where the other side's conduct justifies it.

**California's liquidated damages rule for residential purchases:** in a sale of residential property of one to four units that the buyer intends to occupy, a liquidated damages provision may allow the seller to retain the buyer's deposit only up to **3% of the purchase price**, and amounts above that are presumed unreasonable. The clause must be separately signed or initialed by both parties to be effective. <!-- VERIFY: current initialing and form requirements -->

Finally, **statutes of limitations** cap how long a party has to sue. Written contract actions and oral contract actions carry different periods in California, as do fraud claims, and the clock's starting point varies by claim. <!-- VERIFY: current limitation periods before publishing specific numbers to students --> The concept — rights expire if you sleep on them — is what gets tested.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — The counteroffer that killed the deal (La Verne)

A buyer offers $925,000 on a La Verne home. The seller crosses out the price, writes $945,000, initials, signs, and returns it. Before the buyer responds, a second buyer offers $960,000. The seller wants the second offer.

**Analysis.** The seller's counteroffer **rejected and terminated** the original $925,000 offer and became a *new offer* from the seller. Until the first buyer accepts it and communicates that acceptance, no contract exists. The seller may **revoke the counteroffer** and accept the $960,000 offer. Best practice is to deliver a written revocation of the counter *before* accepting the second offer, so there is no race between the buyer's acceptance and the seller's revocation. If the first buyer's signed acceptance is delivered first, the seller is bound at $945,000 and taking the second offer becomes a breach.

### Example 2 — Void, not voidable (Fontana)

A 17-year-old with an inheritance signs a purchase agreement for a Fontana condo. Two months later, one week after her 18th birthday, she wants to complete the purchase; the seller has found a better offer and refuses.

**Analysis.** In California a minor's contract concerning real property is **void**, not voidable. There is no contract to enforce and nothing that "ripens" at 18. The buyer's remedy is to negotiate a new agreement, which the seller is free to decline. Contrast this with a contract by an unadjudicated person of unsound mind — that one is **voidable** and can be affirmed or rescinded.

### Example 3 — Liquidated damages math (Ontario)

A buyer defaults on a $780,000 Ontario purchase of an owner-occupied home. The buyer's deposit is $35,000 and the parties initialed the liquidated damages clause.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Statutory cap: $780,000 × 0.03 = **$23,400**
2. Deposit held: **$35,000**
3. Seller may retain up to the capped amount: **$23,400**
4. Balance refundable to the buyer: $35,000 − $23,400 = **$11,600**

The exam's distractor here is $35,000 — the whole deposit, which the clause appears to give the seller. The 3% cap governs, and amounts above it are presumed unreasonable. Note also that if the parties never initialed the clause, the seller's remedy reverts to proving **actual damages**, which may be more or less than $23,400.

### Example 4 — Assignment vs. novation (Chino)

A buyer under contract for a Chino property assigns her purchase rights to an investor before closing. The seller later sues the original buyer when the investor fails to close.

**Analysis.** An **assignment** transfers rights but does not automatically release the assignor — the original buyer generally remains **secondarily liable**. Had the parties executed a **novation**, substituting the investor as a party with the seller's agreement and intent to extinguish the original obligation, the original buyer would be released. The lesson for your clients: if they want *out*, an assignment is not enough. They need the seller's written agreement to substitute parties.

### Example 5 — The oral extension (Glendora)

Closing on a Glendora sale is set for the 15th. The buyer's lender needs four more days. The listing agent and buyer's agent agree by phone; the sellers verbally say "fine." On the 16th the sellers, having received a better offer, declare the buyer in default.

**Analysis.** The written contract's closing date controls. An oral modification of a contract required to be in writing is generally **unenforceable**, so the buyer cannot rely on the phone call. Whether the sellers waived the date by their conduct, or whether the buyer can argue estoppel, is now expensive litigation instead of a routine closing. **The fix costs nothing and takes ten minutes:** a signed extension addendum, delivered the same day the request is made. Teach yourself this reflex now — verbal agreements in real estate are conversations, not contracts.

## 10. Exam Tips

- **Four elements, always:** capable parties, mutual consent, lawful object, consideration. If a question asks what is missing, run the checklist.
- **Know which defect produces which status.** Illegality and minors' real property contracts → **void**. Fraud, duress, menace, undue influence, unadjudicated incompetence → **voidable**. Statute of frauds problems → **unenforceable**.
- **Adjudication is the switch** for mental capacity: judicially declared incompetent = void; unsound mind without adjudication = voidable.
- **A counteroffer kills the original offer.** Any fact pattern with a crossed-out price is testing this.
- **Specific performance exists because land is unique.** That phrase is usually in the correct answer.
- **Liquidated damages cap = 3% of the purchase price** for a buyer's deposit in owner-occupied 1–4 unit residential sales. Compute the cap; the full deposit will be sitting there as a distractor.
- **Assignment ≠ novation.** Assignment transfers rights and keeps you liable; novation substitutes parties and releases you.
- **Adequacy of consideration doesn't matter.** A $1 sale is valid as to consideration. Do not pick "void for inadequate consideration."
