# Unit 1 — The Real Estate Business & California License Law

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Describe** the structure of the California real estate industry and the roles within a brokerage.
2. **Explain** the purpose and authority of the California Department of Real Estate (DRE) and the Real Estate Commissioner.
3. **Distinguish** between a salesperson license and a broker license, and between a licensee and a Realtor®.
4. **List** the requirements to obtain and renew a California salesperson license.
5. **Identify** which activities require a real estate license and which are exempt.
6. **Explain** the relationship between a salesperson and their responsible broker, including who may pay a salesperson.
7. **Recognize** the main grounds for license discipline and the role of the Consumer Recovery Account.

---

## 1. The Industry You're Entering

Real estate is California's signature industry. The state has more than 400,000 licensed salespersons and brokers, and every one of them operates inside a legal framework designed to protect the public: the **Real Estate Law**, found in the California Business and Professions Code (starting at §10000) <!-- VERIFY -->, plus the Commissioner's Regulations that implement it.

Before we talk about law, picture the business itself. A typical residential transaction in the San Gabriel Valley involves a **listing brokerage** representing the seller, a **buying brokerage** representing the buyer, a lender, an escrow company, a title insurer, inspectors, and an appraiser. The agents you see on for-sale signs are almost always **salespersons** — licensees who must work under a **broker**. The broker is the business; the salesperson is the licensed professional working under that broker's supervision and license.

You, the agent, will spend your career at the center of that web. Everything in this course — property law, contracts, agency, finance, disclosures — exists so you can move a client from "I want to sell my house in Alhambra" to a recorded deed without harming the public or losing your license.

### Where the money comes from

Brokerages earn **commissions**, almost always a negotiated percentage of the sales price, paid at closing through escrow. Commissions are *always negotiable* between the client and the broker — there is no "standard" rate, and claiming one is fixed can violate antitrust law. The broker then splits the commission with the salesperson under their independent-contractor or employment agreement. One rule to burn in now: **a salesperson may accept compensation for licensed activity only from their own employing broker** — never directly from a seller, buyer, another broker, or an escrow company.

### Realtor® vs. licensee

"Realtor®" is not a synonym for "agent." A **Realtor®** is a licensee who has joined the National Association of Realtors (**NAR**) — a private trade association — through a local board, and who agrees to follow NAR's Code of Ethics. Every Realtor® is a licensee, but plenty of licensees are not Realtors®. The state exam loves this distinction: the *license* comes from the DRE; the *Realtor® designation* comes from a trade association.

## 2. The DRE and the Real Estate Commissioner

The **Department of Real Estate (DRE)** is the state agency that administers the Real Estate Law. Its mission is consumer protection, not licensee protection. The DRE:

- issues, renews, restricts, suspends, and revokes real estate licenses;
- investigates complaints against licensees;
- approves pre-licensing and continuing-education courses (like this one — LicensePath's approval is *pending*);
- regulates certain subdivision sales through public reports;
- publishes regulations, bulletins, and the exam itself.

The DRE is headed by the **Real Estate Commissioner**, appointed by the Governor. The Commissioner's job is to enforce the Real Estate Law, and the **Commissioner's Regulations** — adopted through formal rulemaking — carry the force of law. The Commissioner is an enforcement official, not a judge of private disputes: if a buyer and seller fight over a deposit, that's for the courts; if a licensee mishandles the deposit, that's for the DRE.

What the Commissioner *cannot* do is also tested: the Commissioner cannot practice law, set commission rates, or resolve purely private contract disputes between parties.

### Discipline and the Consumer Recovery Account

The Commissioner may **suspend or revoke** a license (or deny an application) after an administrative hearing for violations such as misrepresentation, commingling trust funds, secret profits, discrimination, or criminal convictions substantially related to licensed activity. Discipline usually starts with a complaint or audit, proceeds through an **accusation** (against a licensee) or **statement of issues** (against an applicant), and is heard under the Administrative Procedure Act.

If a licensee's fraud leaves a victim with an uncollectible court judgment, the victim may seek payment from the DRE's **Consumer Recovery Account**, funded by license fees. Payouts are capped per transaction and per licensee <!-- VERIFY: current caps -->, and a licensee whose misconduct triggers a payout has their license suspended until the account is repaid with interest.

## 3. Who Needs a License — and Who Doesn't

Business and Professions Code §10131 <!-- VERIFY --> defines the activities that require a license when performed **for another person, for compensation (or expectation of it)**. The big ones:

- selling, buying, or exchanging real property or business opportunities;
- leasing or renting, or collecting rents;
- soliciting listings or buyers;
- negotiating loans secured by real property;
- selling promissory notes secured by real estate.

Both elements matter: *for another* and *for compensation*. Miss either and no license is required. That's why the main **exemptions** make sense:

- **Owners** dealing with their own property (a "For Sale By Owner" needs no license).
- A person with a **power of attorney** handling a specific transaction for the principal.
- **Attorneys** performing legal services in the ordinary course of practice (not running a brokerage).
- **Trustees** selling under a deed of trust, executors, receivers, and others acting under court order.
- **Resident managers** of apartment buildings and certain employees of property management companies, within limits.
- Unlicensed assistants may do clerical work, but may not solicit, show property to prospective buyers and negotiate, or discuss terms.

A useful exam reflex: if someone negotiates a real estate transaction *for another, for pay, without a license*, they cannot sue to collect the promised fee, and they may face criminal penalties. Paying a referral fee or commission to an unlicensed person is itself a violation by the payer.

## 4. The Two Licenses: Salesperson and Broker

### Salesperson

A **salesperson** may perform licensed activities *only* as an agent of a responsible broker. Requirements:

- **18 or older**; honesty and truthfulness (fingerprinting and background check via Live Scan);
- **three college-level courses**: Real Estate Principles, Real Estate Practice, and one elective (LicensePath's elective is Legal Aspects of Real Estate);
- pass the **state salesperson exam** — 150 multiple-choice questions, 3 hours 15 minutes, 70% (105 correct) to pass;
- apply for the license, pay fees, and — to *work* — be sponsored by an employing broker.

There is no California residency requirement, and California has **no reciprocity** with any other state: an out-of-state licensee must qualify and pass California's exam like anyone else.

A salesperson's license, once issued, lasts **four years**. Renewal requires **45 hours of DRE-approved continuing education**, including specified mandatory topics (ethics, agency, trust funds, fair housing, risk management, and implicit bias among them <!-- VERIFY: current CE topic list -->).

### Broker

A **broker** may run a brokerage, hire salespersons, and contract directly with clients. The broker candidate needs **eight college-level courses** and, generally, **two years of full-time licensed salesperson experience within the last five years** (a four-year degree with a major or minor in real estate can substitute for experience <!-- VERIFY -->), then must pass the longer broker exam.

### The employment relationship

The Real Estate Law treats the salesperson as the **employee of the broker for supervision purposes**, even though most salespersons are **independent contractors for tax purposes**. Both things are true at once, and the exam tests it:

- The **broker must supervise** the salesperson's licensed activity, review contracts, and is responsible to the public for the salesperson's conduct in the scope of the agency.
- A **written agreement** between broker and salesperson is required, and the broker must retain it.
- When a salesperson changes brokers, the responsible broker must notify the DRE. <!-- VERIFY: current notification procedure/mechanics -->
- All advertising and contracts are done in the broker's name; the client's listing belongs to the **broker**, not the salesperson.

### Fictitious business names, branches, and teams

A broker may operate under a **fictitious business name (DBA)** only if the DRE licenses the broker under that name. Branch offices require branch licenses. Team names used by salesperson teams are permitted without a separate license if they meet statutory rules (include a team member's surname and "team," "group," or "associates," and don't imply the team is an independent brokerage) <!-- VERIFY: current team-name rules -->.

## 5. Advertising and Everyday Compliance

From your first day, license law follows your marketing:

- **License disclosure in ads:** solicitation materials must identify the responsible broker and include the licensee's name and license number as required by regulation <!-- VERIFY: current ad ID requirements -->.
- **No blind ads:** an ad that hides the fact that the advertiser is a licensee acting as an agent is a "blind ad" and is prohibited.
- **Truthful marketing:** misrepresentation in advertising is a disciplinary offense. (This is the same principle behind LicensePath's own rule to say "pending DRE approval" until approval is in hand — truth in advertising is not optional in this industry.)
- **Do not practice law:** filling in the blanks of standard contract forms incident to a transaction is fine; drafting custom legal language or advising on legal consequences is the unauthorized practice of law. Refer clients to a California real estate attorney.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Who can pay whom? (Monrovia condo)

Maria, a salesperson with Broker Chen in Monrovia, closes a $650,000 condo sale. Escrow is instructed to pay the total commission to the two brokerages. The seller is grateful and hands Maria a personal check for $1,000 as a "bonus."

**Analysis.** Maria cannot accept it. A salesperson may receive compensation for licensed activity **only from her employing broker**. The correct path: the seller may pay the bonus *through escrow or directly to Broker Chen*, and Chen may then pay Maria per their written agreement. If Maria deposits the seller's check, both she and anyone who knowingly facilitated it have violated license law.

### Example 2 — Licensed activity or not? (Rancho Cucamonga fourplex)

Daniel, unlicensed, manages his mother's fourplex in Rancho Cucamonga under a notarized power of attorney: he signs leases, collects rents, and hires plumbers. His neighbor asks Daniel to find tenants for *the neighbor's* triplex for $500 per placement.

**Analysis.** Managing his mother's building under a power of attorney falls within an exemption. Finding tenants for the neighbor *for compensation* is licensed activity — soliciting and negotiating leases for another for pay. Daniel needs a license for that; without one he cannot lawfully collect the $500, and a court would not help him collect it.

### Example 3 — Commission math (Claremont sale)

A Claremont house sells for $1,120,000 with a total negotiated commission of 5%, split 50/50 between the listing and buyer-side brokerages. The listing salesperson's agreement with her broker gives her 70% of her side.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Total commission: $1,120,000 × 0.05 = **$56,000**
2. Listing side: $56,000 × 0.50 = **$28,000**
3. Salesperson's share: $28,000 × 0.70 = **$19,600**
4. The broker retains $28,000 × 0.30 = $8,400.

Escrow pays each *brokerage*; each broker then pays their salesperson. Exam questions frequently make you run exactly this chain — and the wrong answers will be the intermediate numbers ($56,000, $28,000, $8,400).

### Example 4 — Realtor® vs. licensee (Pasadena open house)

At a Pasadena open house, a visitor asks agent Priya, "Are all agents Realtors?" Priya explains: she holds a DRE salesperson license, which the state requires; she has *also* chosen to join her local association of Realtors®, making her a NAR member bound by its Code of Ethics. Her colleague Marcus holds the same license but never joined NAR — he is a licensee, lawfully practicing, but he may not call himself a Realtor®.

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **DRE** | The California Department of Real Estate — the state agency that licenses and regulates real estate licensees and approves courses. |
| **Real Estate Commissioner** | Appointed head of the DRE; enforces the Real Estate Law and issues regulations with the force of law. |
| **Real Estate Law** | The licensing law in the Business and Professions Code, plus the Commissioner's Regulations. |
| **Salesperson** | A licensee who may perform licensed activity only under an employing responsible broker. |
| **Broker** | A licensee who may operate a brokerage, employ salespersons, and deal directly with the public for compensation. |
| **Responsible broker** | The broker responsible for supervising salespersons and for the brokerage's compliance. |
| **Realtor®** | A licensee who is a member of the National Association of Realtors; a trade-association designation, not a license class. |
| **NAR** | National Association of Realtors — the private trade association behind the Realtor® designation and Code of Ethics. |
| **Reciprocity** | Recognition of another state's license. California has none — every applicant qualifies under California's requirements. |
| **Blind ad** | An advertisement that fails to reveal the advertiser is a licensee acting as an agent; prohibited. |
| **Consumer Recovery Account** | DRE-administered fund that pays victims of licensee fraud holding uncollectible judgments, up to statutory caps. |
| **Accusation** | The formal DRE pleading that begins disciplinary proceedings against an existing licensee. |
| **Commingling** | Mixing client trust funds with a licensee's own money; a disciplinary violation (covered in depth in Unit 18). |
| **Fictitious business name (DBA)** | A trade name a broker may operate under only when the DRE has licensed the broker under that name. |

## 8. Exam Tips

- **"Only from the employing broker."** Any question about who may pay a salesperson has one answer: their own broker. Sellers, escrow, other brokers — all wrong.
- **License vs. designation.** The DRE issues licenses; NAR confers "Realtor®." Questions that swap these are testing vocabulary, not law.
- **Two-part test for licensed activity:** *for another* + *for compensation*. FSBO owners, attorneys-in-fact, and trustees under a deed of trust are classic exemption answers.
- **Numbers to memorize:** 18+ to apply; 3 courses to sit for the exam; 150 questions / 3h15m / 70% to pass; 4-year license term; 45 hours of CE to renew; broker route = 8 courses + 2 years' experience in the last 5.
- **The Commissioner regulates licensees, not private deals.** If the question describes a buyer–seller dispute with no licensee misconduct, the DRE is the wrong forum.
