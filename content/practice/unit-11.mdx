# Unit 11 — Disclosures & Risk Management in Practice

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Identify** the facts that create a disclosure or escalation duty.
2. **Apply** a practical visual-inspection process within the licensee's scope.
3. **Distinguish** disclosure, investigation, recommendation, and expert opinion.
4. **Document** client communications and material transaction events without creating misleading records.
5. **Recognize** risk patterns involving property condition, fraud, agency, advertising, and privacy.
6. **Use** a disciplined response when a client asks you to conceal, minimize, or delay material information.
7. **Escalate** issues to the broker, attorney, inspector, lender, escrow holder, or other appropriate expert.

---

## 1. Risk Management Is Consumer Protection in Motion

“Risk management” can sound like an insurance-company phrase. In real estate practice, it is the daily habit of seeing a problem early, telling the right person, documenting what happened, and refusing to make an unsupported promise. Done well, it protects the client first. It also protects the brokerage, the transaction, and the public.

Most serious disputes begin with a small decision: an agent decides a stain is not worth mentioning, treats a buyer's question as an invitation to guess, lets an incomplete disclosure wait until after acceptance, or responds to a wire-change email without a phone call. The bad result may not appear for months, but the record begins in that moment.

A useful framework is **observe, disclose, document, escalate**:

1. **Observe** facts without pretending to be an expert.
2. **Disclose** material facts promptly and accurately to the people entitled to know them.
3. **Document** what was observed, communicated, and delivered.
4. **Escalate** when the issue exceeds your license, expertise, or authority.

This is not a substitute for legal advice. It is a professional operating system.

## 2. Materiality: The Question Behind Every Disclosure

A material fact is generally one that would affect a reasonable person's decision to buy, sell, lease, finance, or accept particular terms. Materiality is not limited to defects a person can touch. It can include a known physical condition, a title issue, a governmental notice, a neighborhood condition when legally disclosable, a financing fact that changes the transaction, or information showing a prior statement was inaccurate.

The wrong question is: “Will this kill the deal?” The better question is: “Would a reasonable buyer or seller care before making this decision?” If the answer may be yes, do not decide alone that silence is harmless.

Materiality is also dynamic. A seller may not know about a plumbing issue when a listing begins. Once an inspection reveals a cracked sewer lateral during a failed escrow, that knowledge changes what must be addressed in the next transaction. A disclosure obligation does not vanish because the first buyer cancelled.

### Facts, opinions, and puffery

Facts can be verified: the roof was replaced in 2016; the preliminary report lists an easement; the seller received a code-enforcement notice. Opinions can be useful if clearly identified and honestly held: “I think this kitchen will show better with less furniture.” Puffery is broad promotional language that a reasonable consumer would not treat as a factual guarantee: “beautiful backyard” or “great lifestyle.”

The line is crossed when a promotional statement implies an objectively verifiable fact you have not checked. “Quiet street,” “turnkey,” “move-in ready,” “new roof,” and “largest lot in the tract” can all be risky if they are inaccurate or omit a material limitation. Do not call a property “turnkey” when visible water damage suggests a serious unresolved condition.

## 3. The Licensee's Visual Inspection Duty

In a residential transaction, the listing and selling agents have a duty to conduct a reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of accessible areas and disclose facts materially affecting value or desirability that the inspection reveals or should reveal. This is not a home inspection. It does not authorize you to invade privacy, move furniture, climb on a roof, open sealed areas, test systems, or diagnose the cause of a condition.

The practical standard is neither casual nor impossible. Walk the accessible property with purpose. Look for signs that an ordinary reasonably diligent professional would notice: active staining, obvious cracking, missing handrails, damaged siding, strong odors, exposed wiring, drainage concerns, visible mold-like growth, or unpermitted-looking alterations. Record observations accurately. Recommend an appropriate specialist when the facts warrant it.

### A repeatable walkthrough process

**Before entry:** review seller disclosures, prior reports made available, permits if relevant, listing notes, and known hazard or insurance issues. This gives context, but it does not replace your eyes.

**During the walk:** move systematically: exterior, entry, living areas, kitchen, baths, bedrooms, garage, visible attic/basement areas if safely accessible, systems visible from ordinary observation, and grounds. Do not “inspect” by rushing through while talking about staging.

**After the walk:** compare what you saw with disclosures. If a seller says there are no leaks but you saw a ceiling stain, do not alter the disclosure yourself or decide the stain is old. Ask the seller, document the response, and advise disclosure and specialist review as appropriate. Bring the broker in when needed.

### Worked Example 1 — The stain the seller calls old

At a Monrovia listing appointment, you see a broad brown stain in the upstairs ceiling. The seller says, “That was an old roof leak; it is fixed, and I don't want to scare anyone.”

The agent should not promise it is fixed, nor should they decide that an old condition is irrelevant. The appropriate response is: document the visible condition, ask what was repaired and when, recommend that the seller provide accurate information and any repair documentation, and escalate to the broker if there is disagreement. A roofer or inspector—not the agent—can determine whether the roof is currently sound.

If the seller refuses to disclose a material fact, the agent cannot simply comply. Discuss the issue with the broker and consider whether continued representation is appropriate. A listing is not worth a claim based on concealment.

## 4. Disclosure Is Not Diagnosis

Students sometimes swing from one error to another. One error is saying nothing because you are not an expert. The other is making a diagnosis because you want to be helpful. The professional middle is to describe what you observed and recommend the right investigation.

Say: “There is a visible horizontal crack in the garage wall. I cannot determine its cause or significance. You should have a qualified professional evaluate it.”

Do not say: “That is only settlement,” “That is definitely foundation failure,” or “It will cost about $4,000 to fix,” unless you are independently qualified and authorized to provide that opinion. Even then, brokerage policy and transaction role matter.

The same boundary applies to environmental conditions, tax consequences, legal rights, insurance availability, survey questions, and repair quality. The agent's value lies in making sure a concern is not missed—not in impersonating every specialist in the transaction.

## 5. Paper Trail Without Paper Theater

Documentation should make a transaction clearer, not merely create a pile of forms. The record should allow a reviewer to understand what happened, when it happened, what information was given, and what decision the client made.

Document:

- agency disclosures and representation choices;
- delivery of seller, agent, hazard, lead, and other required disclosures;
- material observations and the recommendation to investigate;
- contingency, notice, and extension communications;
- the client’s instructions and material changes in those instructions;
- offers presented and responses received;
- referrals, especially where the client is choosing among service providers;
- fraud warnings and verified wire instructions;
- broker consultations and escalation decisions.

Do not create fake certainty in notes. “Buyer understands all legal consequences” is rarely a useful or provable statement. “On June 3, agent sent buyer the inspection report and recommended review with a licensed roofing contractor; buyer acknowledged receipt” is specific and useful.

Keep records in the brokerage's approved system. Personal text threads, disappearing messages, and private email accounts are poor transaction files. If a material instruction arrives by text, preserve it according to brokerage policy and confirm it in the transaction record.

## 6. Recurring Risk Categories

### Agency and confidentiality

Agency risk often appears as a casual statement. A buyer tells an open-house agent their maximum price, then the agent later represents the seller. A dual agent discloses one party's confidential bottom line to the other. A team member assumes the whole team shares the same agency role without checking the actual relationship. These are not small communication problems; they are fiduciary-duty problems.

Use written agency disclosures early, clarify whom you represent, and do not disclose confidential information without authorization. When dual agency or a conflict becomes possible, slow down and involve the broker.

### Cybercrime and wire fraud

Criminals target real estate transactions because the timeline is urgent and wire amounts are large. A fraudulent email may mimic escrow, title, a lender, or an agent. Your standing warning should be repeated, not buried: **never send or change wire instructions based solely on email. Verify using a known phone number independently obtained from a trusted source.**

The “known” part matters. Calling the number in the suspicious email does not independently verify anything.

### Privacy

Do not send a buyer's loan documents, social-security information, account balances, or identification through casual channels. Do not discuss a client's financial position in an open house, elevator, or group text. Need-to-know is a practical filter: does this person need this exact information to perform a legitimate transaction role?

### Advertising and online content

A post remains risky even if it disappears after 24 hours. Confirm factual claims, use accurate brokerage/license identification, obtain permission before using someone else's photographs or testimonial, and avoid discriminatory wording or targeting. AI-generated imagery needs special care: it must not conceal a property defect or present a fabricated improvement as real.

### Unlicensed activity and scope creep

A licensee may coordinate a transaction, use approved forms within the authorized role, and share factual information. A licensee should not draft bespoke legal provisions, give legal opinions, perform inspections, advise on tax strategy, or hold themselves out as a lender. “I have done this before” does not create a license to practice another profession.

### Fair housing is a risk-management discipline

Fair-housing compliance is not a marketing afterthought. It changes the words you use, the questions you ask, the properties you show, and the way you respond when a client asks for a preference that would discriminate. Describe a property's features, not the type of person who would supposedly belong there. "Two bedrooms near a public park" describes a property. "Perfect for young professionals" or "not suitable for children" describes people and can exclude protected consumers.

The same care applies to steering. Do not select or avoid neighborhoods, schools, buildings, or listings based on a customer's protected characteristic or a proxy for it. A professional can provide objective resources, follow the client's stated non-discriminatory search criteria, and let the client decide. When someone asks which neighborhood is "best" for a racial, religious, familial-status, disability, or other protected group, do not turn the question into a recommendation about who lives where. Bring the broker in and offer neutral, reliable sources instead.

**Worked Example 4 — The seemingly harmless search filter.** A buyer says, "I want somewhere with people like us and no apartments nearby." The agent should not translate that request into racial, national-origin, familial-status, or other protected-class filtering. The agent can ask about lawful, objective needs: commute, budget, property type, bedrooms, accessibility features, and proximity to chosen amenities. The agent should document the clarification and involve the broker if the request continues to raise a fair-housing concern.

## 7. A File That Can Be Understood in Order

Good risk management is chronological. A future reviewer should be able to follow the transaction without guessing what happened between two emails. At the beginning, establish the client, agency relationship, communication preferences, and secure method for transmitting sensitive information. During marketing and escrow, preserve the material disclosures, representations, inspection communications, offers, counteroffers, notices, and written client instructions. At closing or cancellation, retain the final documents and a short factual note identifying unresolved issues or promised follow-up.

Use a neutral writing style. Transaction notes are not a place for sarcasm, assumptions about a party's motives, or legal conclusions. Record the fact, the source, the action taken, and the next owner of the issue. For example: "Seller reported a 2022 sewer repair; agent requested invoice and advised seller to complete disclosure with accurate details; broker copied." This note is both more useful and less risky than "Seller said sewer is fine."

Before you send a consequential message, make a brief decision check: **Is it accurate? Is it authorized? Is it complete enough for the recipient to act? Does it need a qualified reviewer?** That pause catches many preventable problems. It is especially valuable when an issue involves disclosure, agency, wire instructions, a deadline, or an unfamiliar form.

## 8. The Escalation Ladder

Not every concern needs a lawyer. But every concern needs an owner.

| Situation | First escalation |
|---|---|
| Seller refuses a material disclosure | Responsible broker; consider legal counsel |
| Visible condition with unknown cause | Appropriate licensed specialist; broker if material |
| Disputed contract interpretation | Broker, then attorney as appropriate |
| Suspicious wire instruction | Escrow/title through independently verified number; broker; financial institution if funds sent |
| Fair-housing concern or discriminatory client request | Broker immediately; follow approved refusal and documentation process |
| Lender status threatens a deadline | Buyer and lender; broker; communicate factual timeline status |
| Possible title/vesting issue | Escrow/title and broker; attorney for legal interpretation |

Escalation is not passing the problem away. Stay responsible for follow-up, communication, and the transaction timeline while the qualified person addresses the specialized issue.

### Worked Example 2 — “Don't mention the neighbor”

The seller tells you that a neighbor regularly drives a loud truck through the alley at 5 a.m. and says, “Please don't put that in writing; it will hurt the price.” You do not need to decide whether the conduct is legally actionable. You do need to recognize a potential material neighborhood-condition issue, obtain the relevant facts, and consult the broker regarding disclosure obligations. Promising silence is not an option.

### Worked Example 3 — The fraudulent payoff email

A listing agent receives an email appearing to be from escrow: “The seller's payoff account has changed. Please forward the attached wire instructions.” The sender name looks familiar; the domain contains one altered character.

The agent should not forward the attachment. Call escrow using the number already in the verified transaction file, report the email, alert the broker and parties as appropriate, and preserve the message. A quick independent phone call can prevent an irreversible loss.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Material fact** | A fact that could affect a reasonable person's decision regarding a real estate transaction. |
| **Visual inspection** | A reasonably competent and diligent observation of accessible property areas by a licensee; not a specialist inspection or diagnosis. |
| **Escalation** | Directing an issue to a broker or qualified professional while maintaining appropriate transaction follow-up. |
| **Confidential information** | Nonpublic client information that an agent must protect absent authorization or legal duty. |
| **Wire fraud** | Theft or attempted theft through deceptive instructions to send transaction funds electronically. |
| **Scope of competence** | The limit of work a professional may responsibly perform based on authority, education, and expertise. |
| **Risk management** | Systematic identification, communication, documentation, and mitigation of transaction risk. |
| **Puffery** | Broad promotional opinion not reasonably understood as a verifiable factual promise. |
| **Steering** | Guiding a person toward or away from housing based on a protected characteristic rather than lawful client criteria. |

## 10. Exam and Practice Tips

- Describe what you observed; do not diagnose its cause.
- “As-is” does not erase a duty to disclose known material facts.
- A seller cannot instruct a licensee to conceal a material issue.
- Document delivery, recommendations, instructions, and deadline communications.
- Never trust changed wire instructions sent only by email.
- Bring the broker in early for disclosure disputes, agency conflicts, fair-housing concerns, or ambiguous contract issues.
- The right expert is part of the professional solution—not evidence that the agent failed.
- Explain property features and neutral facts; never market or guide consumers based on protected characteristics.
