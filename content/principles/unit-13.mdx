# Unit 13 — Appraisal & Valuation

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Define** market value and **distinguish** it from price, cost, and assessed value.
2. **List** the four elements of value (DUST) and explain why all four must be present.
3. **Apply** the major principles of value, including substitution, highest and best use, conformity, and contribution.
4. **Perform** a sales comparison analysis, adjusting comparables in the correct direction.
5. **Calculate** value using the cost approach, including all three forms of depreciation and the age-life method.
6. **Calculate** value using the income approach, including net operating income, capitalization rate, and gross rent multiplier.
7. **Explain** reconciliation and why appraisers weight approaches differently by property type.
8. **Distinguish** a licensee's CMA from a licensed appraiser's appraisal and identify the line you must not cross.

---

## 1. Value, Price, and Cost

These three words get used interchangeably in conversation and mean very different things in appraisal.

**Market value** is the most probable price a property should bring in a competitive and open market under conditions requisite to a fair sale, where buyer and seller are each acting prudently and knowledgeably, and the price is not affected by undue stimulus. Unpack that definition, because every clause is doing work:

- **Most probable price** — not the highest possible, not the average.
- **Open and competitive market** — the property was properly exposed.
- **Prudent and knowledgeable parties** — neither is ignorant or desperate.
- **No undue stimulus** — no duress, no unusual financing concessions distorting the number.

**Price** is what a property actually sold for. A price becomes evidence of value only when the sale met those conditions. A distress sale between relatives at half market is a real price and terrible evidence of value.

**Cost** is what it took to create the improvements. Cost does not equal value, and the gap between them is where owners get their feelings hurt. A homeowner who spends $180,000 on a pool, a home theater, and elaborate landscaping in a neighborhood of $700,000 homes has not created $180,000 of value. This is the **principle of contribution**: a component is worth what it *adds to the value of the whole*, not what it cost.

**Assessed value** is the county's figure for property tax purposes, governed by Proposition 13 (Unit 16). Because of Prop 13's limits, assessed value can be wildly below market value for a long-held property, and it is not evidence of market value.

## 2. The Four Elements of Value — DUST

For a thing to have value in the economic sense, four elements must be present simultaneously:

- **D — Demand.** Someone must want it, backed by purchasing power. Desire without money is not demand.
- **U — Utility.** It must satisfy a need or use. Land that cannot be built on, accessed, or used has impaired utility.
- **S — Scarcity.** Supply must be limited relative to demand. Air is useful and in demand but not scarce.
- **T — Transferability.** Title must be capable of being transferred. A parcel with a clouded title or an unresolved lis pendens has impaired transferability, which is why the encumbrances in Unit 5 affect value directly.

Remove any one and value collapses. This is a favorite exam question because students memorize the acronym without grasping that **all four are required together**.

## 3. Principles of Value

**Substitution.** A buyer will pay no more for a property than the cost of acquiring an equally desirable substitute. This is the single most important principle in appraisal — it underlies all three approaches, and the sales comparison approach is essentially substitution expressed as arithmetic.

**Highest and best use.** The reasonably probable use that is **legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive**. All four tests, in that order. A corner lot in Alhambra zoned residential might be more profitable as a retail pad, but if rezoning is not reasonably probable, retail is not the highest and best use — it fails the legal test. Land is always valued at its highest and best use, which is why an aging bungalow on a commercially zoned lot may sell for land value with the house treated as having no contributory value.

**Conformity.** Value is maximized when a property reasonably conforms to those around it. This produces two companion principles:

- **Progression** — a modest home gains value from surrounding higher-value homes.
- **Regression** — an over-improved home loses value from surrounding lower-value homes.

Together these explain why "the worst house on the best street" is sound advice and why the biggest house in a modest tract is a difficult resale.

**Contribution.** As above: a feature is worth what it adds to the whole. Kitchens and bathrooms typically contribute well; swimming pools in most Inland Empire tracts contribute far less than they cost.

**Anticipation.** Value reflects expected *future* benefits. This is the foundation of the income approach — an investor buys a stream of future income, not bricks.

**Change.** Nothing is static. Neighborhoods move through growth, stability, decline, and revitalization, and an appraisal is always as of a specific **effective date**.

**Supply and demand.** Prices rise when demand exceeds supply. In coastal California, structurally constrained supply is the dominant long-run price driver.

**Competition.** Excess profit invites competition, which erodes the profit — visible whenever a hot rental submarket draws a wave of new construction.

**Balance.** Value is maximized when land, labor, capital, and coordination are in proper proportion. Too much building on too little land produces an over-improvement.

**Plottage / assemblage.** **Assemblage** is combining adjacent parcels under one owner; **plottage** is the increment of value that combination creates. Two adjoining $500,000 lots that are worth $1.3 million together as a development site show $300,000 of plottage value.

## 4. The Sales Comparison Approach

The primary approach for single-family homes. You find recent sales of comparable properties and adjust them to the subject.

**The one rule that decides most exam questions: you adjust the COMPARABLE, never the subject.**

- If the **comparable is superior** in some respect, **subtract** from the comparable.
- If the **comparable is inferior**, **add** to the comparable.

The mnemonic that has saved generations of test takers: **CBS — Comparable Better, Subtract; CIA — Comparable Inferior, Add.**

**Elements of comparison,** in the order appraisers apply them: property rights conveyed, financing terms, conditions of sale (was it arm's length?), expenditures made immediately after purchase, market conditions (time — this is where you adjust for a rising or falling market), then location, and finally physical characteristics.

**Selecting comparables:** recent sales, similar size and features, same neighborhood or a genuinely comparable one, arm's-length transactions. The fewer and smaller the adjustments, the more reliable the comparable — an appraiser weights a comp requiring $8,000 of adjustments far above one requiring $95,000.

## 5. The Cost Approach

**Value = land value + replacement cost of improvements − accrued depreciation.**

Note that **land is valued separately** (by sales comparison) and is **never depreciated**.

**Reproduction cost** is the cost to build an exact replica, including outdated features. **Replacement cost** is the cost to build a structure of equal utility using current materials and methods. Replacement cost is used far more often — nobody wants an exact replica of 1962 wiring.

The cost approach is most reliable for **new construction** and **special-purpose properties** (churches, schools, fire stations) where comparable sales barely exist and there is no income stream to capitalize. It is least reliable for older homes, where estimating accrued depreciation becomes guesswork.

### The three forms of depreciation

**Physical deterioration** — wear and tear. Worn carpet, a failing roof, deferred maintenance. Usually **curable** if the cost to fix is less than the value added.

**Functional obsolescence** — defects in the design or utility of the structure *itself*. A five-bedroom house with one bathroom, a bedroom you can only reach through another bedroom, a 1970s floor plan chopped into small rooms. Can be curable or incurable.

**External (economic) obsolescence** — loss in value caused by factors **outside the property lines**: a new freeway ramp, a shuttered anchor employer, an airport flight path. External obsolescence is **always incurable**, because the owner cannot fix what is off the property.

That "always incurable" fact is heavily tested, and the logic is worth stating for students: you can replace a roof and you can remodel a bad floor plan, but you cannot move the freeway.

### Age-life (straight-line) depreciation

The simplest method: divide **effective age** by **total economic life** to get the depreciation percentage.

**Effective age** is the apparent age based on condition and updating — which can be much less than actual age. A meticulously maintained and updated 1955 house may have an effective age of 20 years. **Economic life** is the period over which improvements contribute to value.

## 6. The Income Approach

Used for income-producing property. It rests on **anticipation**: value is the present worth of future benefits.

**Building net operating income (NOI):**

1. **Potential gross income (PGI)** — all rent if fully occupied, plus other income.
2. Less **vacancy and collection loss** = **Effective gross income (EGI)**.
3. Less **operating expenses** = **Net operating income (NOI)**.

**Critical:** operating expenses **exclude** debt service (mortgage payments), depreciation, and income taxes. NOI measures what the *property* produces, independent of how any particular owner financed it. Students include the mortgage payment constantly, and it is always wrong.

**The IRV formula:**

> **I = R × V**  ·  **R = I ÷ V**  ·  **V = I ÷ R**
>
> (Income = Rate × Value)

Value = NOI ÷ capitalization rate. Memorize it as a triangle with I on top, R and V below.

**The inverse relationship matters:** for the same income, a **higher cap rate produces a lower value**. Higher cap rates signal higher perceived risk or weaker growth expectations. When an investor says "cap rates expanded," values fell.

**Gross rent multiplier (GRM)** is a shortcut, not a substitute for the income approach:

> **GRM = Sale price ÷ Gross rent**, then **Value = Gross rent × GRM**

GRM uses **gross** rent and ignores expenses entirely, which is why it is a screening tool. By convention GRM often uses monthly rent for residential and the **gross income multiplier (GIM)** uses annual income for commercial — read the question carefully to see which the numbers imply.

## 7. Reconciliation, and the CMA Line

**Reconciliation** is the appraiser's weighing of the value indications from the three approaches into a single final opinion. It is **judgment, not arithmetic** — appraisers do not average the three numbers. Averaging is a wrong answer on the exam.

Weighting by property type:

| Property type | Primary approach |
|---|---|
| Single-family residence | Sales comparison |
| New construction, special-purpose | Cost |
| Apartments, commercial, investment | Income |
| Vacant land | Sales comparison |

**The appraisal process** in order: define the problem → determine scope of work → collect and analyze data → estimate land value → apply the three approaches → reconcile → report the opinion, as of a stated **effective date**.

### CMA vs. appraisal — know where the line is

A **competitive (or comparative) market analysis** is a licensee's pricing analysis using comparable sales. It is a normal and expected part of your job — you prepare CMAs for listing presentations and for buyers deciding what to offer.

An **appraisal** is an opinion of value prepared by a **licensed or certified appraiser** under USPAP (Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice), and in California appraisers are regulated by the Bureau of Real Estate Appraisers. <!-- VERIFY: current agency name and licensing tiers -->

The rules for you:

- You **may** prepare a CMA and give a pricing opinion for listing or purchase purposes.
- You **may not** call it an appraisal, present it as one, or perform an appraisal for a lender in a federally related transaction without an appraiser license.
- You **may not** be paid a fee contingent on reaching a particular value — that is true for appraisers and it is the right instinct for you as well.
- Never pressure an appraiser toward a number. Providing factual data and relevant comps is legitimate; asking for a value is not, and after 2008 the rules around appraiser independence are enforced seriously. <!-- VERIFY: current appraiser independence requirements -->

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Sales comparison adjustments (Glendora)

Your subject is a 3-bedroom, 2-bath Glendora home with a 2-car garage and no pool. A comparable sold for **$812,000** and has:

| Feature | Comparable vs. subject | Adjustment value |
|---|---|---|
| 4th bedroom (comp has it) | Comp superior | $25,000 |
| Pool (comp has it) | Comp superior | $18,000 |
| 1-car garage (comp) | Comp inferior | $12,000 |
| Sold 8 months ago, market up 3% | Comp inferior (time) | $24,000 |

**Walkthrough — adjust the comparable toward the subject.**
1. Start: **$812,000**
2. Comp has an extra bedroom → **subtract** $25,000 → $787,000
3. Comp has a pool → **subtract** $18,000 → $769,000
4. Comp has a smaller garage → **add** $12,000 → $781,000
5. Comp sold before the market rose → **add** $24,000 → **$805,000**

**Indicated value of the subject: $805,000.**

Run the CBS/CIA check on every line. The most common error is adjusting in the direction that feels intuitive ("the subject doesn't have a pool, so subtract from the subject") — but you never touch the subject. Only the comparable moves.

### Example 2 — Cost approach with age-life depreciation (Ontario)

An Ontario home has a **replacement cost** of **$465,000**. It is **18 years old** with an estimated **total economic life of 60 years**, and no functional or external obsolescence. The **land** is worth **$290,000**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Depreciation rate: 18 ÷ 60 = 0.30 = **30%**
2. Accrued depreciation: $465,000 × 0.30 = **$139,500**
3. Depreciated improvement value: $465,000 − $139,500 = **$325,500**
4. Add land (never depreciated): $325,500 + $290,000 = **$615,500**

**Indicated value: $615,500.**

Now a variation: if the home has been fully renovated and its **effective age is 10 years** rather than 18:
- Depreciation: 10 ÷ 60 = 16.67%; $465,000 × 0.1667 = $77,500
- Value: ($465,000 − $77,500) + $290,000 = **$677,500**

The $62,000 difference is why effective age, not actual age, drives the calculation.

### Example 3 — Income approach and cap rate (Covina fourplex)

A Covina fourplex rents for **$2,450 per unit per month**. Vacancy and collection loss runs **5%**, and annual operating expenses are **$38,600**. Comparable sales indicate a **6.25% cap rate**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Potential gross income: $2,450 × 4 units × 12 months = **$117,600**
2. Vacancy and collection loss: $117,600 × 0.05 = $5,880
3. Effective gross income: $117,600 − $5,880 = **$111,720**
4. Less operating expenses: $111,720 − $38,600 = **NOI $73,120**
5. Value: $73,120 ÷ 0.0625 = **$1,169,920**

**Now the reverse question**, which the exam loves. If that property is listed at **$1,295,000**, what cap rate does the buyer get?
- R = I ÷ V = $73,120 ÷ $1,295,000 = 0.05646 = **5.65%**

Lower cap rate at a higher price — the inverse relationship in action. And note what never entered any step: **the mortgage payment.** NOI is calculated before debt service.

### Example 4 — Gross rent multiplier (Fontana)

Three recent Fontana duplex sales:

| Sale | Price | Monthly gross rent | GRM |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $702,000 | $3,900 | 180.0 |
| B | $745,000 | $4,100 | 181.7 |
| C | $688,000 | $3,800 | 181.1 |

**Walkthrough.**
1. GRM for each: price ÷ monthly gross rent (A: 702,000 ÷ 3,900 = 180.0, and so on)
2. The three cluster tightly around **181**.
3. Your subject duplex rents for **$4,000/month**. Indicated value: $4,000 × 181 = **$724,000**

Note the limitation and teach it to clients: GRM uses **gross** rent and ignores expenses. If your subject carries a much higher tax bill or an HOA the comps lack, the GRM will overstate its value. GRM screens; the income approach decides.

### Example 5 — The over-improvement conversation (San Gabriel Valley)

A seller has spent **$210,000** over four years: a $95,000 kitchen, a $60,000 pool, $30,000 in landscaping, and a $25,000 home theater. Comparable sales in the tract top out around **$840,000**. Unimproved, her home would sell around **$760,000**.

**Analysis.** Run **contribution**, not cost. The kitchen likely contributes meaningfully — kitchens usually do. The pool contributes far less than the $60,000 spent in most inland tracts. The theater is highly specific to her taste and may contribute close to nothing. **Regression** compounds the problem: surrounding lower-value homes drag on her value regardless of what she spent.

A realistic indicated value might land near the **$840,000** ceiling — roughly **$80,000 more** than unimproved, against **$210,000 spent**. That is a $130,000 gap, and she deserves to hear it before she lists, not after 45 days on market.

How you deliver it matters: "Here are the comparable sales, here's what the market has paid for similar features, and here's the range they support." You are presenting market evidence, not judging her choices — she enjoyed that kitchen for four years, and that has real value that simply doesn't appear on a settlement statement.

## 10. Exam Tips

- **Adjust the COMPARABLE, never the subject.** CBS/CIA. If you remember one thing from this unit, remember this — it decides more valuation questions than any other rule.
- **NOI excludes debt service.** Any income-approach problem that tempts you to subtract the mortgage payment is testing exactly this.
- **V = I ÷ R.** Practice all three arrangements, because the exam asks for value, for rate, and for income.
- **Higher cap rate = lower value** for the same income. Expect a conceptual question on the inverse relationship.
- **Land is never depreciated**, and it is valued separately by sales comparison.
- **External obsolescence is always incurable.** Physical is usually curable; functional may go either way.
- **Effective age, not actual age**, drives age-life depreciation. Watch for a renovated older home.
- **Reconciliation is judgment, not averaging.** "Average the three approaches" is always wrong.
- **Match approach to property type:** homes → sales comparison; new/special-purpose → cost; income property → income.
- **GRM uses gross rent and ignores expenses** — that limitation is itself a test item.
- **Cost ≠ value.** Over-improvement questions are testing the principle of contribution plus regression.
