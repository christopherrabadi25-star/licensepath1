# Unit 3 — Fair Housing in Practice & Implicit Bias

> **Required component.** Effective January 1, 2024, any Real Estate Practice course submitted to qualify an applicant for a California license examination must include components on **implicit bias** and **fair housing**, with an **interactive participatory component in which the applicant role plays as both the consumer and the real estate professional**.
>
> This unit is built as structured activities, not reading. Exercises marked **ACTIVITY** require a written or recorded response and are scored for completion, not correctness — several have no single right answer. **Activity 4 satisfies the dual-role requirement and is mandatory in both roles.**
> <!-- VERIFY: confirm exact statutory hour allocation and completion-documentation standards with the DRE before submission -->

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Recognize** steering, differential service, and discriminatory advertising as they actually appear in daily practice.
2. **Respond** in real time to a client's unlawful instruction using scripted language you have rehearsed.
3. **Define** implicit bias and **explain** how it produces discriminatory outcomes absent discriminatory intent.
4. **Audit** your own service patterns using objective data rather than self-assessment.
5. **Apply** disability accommodation rules to live requests.
6. **Analyze** documented discrimination cases and identify the decision points where the outcome could have changed.
7. **Produce** a written personal practice standard governing how you will handle every client.

## 1. Why Reading About This Does Not Work

You already know discrimination is illegal. Every agent who has ever been sanctioned knew that too.

The gap between knowing the rule and following it is not an information gap. It is a **reflex gap**. Fair housing violations happen in the four seconds after a seller says something you did not expect, or in the unexamined decision about how quickly to return a call. Nobody deliberates their way into steering. They improvise, helpfully, and the improvisation is the violation.

So this unit is built around rehearsal. You will write responses, record yourself saying them, examine your own data, and commit to a standard. Reading the law is Principles Unit 15. This unit is about what your mouth does under pressure.

## 2. What the Law Requires — Working Recap

The full legal framework is in Principles Unit 15. The operational essentials:

**Federal protected classes (seven):** race, color, religion, national origin, sex (including sexual orientation and gender identity), disability, familial status.

**California adds, among others:** marital status, ancestry, **source of income** (housing vouchers), gender expression, genetic information, **citizenship and immigration status**, **primary language**, military and veteran status, age. FEHA and the Unruh Act reach broadly, and Unruh covers all business establishments.

**The four named practices:** steering, blockbusting, redlining, discriminatory advertising.

**Three consequences stack:** civil liability with damages and attorney's fees, administrative enforcement, and DRE license discipline. Settling one resolves none of the others.

**Federal exemptions never apply when a licensee is involved**, and never permit discriminatory advertising.

## 3. ACTIVITY 1 — Scripted Response Drill

**Format:** Written response, then recorded verbal delivery. Approximately 30 minutes.

For each scenario below, write what you would actually say — the words, not a description of your approach. Then say it aloud and record it. The recording matters: language that reads fine often sounds accusatory or evasive when spoken, and you want to discover that here rather than in a client's living room.

**Scenario A — The seller's instruction.**
At the listing appointment, your seller says: *"One thing — I'd rather not sell to a young family. The neighbors are older and it's a quiet street."*

Write your response.

*What a strong response does:* declines clearly, explains the legal obligation without lecturing, preserves the relationship where possible, and states what you will do instead. A model: "I have to stop you there, and I'd rather do it now than after we have offers. Familial status is a protected class, so I can't screen buyers that way and neither can you — it would put your sale and my license at risk. What I can do is present every offer with the terms laid out clearly so you can choose on price, financing, and timeline. Those are the things you get to decide on."

**Scenario B — The buyer's question.**
Driving to a showing, your buyer asks: *"Be honest — what kind of people live in this neighborhood?"*

Write your response.

*What a strong response does:* refuses to characterize residents, redirects to objective sources, does not make the client feel accused of something. A model: "I'm going to give you a non-answer and then something more useful. I can't describe who lives in an area — fair housing rules are strict about that, and honestly my read wouldn't be worth much anyway. What I can do is send you the census data, school reports, and crime statistics so you can look at the actual numbers. A lot of buyers also just spend a Saturday morning in the neighborhood. That tells you more than I could."

**Scenario C — The landlord's screening rule.**
A property management client says: *"Screen out the voucher applicants. I'm not dealing with that paperwork."*

Write your response.

*Note:* source of income is protected in California, and the objection here is administrative rather than financial — which makes the refusal easier to frame constructively.

**Scenario D — The colleague.**
Another agent at your brokerage says over lunch: *"I sent the Hernandez family to the listings in the east side — they'll be more comfortable there."*

Write your response.

*This one is harder, and deliberately so.* It involves no client and no immediate transaction, only a peer describing steering casually. Consider what you say in the moment, and separately whether this is something you raise with your broker.

**Submission:** four written responses plus audio of yourself delivering at least two.

## 4. Implicit Bias: the Mechanism

**Implicit bias** is unconscious association that shapes judgment without conscious intent. It is not a character accusation — it is a well-documented feature of how human cognition handles speed and ambiguity. Everyone has it, including people who are sincerely committed to fairness, and including members of the groups the bias disadvantages.

It matters legally because **fair housing enforcement examines conduct and outcomes**, not motives. "I didn't mean to" is not a defense.

**Where it enters real estate work:**

- **Time allocation.** How fast you return calls; how many homes you show before deciding a buyer is "serious."
- **Qualification assumptions.** Deciding who looks financially capable before you have seen anything financial.
- **Preference assumptions.** Deciding where someone will "fit" — the engine of steering.
- **Communication.** How you interpret an accent, a name, or a question asked in unfamiliar English.
- **Valuation.** Documented disparities in appraised values in majority-Black and Latino neighborhoods have prompted federal enforcement attention.

**The reason it survives good intentions** is that bias operates fastest exactly when you are busiest — under time pressure, with incomplete information, making rapid judgments. Which describes most of your working day.

**What actually reduces it** is not attitude adjustment. It is **process**. Standardized intake, uniform response standards, criteria set by the client rather than inferred by you, and periodic examination of your own data. Consistency defeats bias more reliably than resolve.

## 5. ACTIVITY 2 — Personal Service Audit

**Format:** Data collection and written analysis. Approximately 45 minutes. Repeat every six months in practice.

New licensees without a client history should complete this using a **hypothetical roster** supplied in the course workbook; practicing licensees use their own CRM.

**Step 1 — Pull the data.** For your last 20 client interactions, record:

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Inquiry source | Where they came from |
| Response time | Minutes from inquiry to your first reply |
| Showings provided | Number before they wrote an offer or went cold |
| Follow-up touches | How many times you reached out after first contact |
| Outcome | Closed, ongoing, lost, or you disengaged |

**Step 2 — Group and compare.** Without recording anyone's protected characteristics in your file, examine whether the numbers cluster — by neighborhood requested, by price point, by referral source, by language of the initial contact.

**Step 3 — Write.** One paragraph on each:

1. What pattern, if any, did you find?
2. What non-bias explanation might account for it? Be genuinely fair to yourself here.
3. What process change would eliminate the pattern regardless of its cause?

**Why this works.** You cannot introspect your way to accurate self-knowledge about bias — the research on this is consistent. But you can count things. An agent who discovers a two-hour response gap has found something actionable that no amount of good intention would have surfaced.

**Submission:** the completed table plus three paragraphs.

## 6. ACTIVITY 3 — Case Study Analysis

**Format:** Written analysis. Approximately 40 minutes.

The following composite cases are drawn from the documented patterns that fair housing enforcement actions and paired-testing investigations have repeatedly surfaced. They are constructed for teaching and are not accounts of specific real proceedings.

### Case 1 — Paired testing at a brokerage

Two testers contact the same agent within an hour, presenting nearly identical finances: same income, same down payment, same preapproval amount, same stated criteria of "three bedrooms, good schools, under $800,000."

Tester A is shown 11 homes across six neighborhoods. Tester B is shown 4 homes in two neighborhoods, and is asked twice whether they have "looked into what they can really afford." Tester B is also told about two areas that were never mentioned to Tester A, and not told about three areas that were.

**Analyze:**
1. Identify every distinct fair housing violation visible in this fact pattern.
2. The agent will say she treated both professionally. Explain why that belief can be sincere and the conduct still unlawful.
3. Identify the **three specific moments** where a different action would have changed the outcome.
4. What single process change would have prevented all of it?

### Case 2 — The accommodating landlord

A property manager receives an application from a wheelchair user who asks for (a) an assigned parking space near the entrance and (b) permission to install grab bars in the bathroom at his own expense. The manager approves the parking space but denies the grab bars, explaining that the building has a no-alterations policy and that the tenant "can use a shower chair instead."

**Analyze:**
1. Classify each request as an accommodation or a modification, and state who bears the cost of each.
2. Was the denial lawful? Explain.
3. May the manager require restoration on move-out? Does that answer differ for grab bars versus a widened doorway?
4. Draft the two-sentence response the manager should have given.

### Case 3 — The advertisement

A listing goes live with this description:

> *"Immaculate starter home in a quiet, safe family neighborhood. Steps from St. Bernard's and the community center. Ideal for a young professional couple or first-time buyers. Master suite with sitting area. Long-term owner, no rentals in the tract — great pride of ownership. No Section 8."*

**Analyze:**
1. Identify each phrase raising a fair housing concern, and name the characteristic implicated.
2. Two phrases create a risk that is not about protected characteristics at all. Which, and why?
3. Rewrite the description to be both compliant and more effective as marketing.

### Case 4 — The referral that never happened

An agent receives an inquiry from a buyer whose email signature includes a name she cannot pronounce and whose message has minor grammatical errors. She replies four hours later with a short note asking for a preapproval letter before scheduling any showings. Her standard practice with other inquiries is to reply within the hour and offer to meet for a buyer consultation first.

The buyer does not respond. The agent never thinks about it again.

**Analyze:**
1. What violated fair housing here, given that nothing was said or refused explicitly?
2. Why is this case harder to detect — and harder to correct — than Case 1?
3. Which of your own habits does this case most resemble? Answer honestly; nobody grades this one.

**Submission:** written analysis of all four cases.

## 7. ACTIVITY 4 — Dual-Role Play: Consumer and Professional

> **Statutory requirement.** California law requires that this component include an interactive, participatory exercise in which the student **role plays as both the consumer and the real estate professional**. Both roles are mandatory. Completing only one does not satisfy the requirement, and the course completion record documents both. <!-- VERIFY: confirm exact statutory wording and documentation standard before DRE submission -->

**Format:** Live or recorded paired exercise, 30 minutes. Solo learners record both roles using the course's guided prompts.

**Each round is performed twice — once in each role.** Playing the consumer is not filler. Hearing yourself say the discriminatory request out loud, and then hearing how a refusal lands on you, is what makes the professional response stick.

### Round 1 — The seller's request

**As the consumer:** you are a seller who, midway through a listing presentation, says the neighborhood "has changed a lot" and asks whether the agent can "keep it from going to investors or, you know, a certain type of buyer."

**As the professional:** respond.

**Then switch roles and run it again.**

### Round 2 — The buyer's question

**As the consumer:** you are a buyer who asks directly whether a neighborhood is "safe" and whether "there are a lot of families like us."

**As the professional:** respond.

**Then switch roles and run it again.**

### Round 3 — The rental applicant

**As the consumer:** you are an applicant with a housing voucher and an emotional support animal, applying to a building with a no-pets policy. Ask about both.

**As the professional:** respond, applying the source of income and assistance animal rules.

**Then switch roles and run it again.**

### Debrief — discuss and write

1. **As the consumer**, how did the refusal feel? Did it feel like a rejection of you, or like a professional explaining a rule?
2. **As the professional**, which response was hardest to deliver, and why?
3. Did either of you soften the refusal to preserve the relationship? Identify the exact words where that happened.
4. What language worked well enough that you would actually use it on Monday?
5. What did playing the consumer teach you that playing the professional did not?

**Why both roles are required:** agents who have only practiced refusing tend to deliver the refusal correctly and lose the client anyway, because they never learned how it sounds from the other chair. You will encounter these statements. Rehearsed agents stay calm and usually keep the relationship. Unrehearsed agents freeze — and freezing reads as agreement.

**Submission:** recording or attestation of all six role assignments (three rounds × two roles), plus written debrief.

## 8. Disability in Daily Practice

The rules are in Principles Unit 15. The operational reflexes:

**Accommodation = policy change, provider pays. Modification = physical change, tenant pays.**

**Assistance animals are not pets.** No pet rent, no pet deposit, no breed or weight restriction. When the disability is not obvious you may request documentation of the **disability-related need** — never diagnosis, never medical records, never a purchased "registration."

**Showing property:** ask what someone needs rather than assuming. "Is there anything about the property or the showing I should plan for?" is better than either ignoring accessibility or presuming a limitation.

**Never volunteer a client's disability** to a seller, another agent, or a lender. It is not relevant to the transaction and disclosing it invites a discrimination claim.

**Do not steer by accessibility.** Showing only single-story homes to a buyer who uses a mobility device — without being asked to — is steering, however well-intended.

## 9. ACTIVITY 5 — Your Practice Standard

**Format:** Written commitment document, one page. Approximately 25 minutes.

This is the deliverable that outlasts the course. Write a personal practice standard covering:

1. **Response standard.** The maximum time within which you will respond to *any* inquiry, applied to everyone.
2. **Intake standard.** The same questions, asked in the same order, of every buyer and seller. Draft the actual list.
3. **Criteria rule.** Your written commitment that clients define their own search criteria, and the exact sentence you will use when asked to characterize a neighborhood.
4. **Advertising rule.** Your one-line test before any listing copy or social post goes out.
5. **Refusal script.** The words you will use when a client gives an unlawful instruction — the one you rehearsed in Activity 1.
6. **Audit schedule.** When you will repeat Activity 2, and what you will do if you find a pattern.

Keep it where you will see it. Revisit it when you renew your license.

## 10. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Implicit bias** | Unconscious association influencing judgment without conscious intent; addressed through process, not resolve. |
| **Differential service** | Providing unequal service quality, time, or information based on a protected characteristic; unlawful regardless of intent. |
| **Paired testing** | Sending similarly qualified testers of different backgrounds to detect differential treatment; evidence is admissible. |
| **Steering** | Directing prospects toward or away from areas based on a protected characteristic. |
| **Source of income** | A California protected characteristic; refusing housing vouchers is unlawful. |
| **Reasonable accommodation** | A change in rules or policies for a person with a disability, at the provider's cost. |
| **Reasonable modification** | A physical change to premises for a person with a disability, generally at the tenant's cost. |
| **Assistance animal** | A service or support animal exempt from pet policies, deposits, and breed and weight limits. |
| **Practice standard** | An agent's written, uniform process applied identically to every client — the operational defense against bias. |

## 11. Practice Tips

- **Rehearse the refusal before you need it.** The four seconds after an unlawful instruction decide the outcome.
- **Never characterize a neighborhood's residents.** Redirect to objective data every time, with the same sentence.
- **Let clients set criteria.** Inferring where someone will "fit" is the most common steering pathway.
- **Count your own behavior.** Response times and showing counts reveal what introspection cannot.
- **Standardize everything.** Uniform process is simultaneously better service, bias mitigation, and legal defense.
- **When a colleague describes steering casually, that is a real moment.** Decide now what you will say.
