# Unit 4 — Listing Presentations & Seller Counseling

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Prepare** for a listing appointment, including the research completed before you arrive.
2. **Structure** a listing presentation around the seller's decisions rather than your credentials.
3. **Deliver** required disclosures and agreements in the correct sequence.
4. **Counsel** a seller on price using market evidence rather than persuasion.
5. **Complete** a listing agreement with all statutorily required elements.
6. **Handle** the common objections — commission, price, and term — professionally.
7. **Recognize** when to decline a listing and how to do it well.

---

## 1. The Appointment Begins Before You Arrive

An unprepared listing presentation is obvious within ninety seconds, and sellers are interviewing more than one agent.

**Research before you go:**

- **Public records:** ownership and vesting, purchase date and price, recorded liens, and lot and square footage. Vesting matters immediately — a trust, a life estate, or "a married man as his sole and separate property" each changes who must sign (Principles Unit 4).
- **Preliminary title work**, where your brokerage can order it early. Finding a lis pendens or an unreleased trust deed *before* you market is the difference between a solvable problem and a dead escrow.
- **Sales history and comparables** (Unit 5 covers CMA methodology in depth).
- **The property itself:** prior listing photos, permit history where available, and anything visible from the street.
- **The neighborhood:** current inventory, days on market, and absorption.

**Bring, at minimum:** the agency disclosure, the listing agreement, your CMA, a net proceeds estimate, a disclosure packet, and a written marketing plan.

## 2. Sequence Matters

California requires specific disclosures at specific times, and the listing appointment is where the sequence starts (Principles Unit 6).

**The order:**

1. **Agency disclosure first.** Deliver the statutory agency disclosure form and explain the three possible relationships *before* the seller signs the listing. This is not a formality you slip in at the end — it exists so the seller understands whom you represent before they commit.
2. **Elect** your agency relationship in writing.
3. **Then** the listing agreement.
4. **Then** begin the seller disclosure process — the TDS, NHD ordering, and the rest of the packet (Principles Unit 9). Getting disclosures completed *before* the property is marketed eliminates the post-offer termination right and prevents most disclosure disputes.

Agents who reverse this order — listing signed first, agency disclosure handed over later — create a technical violation and undermine the purpose of the disclosure.

## 3. Structuring the Presentation

Most new agents build a presentation about themselves: their brokerage, their marketing, their track record. Sellers care about three decisions, and a good presentation helps them make those.

**Decision 1 — What is it worth?**
Present the market evidence and let it speak. Show comparable *sold* properties, then active competition, then expired listings — expireds are the most persuasive exhibit you have, because they show what happens at the wrong price.

**Decision 2 — What will I net?**
Sellers think in net proceeds, not gross price. A written **seller's net sheet** is the single most useful document you bring: price, less loan payoff, less commissions, less closing costs, less prorations, equals estimated proceeds. Label it an estimate, and be conservative.

**Decision 3 — What is the plan?**
A written marketing plan with specifics — photography, staging recommendations, MLS timing, syndication, open house schedule, and how you will communicate with them and how often.

**On credentials:** a new agent's honest position is that they have their broker's experience behind them and more time to give this listing than a busy top producer. That is a real argument and sellers respond to it. Inventing a track record you do not have is both a misrepresentation and easy to catch.

## 4. Pricing Counsel

This is where agents fail their clients most often, in both directions.

**The overpricing trap.** Sellers reward agents who agree with their number, and inexperienced agents win listings by agreeing. Then the property sits, the listing goes stale, and the eventual sale price is *below* what a correct initial price would have achieved. Buying a listing with a price you cannot defend is a disservice you will pay for in months of frustration.

**How to counsel instead:**

- Present the data before the recommendation, so the number arrives as a conclusion rather than an opinion.
- Show the **cost of overpricing**: days on market, price reductions, and the pattern that the first two weeks generate the most qualified traffic.
- Give a **range**, and explain what each end assumes about condition and timing.
- **Ask what they need**, and whether the number is driven by a payoff, a purchase, or a belief about the market. A seller who needs $700,000 to clear a payoff has a math problem, not a pricing preference — and that conversation is more useful than arguing.

**On price reductions:** agree the review schedule at the listing appointment, before emotion attaches. "If we have not had an offer by day 21, we will look at the data together" is far easier to say now than on day 22.

## 5. The Listing Agreement

The elements and types are covered in Principles Unit 8. Practical completion points:

**Required and non-negotiable:** a **definite termination date** in any exclusive listing. Omitting it is grounds for discipline.

**Get right, every time:**

- **The correct sellers**, matching vesting exactly. All co-owners; both spouses on community property; the trustee with a certification of trust; all remaindermen plus the life tenant.
- **Price and terms**, including any seller financing willingness.
- **Compensation** and how it is earned, plus any **named exclusions** you negotiated (Principles Unit 8).
- **Safety clause** period, and calendar the prospect-list deadline the day the listing expires.
- **Included and excluded items** — settle the fixture fight now, in writing (Principles Unit 2).
- **Showing instructions**, lockbox authorization, and sign authorization.
- **Occupancy status**, and if tenants are in place: the lease, rent roll, deposits held, and estoppel certificates (Principles Unit 17).

**Deliver copies.** The seller gets a copy of everything they sign, when they sign it.

## 6. Handling Objections

**"Your commission is too high."** Commissions are negotiable and you may negotiate. What you may not do is claim a rate is standard or fixed — that risks an antitrust problem (Principles Unit 1). Respond by explaining what your side of the commission funds and what the seller receives for it, and be prepared to walk if the number does not work for you.

**"Another agent said they could get more."** This is the moment to be useful rather than competitive. "They may be right, and I'd rather show you my reasoning than argue. Here are the six closed comparables and the three expireds. If you see something in this data that supports a higher number, I want to know."

**"I want a 3-month listing."** Reasonable in a fast market, harder in a slow one. Explain what the term needs to accommodate: marketing lead time, escrow length, and the reality that a 30-day escrow signed on day 85 closes outside a 90-day term.

**"Can you cut your commission if I buy through you too?"** You may. Just document it clearly, and remember that representing them on both sides may create dual agency requiring informed written consent (Principles Unit 6).

## 7. When to Decline

Taking every listing is not a strategy. Decline when:

- The seller insists on a price you cannot defend and will not agree to a review schedule.
- The seller directs unlawful screening and persists after you explain the law (Unit 3).
- The title picture is unresolved and the seller will not address it.
- You cannot identify or reach all required signers.
- The seller wants you to conceal a known material defect.

Decline plainly and without insult: "I don't think I'm the right agent for this at that price, and I'd rather tell you now than take your listing and disappoint you. If things change, call me." Some of those sellers call back in sixty days.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Seller's net sheet (Covina)

A seller expects to sell at **$845,000**. Loan payoff is **$412,000**. Total commission **5%**. Estimated closing costs: escrow and title **$4,900**, county transfer tax at $0.55/$500, natural hazard report **$125**, home warranty **$650**, and a **$3,200** termite repair credit already negotiated in principle. Property taxes of **$9,180** for the fiscal year beginning July 1 are prepaid; closing is **June 20** with the buyer owning the day of closing.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Commission: $845,000 × 0.05 = **$42,250**
2. Transfer tax: $845,000 ÷ 500 = 1,690 × $0.55 = **$929.50**
3. Escrow/title, NHD, warranty: $4,900 + $125 + $650 = **$5,675**
4. Termite credit: **$3,200**
5. Tax proration — seller owned July 1 through June 19 = 349 days; daily $9,180 ÷ 360 = $25.50; seller's share = $8,899.50; prepaid remainder **credited to seller** = $9,180 − $8,899.50 = **+$280.50**
6. Total deductions: $42,250 + $929.50 + $5,675 + $3,200 = **$52,054.50**
7. Estimated net: $845,000 − $412,000 − $52,054.50 + $280.50 = **$381,226**

Present this as an **estimate**, note that figures may shift, and never hand a seller a net sheet without the disclaimer. Sellers remember the number you showed them.

### Example 2 — The overpricing conversation (Arcadia)

Your CMA supports **$1,140,000–$1,185,000**. The seller wants **$1,295,000** because a neighbor "got that last year."

**How to handle it.**

Pull the neighbor's sale. If it closed 14 months ago at $1,278,000 with 400 more square feet and a remodeled kitchen, you now have specifics rather than a disagreement: "You're right that it sold for close to that. Here's what's different — 400 square feet, the kitchen, and it closed fourteen months ago when rates were lower. Adjusting for those, this data supports about $1,160,000."

Then show the **expireds**: three properties in the tract that listed above $1,250,000 last year, sat 90+ days, and either withdrew or sold in the $1,150,000 range after two reductions. That is the cost of testing a high number, in evidence.

Then offer the structure: "If you want to test $1,225,000, I'll do it — but let's agree now that if we have no offer by day 21 we reduce to $1,175,000. I'd rather agree that today than negotiate it when you're frustrated."

You have given them the decision, the evidence, and a plan. If they insist on $1,295,000 with no review schedule, decline.

### Example 3 — The vesting problem discovered early (Alhambra)

At the appointment, the seller says the house is "hers." Public records show vesting as **"Rosa M., a life estate; remainder to David M. and Ana M., as tenants in common."**

**Analysis.** Rosa alone can convey only her life estate (Principles Unit 3). Delivering insurable fee title requires Rosa **and both remaindermen** to sign. Before you take this listing you need: confirmation all three will cooperate, all three as signatories on the listing agreement, and the family's own counsel advising on how proceeds get allocated between the life tenant and remaindermen — which is a legal and tax question you must not answer.

Finding this at the listing appointment costs you twenty minutes. Finding it in escrow costs everyone the transaction.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Seller's net sheet** | A written estimate of proceeds after payoff, commissions, costs, credits, and prorations. |
| **Definite termination date** | The end date required in an exclusive listing; omission is grounds for discipline. |
| **Named exclusion** | A specific prospect carved out of an exclusive right to sell listing by written agreement. |
| **Price reduction schedule** | An agreement made at listing time on when price will be reviewed against market response. |
| **Days on market (DOM)** | Elapsed marketing time; the primary evidence of a pricing problem. |
| **Estoppel certificate** | A signed tenant statement confirming lease terms, used when listing occupied income property. |
| **Marketing plan** | The written plan of photography, staging, MLS timing, syndication, showings, and client communication. |

## 10. Practice Tips

- **Agency disclosure before the listing agreement.** Every time, in that order.
- **Research vesting before you arrive.** Who must sign is a threshold question, not a closing detail.
- **Bring a net sheet.** Sellers decide in net proceeds; give them the number in writing, labeled an estimate.
- **Show expireds.** They are the most persuasive pricing exhibit you have.
- **Agree the reduction schedule at the appointment**, before emotion attaches to the number.
- **Never claim a commission rate is standard.** Negotiate freely; describe fixed rates never.
- **Complete disclosures before marketing** to eliminate the post-offer termination right.
- **Decline listings you cannot serve.** Plainly, without insult, and leave the door open.
