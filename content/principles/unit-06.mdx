# Unit 6 — Agency & Fiduciary Duties

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Define** agency and **identify** the principal, agent, and third party in a real estate transaction.
2. **Describe** the four ways an agency relationship is created, including ostensible agency and ratification.
3. **Apply** the six fiduciary duties (OLD CAR) to realistic transaction scenarios.
4. **Distinguish** the duties owed to a principal from the duties owed to third parties.
5. **Explain** dual agency, the informed written consent requirement, and what a dual agent may never disclose.
6. **Describe** California's agency disclosure requirements and when each step must occur.
7. **List** the ways an agency relationship terminates.
8. **Recognize** conduct constituting secret profit, misrepresentation, and breach of loyalty.

---

## 1. Why This Unit Carries the Most Weight

Agency law is roughly **17% of the California salesperson exam** — the second-largest blueprint area, and arguably the most consequential in practice. It is also where new agents get sued. Most license discipline cases trace back to an agent who did not understand whom they represented, what they owed that person, or what they were required to disclose.

**Agency** is the relationship in which one person, the **agent**, is authorized to represent another, the **principal**, in dealings with **third parties**. In real estate:

- The **principal** (also called the client) is the person the agent represents — the seller under a listing, the buyer under a buyer representation agreement.
- The **agent** is legally the **broker**. This is the point students most often miss: when a salesperson takes a listing, the agency runs between the *broker* and the client. The salesperson is the broker's agent, and the client's *associate licensee*. The listing belongs to the broker.
- The **third party** (customer) is the other side — someone the agent does *not* represent but still owes honesty and fair dealing.

Hold onto that distinction between **client** and **customer**. You owe your client fiduciary duties. You owe your customer honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts. Neither set is optional, and confusing them is how agents end up in front of the Commissioner.

## 2. How Agency Is Created

Agency arises in four ways, and only the first involves paperwork.

**1. Express agreement.** A written listing agreement or buyer representation agreement — the normal, intended route. It states the scope of authority, the term, and compensation.

**2. Implied agency.** Created by the parties' conduct even without a written agreement. An agent who begins advising a buyer, analyzing values, counseling on offer strategy, and negotiating on their behalf may create an implied agency with that buyer — regardless of whom the agent *thought* they represented. This is the accidental-agency trap at open houses.

**3. Ostensible (apparent) agency.** Created when a **principal's** conduct causes a third party to reasonably believe someone is their agent. The belief must be reasonable and traceable to the principal's own words or actions, not the supposed agent's boasting.

**4. Ratification.** Approval after the fact. If someone acts without authority and the principal later accepts the benefits with knowledge of the facts, the principal has ratified the act and is bound by it.

Two notes with practical bite. First, **compensation does not determine agency.** Who pays is a separate question from who is represented — a seller may contribute to buyer-side compensation without the buyer's agent representing the seller. Exam questions test this relentlessly. Second, an agency can be created without a signed agreement, but a **broker cannot sue for a commission without a written, signed employment agreement** — the statute of frauds requires it.

### Scope of authority

- A **special agent** is authorized for one specific transaction with limited authority. Real estate brokers are almost always special agents: authorized to find a buyer and negotiate, not to sign the deed.
- A **general agent** has ongoing authority over a range of matters — a property manager is the classic real estate example.
- A **universal agent** may act for the principal in all matters, typically under a broad power of attorney.

A **subagent** is an agent of the agent — historically, a cooperating broker working through the MLS on the seller's behalf. Subagency has faded from residential practice, but it remains testable vocabulary.

## 3. The Six Fiduciary Duties — OLD CAR

To your **principal**, you are a **fiduciary**: you occupy a position of trust requiring the utmost good faith, integrity, honesty, and loyalty. The six duties are remembered as **OLD CAR**.

**O — Obedience.** Follow the principal's lawful instructions. The word doing the work is *lawful*. A seller's instruction to conceal a known defect, or to screen buyers by race or familial status, must be refused. Obedience never covers unlawful directions, and "my client told me to" has never been a defense.

**L — Loyalty.** Put the principal's interests above your own and above everyone else's. This duty forbids **secret profits** — any undisclosed benefit you gain from the agency. If you list a property and buy it yourself through an LLC without disclosing your interest, you have breached loyalty catastrophically. Licensees must disclose when they are acting as a principal in a transaction.

**D — Disclosure.** Tell your principal everything material you know that could affect their interests. A listing agent who learns the buyer is willing to pay more than the offered price must tell the seller. A buyer's agent who learns the seller is under time pressure must tell the buyer. There is no discretion to withhold material information from your own client to keep a deal together.

**C — Confidentiality.** Protect your principal's confidential information — motivation, financial condition, the lowest price they would accept — both during and after the agency. When a buyer asks "will they take less?", the answer is not the seller's bottom line.

Notice the tension between **D** and **C**: you disclose everything to your client, and you keep your client's confidences from the other side. Both duties point the same direction — toward your principal's interests.

**A — Accounting.** Account for all funds and property entrusted to you: earnest money, keys, documents, personal property. Trust funds must be handled per the rules covered in Unit 18 — commingling and conversion are accounting failures with career-ending consequences.

**R — Reasonable care and diligence.** Exercise the skill and care a competent licensee would. This includes the duty to conduct a **reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection** of accessible areas of residential property of one to four units and to disclose to the buyer any material facts that inspection reveals — a duty California imposes on both the listing and buyer's agents. Note carefully: it is a *visual* inspection of *accessible* areas. You are not a licensed inspector, you do not climb on roofs, and you do not open walls. But you cannot look past a water stain on a ceiling and say nothing.

## 4. Duties to Third Parties

You owe the other side less than you owe your client — but you owe them a great deal.

To a **third party (customer)** you owe:

- **Honesty and fair dealing.** No lies, no half-truths designed to mislead.
- **Disclosure of material facts** you know about the property's condition. A material fact is any fact that would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to purchase or the price they would pay.
- **No fraud or deceit**, including **concealment** — actively hiding a defect is as actionable as lying about it.

What you do **not** owe a third party is loyalty, confidentiality, or advocacy. You may negotiate hard against them. You may not deceive them.

Two distinctions the exam tests:

**Puffing vs. misrepresentation.** "This is the best neighborhood in Pasadena" is **puffing** — a statement of opinion no reasonable buyer would treat as fact. "This home is in the Pasadena Unified attendance zone for X Elementary" is a **statement of fact**, and if wrong it is a misrepresentation, however innocently made. The safe practice: verify facts, attribute them to their source, and never let a sales-y opinion drift into a factual claim.

**Innocent vs. negligent vs. intentional misrepresentation.** All three can create liability. Intentional misrepresentation is fraud. Negligent misrepresentation means you should have known better. Even *innocent* misrepresentation of a material fact can support rescission. "I didn't know" is not a complete defense when a competent agent would have known or verified.

## 5. Dual Agency

**Dual agency** exists when one brokerage represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. Because the salesperson is the broker's agent, dual agency arises at the **brokerage level** — if two agents at the same firm represent opposite sides, the *broker* is a dual agent even though each salesperson works with only one party.

Dual agency is **lawful in California only with the informed written consent of both principals.** Undisclosed dual agency is a serious violation exposing the licensee to discipline, loss of commission, and rescission of the transaction.

The hardest part is what a dual agent may and may not do. A dual agent:

- **May** convey factual information, prepare documents, and facilitate the transaction;
- **May not** disclose to the buyer that the seller will accept less than the listing price;
- **May not** disclose to the seller that the buyer will pay more than the offered price;
- **May not** disclose either party's confidential motivation or financial condition without permission;
- **Must** still disclose all known material facts about the property's condition to both — property condition is never confidential.

Read that list again and notice the shape of it: a dual agent keeps *price and motivation* confidential in both directions, while disclosing *property condition* in both directions.

Some brokerages assign different salespersons to each side ("designated agency" in common parlance) to reduce the practical conflict. This is a business practice that manages the conflict; it does not by itself eliminate the brokerage-level dual agency or the consent requirement. <!-- VERIFY: current California treatment of designated agency and any statutory changes -->

## 6. Agency Disclosure Requirements

California requires a formal, written agency disclosure process in residential transactions of one to four units. Conceptually it has three steps:

1. **Disclose** — deliver the statutory agency disclosure form explaining the three possible relationships (seller's agent, buyer's agent, dual agent) and the duties of each. The listing agent delivers it to the seller before the listing is signed; the buyer's agent delivers it to the buyer before the buyer signs an offer.
2. **Elect** — the agent confirms in writing which party they represent.
3. **Confirm** — the agency relationships are confirmed in writing in or with the purchase contract, so both parties see, at the moment of contracting, exactly who represents whom.

The timing details and form names are statutory and get amended, so verify the current forms and deadlines rather than relying on any single summary. <!-- VERIFY: current agency disclosure form names, timing, and 1–4 unit scope -->

The principle behind the process never changes, and that is what the exam tests: **agency must be disclosed early, elected clearly, and confirmed in writing before the parties are bound.** The disclosure exists so no consumer ever reaches closing uncertain about whose side their agent was on.

## 7. Termination of Agency

An agency ends by:

- **Full performance** — the transaction closes, and the purpose is accomplished.
- **Expiration** of the agreement's term. A listing must have a definite termination date; an exclusive listing with no end date is grounds for discipline.
- **Mutual agreement** to cancel.
- **Revocation by the principal** or **renunciation by the agent** — either may end the relationship, though ending it wrongfully can create liability for damages.
- **Death or incapacity** of either principal or agent (death of the salesperson does not end the broker's listing; death of the *broker* or the *principal* generally does).
- **Destruction of the property** or a change making performance impossible.
- **Bankruptcy** of the principal, in circumstances affecting the property.

Note that fiduciary duties do not all evaporate at termination. **Confidentiality survives.** The seller's bottom line you learned last year does not become fair game because the listing expired.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — The accidental buyer's agent (Pasadena open house)

You hold an open house for your Pasadena listing. A visitor spends 40 minutes with you. You pull comparable sales, tell her the seller "needs to close before September," advise her that "$25,000 under asking would probably get accepted," and help her structure an offer.

**Analysis.** You represent the seller — but by counseling this buyer on strategy and value, you may have created an **implied agency** with her too, producing an *undisclosed dual agency*. Worse, you disclosed the seller's timing motivation, breaching **confidentiality**, and advised the buyer to offer below asking, breaching **loyalty**.

**What you should have done:** identify your representation immediately ("I represent the seller"), deliver the agency disclosure, answer factual questions about the property fully and honestly, and decline to advise on offer strategy. Being helpful to a customer is required. Advising them against your client is not.

### Example 2 — Secret profit through an entity (Inland Empire)

A licensee lists a distressed Riverside property at $420,000, tells the seller "the market is dead, take what you can get," and arranges for an LLC he half-owns to buy it at $360,000. The disclosure form says only "buyer is an entity." Two months later the LLC resells at $455,000.

**Analysis.** Multiple breaches stack here. Failing to disclose his ownership interest in the buyer violates **loyalty** and the rule requiring a licensee to disclose when acting as a principal. Discouraging the seller with a false market characterization is **misrepresentation** and breaches **disclosure**. The $95,000 gain is a **secret profit** the seller may recover. Add likely license revocation, the commission forfeited, and potential fraud liability. The fix was one sentence of honest written disclosure, plus the seller's informed consent — the deal might even have been fine if fully disclosed.

### Example 3 — Dual agency, done right (Glendale)

A brokerage lists a Glendale condo. A different agent at the same firm represents an interested buyer. Both parties sign the agency disclosure and consent in writing to the brokerage's dual agency. The buyer asks the listing agent: "The sellers relocated already, right? Are they desperate?"

**Analysis.** The agent may confirm **facts about the property** and matters the seller authorized her to share. She may **not** disclose that the sellers are under financial pressure or would accept less than list — that is confidential motivation, protected in both directions. If she also knows the roof has an active leak, she **must** disclose it to the buyer: property condition is never confidential, dual agency or not.

### Example 4 — The visual inspection duty (Whittier)

Showing a 1948 Whittier home, you notice a brown ring on the dining-room ceiling directly beneath an upstairs bathroom, and a fresh paint patch around it. The seller's TDS discloses no leaks. Your buyer says the house looks perfect.

**Analysis.** You are not an inspector and cannot diagnose a leak. But your **reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection** duty covers accessible areas, and a stain with fresh paint over it is exactly the red flag the duty exists to catch. You must **disclose what you observed** in writing to your buyer and recommend a qualified inspection of that area. You do not opine on cause or cost.

Notice the professional posture: *observe, disclose, refer.* You are not liable for failing to detect what a licensed inspector would find inside a wall. You are absolutely liable for seeing a stain and saying nothing.

### Example 5 — Commission and agency are different questions (Ontario)

An Ontario seller agrees to contribute $12,000 toward the buyer's brokerage compensation. At closing, the buyer's agent's brokerage receives that $12,000. The seller later argues the buyer's agent "worked for me since I paid."

**Analysis.** Wrong. **Compensation does not create agency.** The buyer's broker represented the buyer under a signed buyer representation agreement and owed the buyer full fiduciary duties throughout, regardless of who funded the compensation. The seller's payment is a term of the transaction, not evidence of representation. This has always been the rule, and it matters more than ever as compensation practices continue to shift — which is why every party's representation must be **confirmed in writing in the contract**.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Agency** | The relationship in which an agent is authorized to represent a principal in dealings with third parties. |
| **Principal (client)** | The person the agent represents and to whom fiduciary duties are owed. |
| **Third party (customer)** | The other side of the transaction, owed honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts, but not fiduciary duties. |
| **Associate licensee** | A salesperson acting for the broker; the agency runs between the broker and the client. |
| **Fiduciary** | One in a position of trust owing utmost care, integrity, honesty, and loyalty. |
| **OLD CAR** | Obedience, Loyalty, Disclosure, Confidentiality, Accounting, Reasonable care — the six fiduciary duties. |
| **Express agency** | Agency created by written or spoken agreement, such as a listing. |
| **Implied agency** | Agency created by the parties' conduct without an express agreement. |
| **Ostensible (apparent) agency** | Agency created when a principal's conduct causes a third party to reasonably believe someone is their agent. |
| **Ratification** | Approval of an unauthorized act after the fact, binding the principal who accepts its benefits knowingly. |
| **Special agent** | An agent authorized for one specific transaction with limited authority — the usual role of a real estate broker. |
| **General agent** | An agent with ongoing authority over a range of matters, such as a property manager. |
| **Universal agent** | An agent authorized to act in all matters, typically under a broad power of attorney. |
| **Subagent** | An agent of an agent, owing the same duties to the original principal. |
| **Dual agency** | One brokerage representing both buyer and seller; lawful in California only with informed written consent of both. |
| **Secret profit** | Any undisclosed benefit an agent derives from the agency; recoverable by the principal and grounds for discipline. |
| **Puffing** | Exaggerated opinion a reasonable person would not rely on as fact; distinguished from factual misrepresentation. |
| **Misrepresentation** | A false statement of material fact — intentional, negligent, or innocent — that may support damages or rescission. |
| **Concealment** | Actively hiding a known material defect; as actionable as an affirmative false statement. |
| **Material fact** | A fact that would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to purchase or the price they would pay. |
| **Visual inspection duty** | The agent's duty to conduct a reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of accessible areas of 1–4 unit residential property and disclose material facts revealed. |
| **Agency disclosure** | California's statutory disclose–elect–confirm process identifying in writing whom each licensee represents. |

## 10. Exam Tips

- **The broker is the agent.** Whenever a question involves a salesperson's relationship to a client, trace it back through the broker. Dual agency arises at the *brokerage* level even when two different salespersons are involved.
- **Memorize OLD CAR and apply it, don't just recite it.** Most agency questions are fact patterns asking which duty broke. Confidentiality breaches (revealing motivation) and loyalty breaches (secret profits) dominate.
- **Obedience covers only LAWFUL instructions.** Any question where a client orders concealment or discrimination has the same answer: refuse.
- **Property condition is never confidential.** Even in dual agency, even when the seller asks you to stay quiet, known material defects get disclosed.
- **Compensation ≠ agency.** Who pays does not determine who is represented. Expect this question.
- **Puffing is opinion; anything verifiable is fact.** Statements about schools, square footage, permits, zoning, and boundaries are facts — verify or attribute them.
- **The visual inspection duty is visual and limited to accessible areas** — but it is real. "Observe, disclose, refer" is the tested behavior.
- **Confidentiality survives termination.** Agency ending does not release you to share what you learned.
