# Unit 14 — Land Use, Planning & Zoning

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Explain** police power and **distinguish** it from eminent domain.
2. **Describe** the general plan and its relationship to zoning ordinances.
3. **Distinguish** a variance, a conditional use permit, a rezoning, and a nonconforming use.
4. **Explain** eminent domain, just compensation, and inverse condemnation.
5. **Summarize** the Subdivision Map Act and the Subdivided Lands Act and what each protects.
6. **Describe** the public report requirement and when a subdivider may take offers.
7. **Outline** CEQA and basic environmental compliance affecting transactions.
8. **Calculate** lot dimensions, area, density, and setback problems.

## 1. Police Power: the Foundation of Land Use Control

**Police power** is the government's inherent authority to regulate private activity for the **public health, safety, morals, and general welfare**. Zoning, building codes, subdivision regulation, rent control, and environmental rules all rest on it.

The defining feature: **no compensation is owed.** A city that downzones your land from 20 units per acre to 8 has reduced its value, but it has not "taken" anything in the constitutional sense, and it need not pay. Contrast **eminent domain**, where the government acquires property and *must* pay just compensation.

That line — regulation versus taking — is the central tension in land use law. Regulation can go so far that courts treat it as a taking (a "regulatory taking"), typically when it deprives an owner of all economically viable use. The doctrine is fact-intensive and litigated constantly. <!-- VERIFY: current California regulatory takings standards before teaching specifics -->

The **hierarchy** of land use controls matters:

1. **Federal law** (environmental statutes, fair housing)
2. **State law** (the Planning and Zoning Law, Subdivision Map Act, CEQA)
3. **Local ordinances** (general plan, zoning, building codes)
4. **Private restrictions** (CC&Rs — Unit 5)

Remember the rule from Unit 5: where public zoning and private CC&Rs differ, **the stricter controls**. Private restrictions may be more restrictive than public law, never less.

## 2. Planning and the General Plan

Every California city and county must adopt a **general plan** — the community's long-term blueprint for physical development. It contains mandated elements, historically including land use, circulation, housing, conservation, open space, noise, and safety, with environmental justice added more recently. <!-- VERIFY: current required general plan elements -->

The **housing element** deserves special mention because it drives so much California policy: it must plan for the community's share of regional housing need, and the state has grown considerably more aggressive about enforcing it. A city with a noncompliant housing element loses significant control over project approvals.

**Zoning must be consistent with the general plan.** This is the key structural relationship: the general plan is the constitution, zoning is the statute implementing it. A zoning ordinance inconsistent with the general plan is vulnerable to challenge.

**Zoning ordinances** divide land into districts and regulate:

- **Use** — residential (R-1, R-2, R-3), commercial, industrial, agricultural, mixed use
- **Density** — units per acre, floor area ratio
- **Bulk and placement** — height limits, setbacks (front, side, rear), lot coverage
- **Parking**, landscaping, signage

**Spot zoning** is rezoning a single parcel inconsistently with surrounding zoning and the general plan for the benefit of one owner. It is frequently held invalid.

**Downzoning** reduces permitted intensity; **upzoning** increases it. California's recent housing legislation has repeatedly overridden local zoning to permit additional units — accessory dwelling units and small-lot splits among them — and this area changes rapidly. <!-- VERIFY: current state housing law overrides including ADU and lot-split provisions -->

## 3. Zoning Relief: Four Distinct Tools

Students confuse these constantly, so learn them as a set with distinct triggers.

**Variance.** Permission to deviate from a **dimensional standard** — setback, height, lot coverage — because of a **special hardship unique to the parcel** (an odd shape, extreme slope, unusual size). A variance does **not** change the permitted use. The hardship must attach to the land, not the owner's finances: "I need the money" is never a valid basis.

**Conditional use permit (CUP).** Permission for a use the ordinance **already contemplates** in that zone but only with review and conditions — a church, school, hospital, or daycare in a residential zone. The use is not prohibited; it requires discretionary approval and typically carries conditions on hours, parking, and lighting.

**Rezoning (zone change).** An actual **amendment to the zoning ordinance**, reclassifying the parcel. This is a legislative act by the city council or board of supervisors, unlike a variance or CUP, which are administrative.

**Nonconforming use.** A use that was **lawful when established** but no longer conforms after a zoning change — "grandfathered." Key limits: it generally may not be **expanded**, may not be **rebuilt** if substantially destroyed, and is typically **lost if abandoned** for a specified period. Some ordinances impose **amortization**, requiring the use to terminate after a period long enough to recoup investment.

Quick discrimination test for the exam: dimensional deviation + unique hardship = **variance**. Special use already listed in the ordinance = **CUP**. Changing the classification = **rezoning**. Lawful use predating the rules = **nonconforming**.

## 4. Eminent Domain and Condemnation

**Eminent domain** is the government's power to take private property for **public use** upon payment of **just compensation**. **Condemnation** is the legal proceeding exercising it.

- **Public use** has been interpreted broadly, and California enacted post-*Kelo* restrictions on taking owner-occupied residences for private redevelopment. <!-- VERIFY: current California eminent domain restrictions -->
- **Just compensation** generally means fair market value at highest and best use. Where only part of a parcel is taken, the owner may also recover **severance damages** for the reduction in value to the remainder.
- **Inverse condemnation** is the owner's action when government activity has effectively taken or substantially damaged property *without* formal proceedings — destroyed access, chronic flooding from a public project, severe physical impacts.

**Escheat** (Unit 3) rounds out the sovereign powers: property reverts to the state when an owner dies without a will and without heirs.

## 5. Subdivision Law: Two Acts, Two Purposes

California regulates subdivisions through two statutes that students routinely conflate. Keep them separate by asking **who is protected**.

### The Subdivision Map Act — protects the community

Administered by **local government**. It governs the **physical division of land**: parcel maps, tentative and final maps, dedications of streets and easements, improvement requirements, and consistency with the general plan.

Rough thresholds: **five or more parcels** generally require a **tentative and final subdivision map**; **four or fewer** generally require a **parcel map**. <!-- VERIFY: current map thresholds and exemptions -->

Its purpose is orderly community development — adequate streets, utilities, drainage, and infrastructure.

### The Subdivided Lands Act — protects the buyer

Administered by the **DRE**. It is a **consumer protection** statute governing the **marketing and sale** of subdivided lots.

Its central requirement is the **public report** (sometimes called the "white paper"): before a subdivider may sell or lease lots in a covered subdivision, the Commissioner must issue a public report disclosing material facts — title condition, financing, restrictions, hazards, assessments, and the like.

Rules to know:

- The subdivider must give the public report to a prospective purchaser and obtain a **signed receipt** before taking a purchase money deposit.
- A **preliminary public report** allows the subdivider to take **reservations** — fully refundable and non-binding — but **not** binding purchase contracts.
- Coverage generally begins at **five or more parcels** for standard subdivisions, with different treatment for common interest developments and out-of-state land offered in California. <!-- VERIFY: current thresholds and exemptions -->

**The memory hook:** *Map Act = maps and infrastructure, local government, protects the community. Subdivided Lands Act = marketing and disclosure, the DRE, protects the buyer.*

## 6. Environmental Regulation

**CEQA (California Environmental Quality Act)** requires public agencies to evaluate the environmental impacts of discretionary projects they approve. The process:

1. Determine whether CEQA applies and whether an exemption covers the project.
2. Prepare an **initial study**.
3. If no significant impact, adopt a **negative declaration** (or **mitigated negative declaration** if mitigation reduces impacts below significance).
4. If significant impacts remain, prepare an **environmental impact report (EIR)** analyzing impacts, alternatives, and mitigation.

CEQA is a **disclosure and process** statute — it forces agencies to study and disclose impacts. It does not by itself forbid projects with impacts, though agencies must adopt feasible mitigation and, to approve a project with unavoidable significant impacts, make findings of overriding considerations. CEQA litigation is a major factor in California development timelines.

**Federal counterparts and other regimes** you should recognize by name: NEPA (the federal analogue), CERCLA/Superfund (liability for hazardous substance cleanup, which can attach to current owners — the reason commercial buyers order Phase I environmental site assessments), the Clean Water Act and wetlands permitting, and the Endangered Species Act.

**Environmental issues that reach residential transactions:** underground storage tanks, methane zones in parts of Los Angeles, former agricultural land with pesticide residue, mold, asbestos and lead in older construction (Unit 9), radon (less common in California but not absent), and the wildfire-urban interface issues driving the fire hazard severity zone disclosures.

**Your role is disclosure and referral, never diagnosis.** If a listing has a decommissioned fuel tank or a client asks whether soil is contaminated, you disclose what you know and refer to a qualified environmental professional.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Which tool does the client need? (Alhambra)

Three neighbors come to you in the same week:

**(a)** An owner's lot is a narrow wedge; the required 20-foot front setback would leave a buildable depth of 14 feet. She wants to build 6 feet into the setback.
→ **Variance.** A dimensional deviation justified by a hardship unique to the parcel's shape. The use stays residential.

**(b)** A congregation wants to convert a large house in an R-1 zone into a small church. The zoning ordinance lists churches as permitted in R-1 subject to approval.
→ **Conditional use permit.** The ordinance already contemplates the use; it needs discretionary approval with conditions on parking, hours, and noise.

**(c)** A developer owns a residentially zoned parcel on a busy commercial corridor and wants to build a retail center.
→ **Rezoning.** Changing the classification is a legislative act by the city council, not something a variance or CUP can accomplish. Expect a general plan consistency analysis and probably CEQA review.

The distinctions decide exam questions and, more practically, decide whether you tell a client "a few months and a hearing" or "a year and a land use attorney."

### Example 2 — Lot area and density (Rancho Cucamonga)

A developer owns a rectangular parcel measuring **330 feet by 264 feet**. Zoning permits **8 dwelling units per acre**, and **18%** of the site must be dedicated for streets and drainage.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Gross area: 330 × 264 = **87,120 square feet**
2. Convert to acres (1 acre = 43,560 sq ft): 87,120 ÷ 43,560 = **2.0 acres**
3. Dedication: 2.0 × 0.18 = 0.36 acres → net developable = 2.0 − 0.36 = **1.64 acres**
4. Permitted units: 1.64 × 8 = 13.12 → **13 units** (you round *down*; you cannot build a partial unit)

**Memorize 43,560 square feet per acre.** It appears in land, density, and price-per-acre problems constantly. Also note step 4: density questions always round down.

### Example 3 — Price per square foot and per acre (Inland Empire land)

A 4.75-acre parcel is listed at **$1,235,000**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Price per acre: $1,235,000 ÷ 4.75 = **$260,000/acre**
2. Total square feet: 4.75 × 43,560 = **206,910 sq ft**
3. Price per square foot: $1,235,000 ÷ 206,910 = **$5.97/sq ft**

**Check the reverse:** $260,000 ÷ 43,560 = $5.97/sq ft ✓

Land is quoted per acre in some markets and per square foot in others. Being able to convert instantly is a practical skill, not just an exam trick.

### Example 4 — Nonconforming use lost (Pomona)

A corner market has operated in a Pomona neighborhood since 1974. The area was rezoned exclusively residential in 1991; the market continued as a lawful **nonconforming use**. In 2023 the owner closed the store and left the building vacant. In 2026 a buyer wants to reopen it as a market.

**Analysis.** The nonconforming use was likely **lost by abandonment**. Most ordinances terminate nonconforming status after a continuous period of discontinuance — often six months to a year — and three years of vacancy will almost certainly exceed it. The buyer cannot simply resume the commercial use; they would need a **rezoning** or a **CUP** if the ordinance allows one, either of which is discretionary and may well be denied.

**What this means for you as the listing agent:** never market that property as "commercial use in place" without written confirmation from the city's planning department. A buyer who purchases on that assumption and cannot open has a strong claim against everyone who told them otherwise.

### Example 5 — Public report timing (new subdivision)

A subdivider has a **preliminary** public report on a 60-lot Inland Empire subdivision and wants to start taking $10,000 deposits with signed purchase contracts to gauge demand.

**Analysis.** Not permitted. A **preliminary** public report allows the subdivider to take **reservations only** — fully refundable, non-binding, cancellable by either party. Binding purchase contracts and non-refundable purchase money deposits require the **final public report**, which must be delivered to the prospective buyer with a **signed receipt** obtained before any purchase money deposit is taken.

If you are the sales agent on a new-home community, the receipt-for-report file is one of the first things a DRE audit will examine. Get the signature, keep the receipt.

## 9. Exam Tips

- **Police power = regulation, no compensation. Eminent domain = taking, compensation required.** This distinction anchors the whole unit.
- **Zoning must be consistent with the general plan.** The plan is the blueprint; zoning implements it.
- **Variance = dimensional deviation for a hardship unique to the LAND** (never financial hardship). **CUP = a use the ordinance already lists**, subject to conditions. **Rezoning = legislative change of classification.**
- **Nonconforming uses cannot expand and are lost by abandonment.** Fact patterns with a long vacancy are testing exactly this.
- **Two subdivision acts:** Map Act → local government, physical division, protects the community. Subdivided Lands Act → DRE, marketing and disclosure, protects the buyer.
- **Public report before any purchase money deposit, with a signed receipt.** Preliminary report = reservations only.
- **CEQA is process and disclosure**, not a prohibition on impacts.
- **43,560 square feet per acre.** Density answers always round **down**.
