# Unit 1 — Starting Your Practice

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Compare** brokerage compensation models and calculate your net income under each.
2. **Explain** the broker's supervision duties and what a new licensee should expect from them.
3. **Distinguish** independent contractor tax status from employee status for supervision purposes.
4. **Describe** team structures and the statutory rules governing team names and advertising.
5. **Build** a first-year business plan with realistic transaction and income projections.
6. **Calculate** the true cost of doing business, including the fees new agents overlook.
7. **Identify** the errors and omissions coverage and risk-management basics you need from day one.

---

## 1. You Are Starting a Business

The license makes you legally able to work. It does not make you employed, paid, or busy. On the day your license issues you are, for practical purposes, a self-employed small business owner whose only asset is time.

That reframe matters because most new agents fail for business reasons, not knowledge reasons. They pass the exam, join a brokerage, and run out of money in month five while waiting for a first closing. The National Association of Realtors has long reported that a large share of new licensees leave the business within two years. The material in this unit is what separates the ones who stay.

**The cash flow problem, stated plainly.** You earn nothing until a transaction closes. A buyer you meet in March may not close until July. Your commission then arrives 30 to 90 days after you started actually working with them, minus splits and fees. So your first real paycheck is commonly **four to six months** after you begin — and it is one paycheck, not a salary.

Plan for that or the business plans you.

## 2. Choosing a Brokerage

Every salesperson works under a **responsible broker** (Principles Unit 1). Your broker is legally accountable for supervising your licensed activity, which means the quality of that supervision is not a soft benefit — it is a liability shield.

### The compensation models

**Traditional split.** You receive a percentage of each commission — commonly 50/50 to 70/30 for new agents — often improving after you hit a **cap** (a cumulative amount paid to the brokerage in a year, after which your split improves substantially). The brokerage supplies office space, brand, training, and supervision.

**100% commission / desk fee.** You keep nearly the entire commission and pay a fixed monthly desk fee plus per-transaction fees. Excellent for high producers, brutal for new agents — you pay the fee whether or not you close anything.

**Hybrid cap models.** A split until you have paid the brokerage a set amount, then 100% for the remainder of the anniversary year. Common at large national and cloud brokerages.

**Virtual/cloud brokerages.** No physical offices; technology and support delivered remotely, usually with a cap model and sometimes revenue-share or equity components. Supervision obligations are identical — a virtual broker still must review your documents and oversee your work.

### Doing the arithmetic honestly

New agents compare splits. Experienced agents compare **net income at a realistic transaction count**. Those produce different answers, and the difference is largest in year one.

Ask every brokerage you interview:

- What is the split, and is there a cap? What resets it?
- Monthly desk or technology fees?
- Per-transaction fees, and are they capped?
- Is E&O insurance included, or charged per transaction?
- Franchise fee off the top?
- Who pays for signs, lockboxes, marketing, and the CRM?
- **Who reviews my first purchase contract?**
- **Who do I call on a Sunday when a deal is falling apart?**

Those last two matter more than the split for your first year, and they are the two questions new agents forget to ask.

### Employee vs. independent contractor

Most salespersons are **independent contractors for tax purposes** — you receive a 1099, no taxes are withheld, and you must make quarterly estimated tax payments. But under the Real Estate Law you are treated as the **employee of your broker for supervision purposes** (Principles Unit 1). Both are simultaneously true.

The practical consequences of 1099 status hit new agents hard:

- **Nothing is withheld.** Set aside roughly 25–35% of every commission for federal and state income tax and self-employment tax. <!-- VERIFY: current self-employment tax rate and thresholds --> Open a separate savings account for it on your first day.
- **No employer benefits.** Health insurance, retirement, and paid time off are yours to arrange.
- **Business expenses are deductible** — mileage, dues, marketing, E&O, education. Track them from day one, because reconstructing a year of mileage in April is misery.

Talk to a CPA before your first closing, not after. This is one of the highest-return hours you will spend.

## 3. Teams

A **team** is a group of licensees operating under one brokerage, typically led by a producing agent who supplies leads, systems, and administrative support in exchange for a larger share of each transaction.

For a new agent, joining a team is often the fastest path to competence: you get leads before you can generate your own, you see transactions early, and someone experienced reviews your work. The trade-off is a smaller share — team splits of 50/50 or less on team-provided leads are normal — and less control over your brand.

**Team rules are statutory, and they are commonly violated.** A team is *not* a brokerage. Under California law a team name used by salespersons must generally include the surname of at least one team member together with a term such as "team," "group," or "associates," and it must not suggest the team is an independent brokerage or that it operates independently of the responsible broker. All team advertising must identify the responsible broker. <!-- VERIFY: current team name statute, required disclosures, and license number display rules -->

The failure mode to avoid: an agent builds a strong personal brand, markets under it, omits the broker's name, and creates an unlicensed-brokerage appearance. That is a disciplinary matter, and it is entirely preventable.

## 4. Your First-Year Business Plan

A business plan for a new agent is not a document for investors. It is four numbers and a calendar.

**Number 1 — What do you need to earn?** Start with your actual living expenses, add taxes, and add business costs. That is your required gross commission income.

**Number 2 — What is your average commission?** Take your market's median sale price, multiply by your realistic side commission rate, and multiply by your split.

**Number 3 — How many transactions is that?** Number 1 divided by Number 2. This is usually the moment reality arrives.

**Number 4 — How many conversations does that take?** Work backward through your funnel: contacts → appointments → signed clients → closings. Early on, assume worse ratios than you hope for.

Then put the resulting daily activity on a calendar and treat it as non-negotiable. New agents do not fail from lack of knowledge; they fail from inconsistent activity.

### The costs new agents forget

| Category | Typical first-year items |
|---|---|
| Licensing | Exam and license fees, Live Scan fingerprinting |
| Association | Local, state, and national dues; MLS fees; lockbox key access |
| Brokerage | Desk fee, technology fee, per-transaction fees, franchise fee |
| Insurance | E&O (if not included), auto business coverage, health insurance |
| Marketing | Signs, riders, photography, CRM, website, printed materials, ads |
| Operating | Vehicle and fuel, phone, computer, professional attire, continuing education |
| Taxes | Quarterly estimated payments |

The single most important line is not on that table: **six to twelve months of living expenses in reserve.** Nothing else on this list will end your career. That one will.

## 5. Errors, Omissions, and Risk From Day One

**E&O insurance** covers claims arising from professional services — a missed disclosure, a mistake in a contract, alleged negligence. Some brokerages include it; others charge per transaction. Know which, know the deductible, and know whether it covers you for property management or referral activity if you plan to do those.

E&O does **not** cover intentional wrongdoing, fraud, or commingling. There is no insurance product for taking client money.

**Risk management habits to build immediately**, before you have anything at stake:

1. **Put it in writing.** Every agreement, every extension, every material conversation confirmed by email the same day.
2. **Never fill in blanks you do not understand.** Ask your broker. Drafting custom legal language is unauthorized practice of law (Principles Unit 1).
3. **Disclose what you observe.** Observe, disclose, refer (Principles Unit 6). You are not an inspector and you are not a lawyer.
4. **Keep the file.** Three years minimum for brokerage transaction records, and your file is the evidence that you did your job.
5. **Standardize your process** and apply it identically to every client. This is your fair housing protection (Unit 3) and your competence system at once.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Comparing two brokerage offers (San Gabriel Valley)

You expect **6 transactions** in year one. Median local price is **$780,000**, and you will earn a **2.5%** side commission, so **gross commission per side = $19,500**.

**Brokerage A — traditional split.** 60/40 in your favor, no desk fee, $395 per transaction, E&O included.
1. Your side: $19,500 × 0.60 = $11,700
2. Less transaction fee: $11,700 − $395 = **$11,305 per deal**
3. Six deals: **$67,830**

**Brokerage B — cap model.** 80/20 until you pay $16,000 to the brokerage, then 100%. Desk fee $85/month. $295 per transaction.
1. Per deal, brokerage share at 20%: $19,500 × 0.20 = $3,900
2. Cap reached after $16,000 ÷ $3,900 = 4.1 deals — so deals 1–4 are split, deal 5 partially, deals 5–6 essentially at 100%
3. Deals 1–4: you keep $19,500 × 0.80 = $15,600 each, less $295 = $15,305 × 4 = **$61,220**
4. Brokerage collected $15,600 of the cap; remaining $400 comes out of deal 5
5. Deals 5–6: roughly $19,500 − $295 = $19,205 each, less the $400 cap remainder on deal 5 → **$38,010**
6. Less desk fees: $85 × 12 = $1,020
7. Total: $61,220 + $38,010 − $1,020 = **$98,210**

**Brokerage B nets about $30,000 more at six transactions.** But run the same math at **two** transactions:
- A: $11,305 × 2 = **$22,610**
- B: $15,305 × 2 − $1,020 = **$29,590**

B still wins here — but if B's desk fee were $500/month instead of $85, B would net $24,610 versus A's $22,610, and the gap nearly closes. **The fee structure matters most when volume is lowest**, which is exactly when new agents are least able to absorb it.

And none of this arithmetic captures the thing that actually decides your first year: whether someone at that brokerage will pick up the phone when your escrow is falling apart.

### Example 2 — Building the plan backward (Inland Empire)

You need to earn **$60,000** in year one. Median price in your farm area is **$540,000**; you expect a **2.5%** side and a **70/30** split.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Gross commission per side: $540,000 × 0.025 = **$13,500**
2. Your share: $13,500 × 0.70 = **$9,450**
3. Transactions needed: $60,000 ÷ $9,450 = 6.35 → **7 transactions**
4. If your conversion is roughly 1 closing per 3 signed clients' worth of effort, and 1 signed client per 10 serious conversations, you need roughly **210 serious conversations** across the year — about **4 per week**.
5. Remember taxes: $60,000 gross is not $60,000 spendable. At a 28% effective rate, budget roughly **$43,200** to live on, and pay quarterly.

The value of this exercise is that "4 real conversations a week" is a manageable, schedulable thing. "Earn $60,000" is not.

### Example 3 — The team name problem (Pasadena)

A new agent joins a two-person team and wants to market as **"Elevate Realty Group."** Nice brand, but:

- The name includes no team member's **surname**.
- "Realty" suggests an independent **brokerage**.
- The proposed sign shows only the team name and phone number.

**Analysis.** This is likely non-compliant on all three counts and would also produce a **blind ad** (Principles Unit 1) by concealing that the advertiser is a licensee acting under a broker. A compliant alternative: **"The Rabadi Group — Agency 8 Real Estate Group,"** with the responsible broker identified and license numbers displayed as required.

The practical lesson: choose the team name *before* you print anything, and run it past your broker and current statute. Signs, business cards, and a website are expensive to redo.

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Responsible broker** | The broker legally accountable for supervising a salesperson's licensed activity. |
| **Commission split** | The agreed division of a commission between broker and salesperson. |
| **Cap** | A cumulative amount paid to the brokerage in a year, after which the salesperson's split improves, often to 100%. |
| **Desk fee** | A fixed monthly charge in a 100%-commission or hybrid model, owed whether or not the agent closes anything. |
| **Franchise fee** | A percentage taken off the top of a commission by a national brand before the broker/agent split. |
| **Independent contractor** | The salesperson's usual tax status: 1099 income, no withholding, quarterly estimated taxes, deductible business expenses. |
| **Team** | A group of licensees under one brokerage, subject to statutory naming and advertising rules; never an independent brokerage. |
| **E&O insurance** | Errors and omissions coverage for claims arising from professional services; excludes fraud and trust fund misappropriation. |
| **Gross commission income (GCI)** | Total commission earned before splits, fees, and expenses. |
| **Conversion ratio** | The rate at which contacts become appointments, appointments become clients, and clients become closings. |

## 8. Practice Tips

- **Interview at least three brokerages**, and ask the supervision questions, not just the split.
- **Model your net at a realistic transaction count**, not at the count you hope for.
- **Open the tax savings account before your first closing.** Move 25–35% of every check into it the day it lands.
- **Get the team name approved before you print anything.**
- **Reserve six to twelve months of living expenses.** This is the single largest predictor of whether you are still licensed in year three.
- **Track expenses and mileage from day one**, not from April.
