# Unit 9 — Contract-to-Close

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Build** a transaction timeline from the acceptance date and calendar every deadline.
2. **Manage** contingency removal under California's active removal regime.
3. **Apply** the notice to perform and cancellation procedures correctly.
4. **Coordinate** escrow, lender, title, and inspection workflows.
5. **Negotiate** repair requests without converting an as-is sale into a warranty.
6. **Diagnose** the common causes of failed escrows and intervene early.
7. **Calculate** deadline dates and understand what "time is of the essence" makes material.

---

## 1. Day One: Build the Timeline

The single highest-value hour in any transaction is the one immediately after acceptance, spent building the calendar.

**From the acceptance date, calendar:**

| Item | Typical timing |
|---|---|
| Deposit into escrow | Within 3 business days of acceptance |
| Escrow opened, instructions out | Days 1–3 |
| Seller disclosures delivered | Immediately (ideally pre-offer) |
| Preliminary report ordered and delivered | Days 1–7 |
| Inspections scheduled | Days 1–5 |
| Investigation contingency | Commonly 17 days |
| Appraisal and loan contingencies | Commonly 21 days |
| Loan documents to escrow | Days 21–28 |
| Final walk-through | Days before closing |
| Signing, funding, recording | Final days |

Those contingency periods are **contractual, not statutory** — read the actual contract every time. <!-- VERIFY: current standard-form default periods -->

**Counting convention:** day one is generally the day *after* acceptance. Confirm against the form you are using, and when a deadline is ambiguous, resolve it in writing with the other agent rather than assuming.

**Because "time is of the essence" applies, every one of those dates is material.** A missed date is a breach, not a delay.

## 2. Contingency Removal: Active, Not Automatic

California residential practice uses **active removal** (Principles Unit 8). This is the single most misunderstood mechanic in the transaction, and getting it wrong costs deals.

**What it means:**

- Contingencies remain in effect **until the buyer removes them in writing**.
- A deadline passing does **not** automatically remove a contingency and does **not** automatically permit the seller to cancel.
- When a deadline passes without removal, the seller's remedy is to deliver a **Notice to Buyer to Perform**, which starts a cure period. Only after that period runs may the seller cancel.

**Notice to Perform mechanics:**
- Must be in writing and properly delivered.
- Gives the buyer a stated period to act — commonly two days, but read the form. <!-- VERIFY: current notice period on the standard form -->
- May generally not be delivered earlier than a stated number of days before the underlying deadline.

**The two failure modes:**

1. **The seller's agent who cancels on the deadline.** No notice, no cure period, unilateral cancellation. This is a wrongful cancellation and the seller may be in breach.
2. **The buyer's agent who assumes a passed deadline is harmless.** It is not — it hands the seller the right to start the notice clock and creates leverage the buyer did not intend to give.

**Cancellation requires mutual written instructions** to release the deposit (Principles Unit 10). Escrow does not decide who is right. When both sides claim the deposit, escrow holds it until they agree or a court orders release — which is why "you'll get your deposit back" is a sentence you should never say casually.

## 3. Inspections and Repair Requests

**Schedule inspections immediately.** The investigation period is short and inspectors book out. A buyer's agent who schedules on day 12 of a 17-day period has created an avoidable crisis.

**Common inspections:** general home inspection, termite/wood-destroying organism, roof, sewer lateral (increasingly essential on older properties), chimney, pool/spa, foundation or structural where indicated, and specialized environmental where the property suggests it.

**The repair request conversation.** After inspections, buyers typically ask for repairs or credits. Practical guidance:

- **Prioritize by materiality**, not by item count. Health and safety, structural, systems, and anything affecting insurability or financing come first. A list of 40 cosmetic items reads as unserious and hardens the seller.
- **Credits often work better than repairs.** The seller avoids managing contractors, and the buyer controls the quality of the work. Note that lenders limit how much credit may be applied. <!-- VERIFY: current seller-credit limits by loan type -->
- **"As-is" does not mean no negotiation.** It means the seller has not agreed in advance to repair. The buyer may still request, and the seller may still say no — and the buyer may still cancel within their contingency (Principles Unit 9).
- **Do not turn the seller into a warrantor.** Careless language in a repair addendum can create ongoing obligations after closing. Keep it specific: what will be done, by whom, by when, and with what evidence of completion.

**Disclosure obligation runs both ways.** If a buyer's inspection reveals a material defect and the transaction later falls through, the **seller now knows** about it and must disclose it to subsequent buyers. Sellers are frequently surprised by this. Tell them before inspections, not after.

## 4. Coordinating the Parties

You are, functionally, the project manager.

**Escrow** needs: the fully executed contract and all addenda, the deposit, the parties' contact and vesting information, payoff demands, and the parties' signed instructions. Escrow acts only on **mutual written instructions** and cannot advise either party (Principles Unit 10).

**The lender** needs: the contract, appraisal access, and the buyer's documents — and needs them promptly. The most common cause of a delayed closing is a buyer who takes nine days to send a bank statement.

**Title** needs: the preliminary report reviewed and any curative items started early. A name-variance judgment lien takes days to clear if you start on day 3 and kills the closing if you start on day 25.

**Weekly communication** is the standard your clients actually judge you on. Even "nothing changed this week, here's where we are" prevents the anxious call.

## 5. Why Escrows Fail — and Early Warning Signs

| Cause | Early warning | Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Loan denial | Buyer slow on documents; changed jobs; new debt | Verify underwriting status weekly; tell buyers not to open credit or change jobs |
| Low appraisal | Aggressive price with thin comparables | Prepare the seller at the offer stage; assemble comps for the appraiser (facts only) |
| Inspection shock | No pre-offer walkthrough; older property | Set condition expectations early; schedule inspections day one |
| Title defect | Prelim not read; name variance; unreleased lien | Read the prelim day one; start curative work immediately |
| Insurance unavailable | Property in a fire hazard severity zone | Have buyers quote insurance in the first days, not the last |
| Buyer's home not selling | Contingent offer | Monitor the dependent transaction weekly |
| Cold feet | Communication goes quiet | Call, do not text; find out what changed |

**The pattern:** almost every escrow that dies gave a signal a week or more before. Agents who check in weekly catch them; agents who go quiet until the closing date do not.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Building the calendar (Rancho Cucamonga)

Offer accepted **Tuesday, September 8**. Investigation 17 days, loan and appraisal 21 days, close of escrow 30 days.

**Walkthrough** (day one = the day after acceptance, September 9):
1. Deposit to escrow: within **3 business days** → by **Friday, September 11**
2. Investigation contingency: September 9 + 16 = **Friday, September 25**
3. Loan and appraisal: September 9 + 20 = **Tuesday, September 29**
4. Close of escrow: September 9 + 29 = **Thursday, October 8**
5. Final walk-through: schedule **October 5–7**

**Backward-planning from those dates:**
- Inspections must be **completed and reviewed** by ~September 22 to leave room to negotiate repairs before the 25th.
- So inspections get scheduled **September 9–11**, not the week of the 21st.
- The appraisal must be ordered immediately to return before the 29th.

That backward pass is the actual work. The deadline dates are arithmetic; the schedule that makes them achievable is judgment.

### Example 2 — The deadline passed. Now what? (Ontario)

It is September 26. The buyer has not delivered a written investigation contingency removal. The listing agent tells the seller they can cancel and take the backup offer.

**Analysis.** The listing agent is **wrong**, and acting on that advice would put the seller in breach.

Under active removal:
1. The contingency **remains in effect** — it did not expire on its own.
2. The seller may deliver a **Notice to Buyer to Perform**, which starts a short cure period.
3. Only if the buyer fails to remove within that period may the seller cancel.
4. Cancellation then requires **mutual written instructions** to release the deposit.

**What the buyer's agent should do the moment the deadline passes:** contact the listing agent, explain the status, and either deliver the removal or request a written extension. Silence is what turns an administrative slip into a cancellation.

### Example 3 — The repair request that hardened the seller (Covina)

Inspection reveals: a failing water heater (~$1,800), an active roof leak (~$4,500), a cracked heat exchanger in the furnace (safety, ~$5,200), and 31 cosmetic items including loose outlet covers and a sticking closet door. The buyer's agent submits all 34 items.

**Analysis.** The three material items total **$11,500** and are genuinely negotiable — one is a safety issue and two affect systems. Burying them in 34 items invites the seller to reject the whole list as unreasonable, and that is usually what happens.

**Better approach:** request the three material items — ideally as a **credit** of roughly $11,500 rather than repairs, so the buyer controls contractor quality — and explicitly waive the cosmetic list in writing. The waiver is itself a negotiating asset: it signals seriousness and makes the remaining request hard to dismiss.

### Example 4 — The disclosure that follows a failed escrow (Whittier)

A buyer's inspection reveals a cracked sewer lateral. The buyer cancels within their investigation contingency. The seller relists.

**Analysis.** The seller now has **actual knowledge** of a material defect and **must disclose it** to every subsequent buyer (Principles Unit 9). The inspection report does not vanish with the buyer.

Sellers routinely believe a cancelled escrow resets everything. It does not. The professional move is to tell the seller this **before** inspections happen, so they can decide whether to repair proactively — and so they are not blindsided by a disclosure obligation they did not anticipate.

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Active removal** | The regime under which contingencies remain until removed in writing; deadlines passing do not remove them automatically. |
| **Notice to Buyer to Perform** | The written notice a seller delivers when a buyer misses a deadline, starting a cure period before cancellation is permitted. |
| **Notice to Seller to Perform** | The buyer's corresponding notice when the seller fails to perform a contractual obligation. |
| **Mutual cancellation instructions** | The written agreement of both parties required for escrow to release a deposit. |
| **Time is of the essence** | The clause making each stated deadline material, so late performance is a breach. |
| **Repair request / request for repairs** | The buyer's post-inspection request for repairs or credits; not a right to renegotiate at will. |
| **Seller credit** | Money credited at closing in lieu of repairs; subject to lender limits. |
| **Sewer lateral inspection** | Inspection of the line from the house to the main; increasingly essential on older California properties. |
| **Final walk-through** | The buyer's pre-closing verification of condition and agreed repairs; not a renegotiation opportunity. |

## 8. Practice Tips

- **Build the calendar the day of acceptance**, then plan backward so deadlines are achievable rather than merely known.
- **Schedule inspections in the first three days.** Inspectors book out; the period is short.
- **A passed deadline does not cancel anything.** Deliver a Notice to Perform, run the cure period, then cancel — in that order.
- **Never tell a client their deposit is automatically refundable.** Release requires mutual written instructions.
- **Request the material items, waive the cosmetic ones in writing.** A focused request is a stronger request.
- **Warn sellers before inspections** that discovered defects must be disclosed to future buyers if the escrow fails.
- **Read the preliminary report on day one** and start curative work immediately.
- **Communicate weekly even when nothing has changed.** Silence is how escrows die unnoticed.
