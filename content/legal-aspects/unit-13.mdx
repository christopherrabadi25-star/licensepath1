# Unit 13 — Common Interest Developments & HOA Law

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Identify** the four types of common interest developments recognized under California's Davis-Stirling Act.
2. **Explain** the governing document hierarchy and which documents control when they conflict.
3. **Apply** the disclosure requirements specific to selling a CID unit.
4. **Analyze** an HOA assessment and lien scenario, including the association's enforcement rights.
5. **Distinguish** an association's rule-making authority from what CC&Rs require to change.
6. **Explain** the board meeting, records-access, and election-transparency requirements owners rely on.
7. **Advise** (within license scope) on why HOA financial health matters to a buyer beyond the monthly dues figure.

## 1. What Is a Common Interest Development?

California's **Davis-Stirling Common Interest Development Act** governs four recognized types of common interest developments (CIDs):

- **Condominiums** — separate ownership of individual airspace units combined with an undivided common interest in shared common areas.
- **Planned developments** — separately owned lots (often single-family-style homes) combined with common areas maintained by an association, common in many newer SoCal subdivisions.
- **Stock cooperatives** — ownership structured through shares in a corporation that owns the property, with a right to occupy a specific unit.
- **Community apartment projects** — undivided interests in the entire property combined with the exclusive right to occupy a specific unit.

Nearly all newer subdivided residential development in California — condos, townhomes, and a large share of single-family planned communities — is now built as some form of CID, making Davis-Stirling one of the most practically important bodies of law for a working California agent, even though it rarely appears as its own topic in general real estate conversation.

## 2. The Governing Document Hierarchy

CIDs are governed by a layered set of documents, and understanding the hierarchy matters when documents appear to conflict:

1. **Declaration of Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions (CC&Rs)** — the foundational recorded document establishing the CID, defining ownership interests, use restrictions, and the association's basic authority; generally the highest-authority governing document, subordinate only to statute.
2. **Articles of Incorporation** — establish the association as a legal (usually nonprofit mutual benefit) corporation.
3. **Bylaws** — govern the association's internal operations (board elections, meetings, officer roles).
4. **Rules and Regulations** — operational rules adopted by the board under authority granted by the CC&Rs and bylaws (e.g., pool hours, parking rules, architectural review procedures).

**A board's rules and regulations cannot conflict with the CC&Rs or bylaws** — if a board adopts a rule inconsistent with the recorded CC&Rs, the CC&Rs generally control, and owners have recognized avenues to challenge an inconsistent rule. This distinction matters practically: a rule the board adopted last month is far easier to change (and far weaker as a restriction) than a use restriction actually written into the recorded CC&Rs, which generally requires a supermajority owner vote to amend.

## 3. CID-Specific Disclosure Requirements

Selling a unit in a CID triggers disclosure obligations beyond the standard TDS/NHD framework (Principles Unit 9):

- **Governing documents** — the seller must provide the buyer copies of the CC&Rs, bylaws, and current rules.
- **Financial disclosures** — including the association's current operating budget, reserve study/reserve funding status, and assessment history.
- **Pending litigation** — the association's disclosure of pending or anticipated legal claims involving the association is required, since litigation can create special assessment risk for every owner regardless of whether their specific unit is the subject of the dispute.
- **Assessment and lien status** — whether the specific unit being sold has any unpaid assessments or an existing lien.

### Worked Example 1 — The reserve study red flag (Chino Hills)

A buyer's agent reviewing HOA disclosures for a 30-year-old condo complex finds the reserve study shows the roofing and plumbing reserve funded at only 15% of the recommended level, with no special assessment yet approved.

**Analysis.** A low-funded reserve is a genuine financial red flag, not a minor technicality — it substantially increases the likelihood of a **special assessment** in the near future to cover a major repair the regular dues were never sized to fund. This directly affects the buyer's true cost of ownership beyond the advertised monthly dues, and it is exactly the kind of detail a buyer's agent should flag and explain clearly, even though the agent cannot predict whether or when a special assessment will actually be imposed.

## 4. Assessments and the Association's Lien Rights

Regular assessments (routine dues) and special assessments (one-time charges for specific needs, such as a major repair) are the association's primary funding mechanism. An association generally has significant statutory authority to enforce unpaid assessments:

- The association can **record a lien** against the delinquent owner's unit for unpaid assessments, following specific statutory notice requirements before doing so.
- In cases of continued nonpayment, the association can, under specified statutory conditions and procedural safeguards, ultimately pursue **foreclosure of the assessment lien** — meaning a homeowner can, in a worst-case scenario, lose their home over unpaid HOA dues, not just an unpaid mortgage. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory minimum delinquency amount/duration and required pre-lien notice/dispute resolution procedures before an association may pursue foreclosure -->

**This surprises many buyers and even some agents:** the mortgage is not the only lien that can lead to foreclosure on a CID property. Because of the seriousness of this remedy, Davis-Stirling imposes procedural protections — required notices, internal dispute resolution offers, and payment plan requirements — before an association can proceed to lien enforcement or foreclosure. <!-- VERIFY: current specific procedural prerequisites -->

## 5. Board Governance, Meetings, and Records Access

Davis-Stirling establishes owner protections around association governance:

- **Open meetings** — most board business must generally be conducted in meetings open to members, with specific exceptions for legitimate executive session topics (litigation, personnel, or specific member discipline matters).
- **Records access** — owners generally have a statutory right to inspect specified association records, including financial records, subject to reasonable procedures.
- **Election procedures** — board elections and certain other significant votes (such as CC&R amendments) must follow specific procedural protections, including secret ballot requirements for contested matters. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory election procedure requirements, an area with recent legislative attention -->

These protections exist because an HOA board wields real regulatory-style authority over owners' homes and finances, and Davis-Stirling's transparency requirements are the primary check on that authority short of litigation.

## 6. Architectural Review and Use Restrictions

CC&Rs commonly require **architectural review committee (ARC) approval** before an owner makes exterior modifications — anything from a fence to a solar panel installation to a paint color change. Notably, California law has specifically protected certain categories (such as solar energy systems and certain drought-tolerant landscaping) from being unreasonably restricted by CC&Rs, reflecting state policy priorities that override otherwise-broad association discretion in those specific areas. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory protections for solar installations, drought-tolerant landscaping, and any other legislatively protected categories, and their exact limits on association discretion -->

### Worked Example 2 — The solar panel denial (Fontana)

A homeowner in a planned development submits an ARC application for roof-mounted solar panels. The board denies the application, citing a CC&R provision requiring uniform roof appearance.

**Analysis.** Depending on the specific facts, this denial may conflict with California's statutory protections for solar energy systems, which generally limit an association's ability to prohibit or unreasonably restrict solar installations even where CC&Rs contain aesthetic uniformity requirements — associations typically may impose only reasonable, cost-effective restrictions, not an outright ban. This is exactly the kind of dispute where an agent should recognize that "the CC&Rs say no" is not automatically the end of the analysis, and refer the homeowner to counsel familiar with this specific statutory protection rather than treating the board's denial as final.

## 7. The Licensee's Role With CID Properties

Listing or representing a buyer for a CID property requires the agent to obtain and review governing documents, financial disclosures, and litigation history early — not treat the HOA package as paperwork to hand over unread at the end of the transaction. Buyers rely heavily on their agent to flag red flags (underfunded reserves, pending litigation, unusually high delinquency rates) in plain language, even though interpreting the ultimate legal or financial significance of those red flags is not the agent's job to finalize.

## 8. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Davis-Stirling Common Interest Development Act** | California's statutory framework governing condominiums, planned developments, stock cooperatives, and community apartment projects. |
| **CC&Rs** | The foundational recorded declaration establishing a CID's ownership structure, use restrictions, and association authority. |
| **Reserve study** | An assessment of an association's long-term capital repair funding needs and current funding level. |
| **Special assessment** | A one-time charge to owners for a specific need beyond regular dues. |
| **Assessment lien** | A lien an association may record against a unit for unpaid assessments, potentially leading to foreclosure. |
| **Architectural review committee (ARC)** | The association body reviewing and approving exterior modification requests. |

## 9. Exam and Practice Tips

- Four CID types: condominiums, planned developments, stock cooperatives, community apartment projects.
- CC&Rs generally outrank board-adopted rules and regulations; a rule inconsistent with the CC&Rs is vulnerable to challenge.
- An underfunded reserve study is a real financial red flag pointing toward likely future special assessments.
- HOA assessment liens can lead to foreclosure — the mortgage is not the only lien with that consequence on a CID property.
- Solar energy systems and certain other categories have specific statutory protection against unreasonable CC&R restriction.
- Selling a CID unit requires governing document, financial, litigation, and lien-status disclosures beyond the standard TDS/NHD package.
