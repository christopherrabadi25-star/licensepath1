# Unit 14 — Property Management & Leasing Basics

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

1. **Describe** the property manager’s agency relationship and fiduciary responsibilities.
2. **Use** a lawful, consistent leasing workflow from inquiry through move-in.
3. **Recognize** fair-housing, privacy, habitability, security-deposit, and notice issues that require care.
4. **Explain** the difference between documenting a condition and diagnosing or giving legal advice.
5. **Track** rent, maintenance, communications, and owner approvals in an auditable file.
6. **Respond** to urgent maintenance and safety reports with appropriate escalation.
7. **Distinguish** a business decision from an unlawful or unauthorized instruction.

---

## 1. Property Management Is Ongoing Agency

Sales transactions have a beginning and a close. Property management is different: the manager makes repeated decisions involving an owner’s asset, a resident’s home, and money belonging to others. That makes routine discipline essential. A missed maintenance message, casual screening comment, undocumented key handoff, or delayed rent record can become a serious dispute because the relationship continues after the day’s task is done.

The property manager normally acts as an agent for the owner under a management agreement. Read the agreement and brokerage policies before taking action. They define the scope of authority, fees, maintenance approval limits, rent handling, reporting, vendor rules, and communication expectations. The manager owes the owner loyalty, reasonable care, accounting, confidentiality, and obedience to lawful instructions. “Lawful” matters. A manager cannot carry out an owner’s discriminatory request, conceal a material condition, misuse funds, or serve a notice outside authorized process merely because the owner demands it.

The resident is not automatically the manager’s client, but the manager must still deal honestly, comply with applicable housing rules, protect privacy, and avoid misrepresentation. Good management is not choosing one party over the other; it is applying a clear process consistently and escalating conflicts that exceed the manager’s authority.

### Worked Example 1 — The owner’s screening instruction

An owner says, “I do not want families with young children upstairs; they are too noisy.” A manager must not translate that into a screening rule or a listing phrase. Familial-status protections and other fair-housing rules make this a serious concern. The manager should tell the owner that the request cannot be implemented, notify the responsible broker, document the conversation professionally, and continue using lawful, consistently applied rental criteria.

## 2. Build a Consistent Leasing Workflow

Consistency is both operationally smart and a fair-housing protection. The process should not become more forgiving for a favored prospect or more demanding for someone the owner dislikes. Use approved, written criteria and apply them in the same order to every applicant, subject to any legally required individualized process and reasonable-accommodation obligations.

A basic workflow is:

1. market the property accurately with neutral, property-focused language;
2. provide the same available information and application path to prospects;
3. collect only approved information through secure channels;
4. evaluate applications under written criteria and required rules;
5. communicate a factual decision using approved notices and procedures;
6. complete the lease, funds, move-in inspection, and key/access process;
7. create a complete resident and property file.

Do not ask questions about protected characteristics, medical diagnoses, nationality, religion, family plans, or other information that is not needed for a lawful housing decision. A request for a reasonable accommodation or modification should be routed through the brokerage’s approved procedure—not dismissed because it seems inconvenient.

### Marketing language: describe the home, not the preferred resident

“Two-bedroom unit with accessible route from parking, subject to verification” describes a feature. “Perfect for able-bodied singles” describes people and is inappropriate. “Near public transit” is a location fact. “Christian neighborhood” or “safe for women” is not neutral property marketing. When in doubt, describe size, layout, amenities, verified location facts, price, availability, and objective rules.

## 3. Applications, Screening, and Privacy

Applications contain sensitive information: identification, income, employment, credit data, prior addresses, and sometimes explanations of disability-related needs. Collect no more than your approved process requires; secure it; limit access to people who need it; and retain or dispose of it under brokerage policy and applicable law. Do not discuss applicants in a group chat or leave paper applications visible during showings.

Screening criteria must be real, written, and consistently used. A manager should never invent a new threshold after seeing an applicant’s name, photo, accent, family composition, or request for an accommodation. If the owner wants an exception, do not casually grant or deny it; find out whether the exception is permitted under the agreement, consistent with the established criteria, and lawful. Escalate to the broker when the answer is not clear.

**Worked Example 2 — The incomplete application.** A prospect who uses a wheelchair has provided all required income documents but accidentally omits one reference. Another applicant without a disability omits the same item. The manager should apply the same approved follow-up process to both. The manager should not add a new burden because the prospect asked whether a ramp could be installed. The accommodation/modification request must be handled through the designated process, separately and fairly.

## 4. Maintenance, Habitability, and the “Do Not Diagnose” Rule

Property management requires prompt attention to maintenance messages because a small condition can affect safety, habitability, property value, and the owner-resident relationship. The manager should create a timestamped work order, classify urgency under the brokerage’s approved system, communicate what is known, arrange authorized service, and document the outcome. Emergency conditions—such as active flooding, fire, suspected gas leak, exposed electrical hazard, or a situation threatening immediate harm—require immediate use of the emergency protocol and, when appropriate, emergency services.

The manager’s job is coordination and documentation, not diagnosis. Say, “The resident reported a strong gas odor; emergency instructions were provided and a qualified vendor was contacted.” Do not say, “It is probably harmless,” “The tenant caused it,” or “The property is legally uninhabitable.” Those conclusions may be inaccurate and may exceed your role.

Habitability, entry, repair timing, rent remedies, and notice obligations can be controlled by changing law and local ordinance. Treat exact rules as a **VERIFY** item before using them in a client file or school final. Follow the responsible broker’s current written procedure and obtain legal guidance where necessary.

### Worked Example 3 — The weekend leak

At 8:15 p.m. Saturday, a resident reports water flowing from a ceiling light fixture. The manager should not wait for Monday or debate whether the resident caused it. Follow emergency protocol: tell the resident to move away from the hazard and contact emergency services if danger is immediate, contact the approved emergency vendor and owner/broker according to policy, document every contact, and preserve invoices and photographs. After the immediate danger is controlled, determine next steps through the authorized process.

## 5. Rent, Deposits, and Owner Accounting

Money handling in property management belongs to the same trust-fund discipline studied in Unit 12. Rent, security deposits, owner reserves, and vendor payments must be handled through approved accounts and records. A manager should not accept rent in a personal account, loan resident money to an owner, use one property’s funds to cover another property, or make an undocumented cash adjustment.

The management file should connect the lease, rent ledger, payment receipt, late communication, owner statement, maintenance invoice, authorization, and disbursement record. The owner needs accurate reporting; the resident needs an accurate account of what was paid and what is claimed; the broker needs records that can be reconciled. The exact treatment of deposits and deductions is legally sensitive. Never promise a resident that a deposit will be returned in full or tell an owner they may keep it as a penalty without current authorized guidance.

## 6. Entry, Privacy, and Boundaries

A resident’s home is not a staging area, even when an owner wants to sell or repair it. Enter only under lawful, authorized procedures and documented notice or emergency conditions. Do not give vendors a key without an approved work order and access process. Do not photograph personal items for marketing, share security codes in casual text threads, or disclose a resident’s location, financial situation, or complaints to people who do not need the information.

**Worked Example 4 — The showing request.** An owner decides to list an occupied condominium and asks the manager to “just pop in with buyers whenever they are nearby.” The manager should decline the informal approach, explain that entry and showings require the broker’s approved notice/access process, establish a documented schedule, and protect the resident’s privacy. Owner convenience is not a reason to disregard lawful access requirements.

## 7. Notices, Disputes, and the Escalation Line

Notices can affect a resident’s home and legal rights. A manager may use broker-approved forms and procedures within authority, but should never draft bespoke legal language, interpret disputed lease clauses, threaten eviction, or make a promise about a court outcome. Preserve the underlying facts: dates, payments, photos, work orders, communications, and signed documents. Then escalate the legal question to the broker and appropriate counsel.

Similarly, do not describe every resident complaint as “retaliation,” “discrimination,” “harassment,” or “breach” unless an authorized professional has advised that conclusion. Take the report seriously, document it neutrally, prevent immediate harm where possible, and route it promptly. The manager’s record may later become crucial; its credibility depends on being factual rather than argumentative.

## 8. Turnover, Vendors, and Owner Reporting

Turnover begins before the old resident is gone. The manager should understand the management agreement, lease, approved inspection procedure, marketing plan, owner budget, and access rules before scheduling vendors or making promises to a future resident. A rushed turnover is not an excuse to skip condition documentation. Photograph and describe observable conditions using the brokerage’s approved process, preserve invoices and estimates, and obtain owner approval where required by the agreement.

Vendor coordination requires the same boundaries as other management work. A vendor can describe the scope of their work and provide an invoice. The manager should not alter a professional recommendation to make it fit a preferred budget, promise that a repair solves an unverified underlying condition, or accept an undisclosed benefit for selecting a vendor. Follow brokerage policy concerning referrals, licensing, insurance, bids, approvals, and emergency authority. The owner deserves candid information about cost, timing, and known options; the resident deserves a safe, professionally handled repair process.

### Worked Example 5 — The low bid with no license information

An owner tells the manager to hire a cousin who offers to repair damaged stairs for cash, even though the vendor will not provide license or insurance information. The manager should not treat a family connection as due diligence. Check the management agreement and broker policy, report the missing information, explain the operational risk factually, and obtain authorized direction. If the repair involves a safety issue, escalate urgency rather than allowing a price discussion to delay protective action.

Owner reporting should make the business understandable. A useful monthly report identifies rent received, outstanding balances, approved expenses, vendor invoices, reserve balance, notable maintenance issues, lease events, and matters requiring owner direction. Do not hide bad news until the statement is due. If a resident reports an urgent condition, notify the owner according to the emergency authority in the agreement while acting within the approved response plan.

Keep the manager’s communication factual and nonjudgmental. “Resident reported no heat at 7:40 a.m.; vendor appointment is scheduled for 10:00 a.m.; estimated cost will follow inspection” is useful. “Resident is always difficult and probably caused it” is an opinion that may damage the file and distract from the task. A strong record tells a future reviewer what happened without asking them to trust the writer’s conclusion.

## 9. Move-In and Move-Out: Build the Record at Both Ends

Move-in documentation creates the baseline for later condition questions. Use the approved inspection form, capture facts consistently, explain how maintenance requests are reported, provide permitted safety and emergency information, and account for every key, remote, parking credential, and access code. Do not make a resident sign an incomplete or inaccurate inspection form simply to finish a busy day. If the resident notes a condition, preserve the note and follow up under policy.

At move-out, use the same neutral approach. Document the observable condition, preserve dates and communications, obtain vendor information where appropriate, and route any proposed deposit accounting or dispute through the broker’s current procedure. Do not characterize ordinary wear, damage, cleaning, or a deduction as a legal conclusion. The final treatment may depend on facts, the lease, current law, notices, invoices, and authorized review.

### Worked Example 6 — The missing remote

At move-out, the manager finds that one garage remote is missing. The right record is not “tenant stole remote.” Record that one remote issued at move-in was not returned, preserve the check-in/check-out documentation, determine replacement cost through the approved process, and send the matter for authorized review. Facts first; conclusions only after the responsible process supports them.

## 10. A Practical Daily Operating System

At the start of each day, review urgent work orders, newly received rent, pending applications, upcoming access appointments, expiring leases, owner approvals, and unresolved resident communications. At the end of the day, make sure material messages and records are stored in the correct file—not solely in an inbox. This modest routine prevents a surprising number of failures: a missed emergency report, a forgotten application, a vendor without authorization, or an undocumented owner instruction.

Before taking action, ask four questions: Do I have authority? Is the action lawful and consistent? What must be documented? Who must be informed? If one of those answers is uncertain, the right move is to pause and escalate. Professional property management is not fast improvisation. It is reliable, fair, documented service over time.

## 11. Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Property management agreement** | Contract defining the manager’s authority and responsibilities for an owner. |
| **Reasonable accommodation** | A change in rule, policy, practice, or service that may be required for a person with a disability. |
| **Reasonable modification** | A structural change that may be requested to permit a person with a disability full use and enjoyment of housing. |
| **Habitability** | Legal standard concerning whether a dwelling meets required living and safety conditions. |
| **Work order** | Record of a reported condition, action requested, vendor work, and outcome. |
| **Rent ledger** | Running record of rent charges, payments, credits, and balances. |
| **Security deposit** | Funds held subject to applicable rules and authorized deductions. |
| **Neutral criteria** | Written, consistently applied non-discriminatory standards. |

## 12. Exam and Practice Tips

- A manager follows lawful owner instructions; an unlawful instruction must be refused and escalated.
- Consistent criteria and consistent process matter as much as the final decision.
- Property features are safe marketing subjects; preferred residents are not.
- Urgent safety reports require the emergency protocol, not a diagnosis.
- Trust funds require approved accounts, receipts, and records.
- Exact notice, entry, habitability, and deposit rules must be verified against current authority.
- Document facts and route legal disputes to qualified professionals.
