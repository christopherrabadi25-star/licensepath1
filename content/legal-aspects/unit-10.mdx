# Unit 10 — Fair Housing & Civil Rights Law

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Compare** the federal Fair Housing Act's protected classes with California's broader protections under the Unruh Act and FEHA.
2. **Distinguish** disparate treatment from disparate impact discrimination theories.
3. **Apply** the legal definitions of steering, redlining, and blockbuking to real transaction facts.
4. **Explain** reasonable accommodation and reasonable modification obligations for disability.
5. **Identify** the enforcement bodies and remedies available under federal and California fair housing law.
6. **Analyze** advertising language for fair housing compliance risk.
7. **Recognize** the limits of familiarity as a defense — "I didn't mean it that way" is not a legal standard.

## 1. Two Overlapping Legal Systems

Real estate fair housing law in California operates on two tracks simultaneously: the **federal Fair Housing Act (FHA)**, and California's own, broader protections under the **Unruh Civil Rights Act** and the **Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA)**. Where the two conflict or one is broader, **the more protective standard generally controls** in California — meaning California's expanded protected class list matters even when federal law would not reach a particular fact pattern.

**Federal FHA protected classes:** race, color, national origin, religion, sex (including sexual orientation and gender identity under current federal interpretation), familial status, and disability. <!-- VERIFY: confirm current federal regulatory/enforcement position on sexual orientation and gender identity coverage, an area with evolving federal guidance -->

**California's protected classes are broader**, additionally covering (among others) marital status, source of income (including housing vouchers), age, ancestry, genetic information, military/veteran status, citizenship/immigration status, and primary language. <!-- VERIFY: current complete FEHA/Unruh protected class list, which has been amended multiple times -->

**Source-of-income protection deserves special attention**: a landlord in California generally cannot refuse to rent to a qualified applicant *because* they intend to pay with a Section 8 voucher or other lawful income source — treating voucher income differently from a paycheck is discrimination under California law, even though it is not necessarily protected the same way federally in every jurisdiction.

## 2. Disparate Treatment vs. Disparate Impact

**Disparate treatment** is intentional discrimination — treating someone differently *because of* a protected characteristic. It is the more intuitive theory: refusing to show a property to a family with children, quoting a higher rent to a prospective tenant because of their national origin, or refusing to negotiate with a buyer because of religion.

**Disparate impact** does not require intent. It applies when a facially neutral policy or practice — one that does not mention any protected class — has a *statistically disproportionate discriminatory effect* on a protected group, and the policy is not justified by a legitimate business necessity that cannot be achieved through a less discriminatory alternative. A blanket "no criminal history of any kind, ever" rental policy, for example, can raise a disparate impact concern because criminal history statistics correlate with race in ways that are well documented — even though the policy never mentions race. <!-- VERIFY: current HUD guidance on criminal history screening policies and disparate impact analysis, an actively litigated and shifting area -->

**Why this distinction matters to agents:** a well-meaning, "neutral" screening or marketing practice can still violate fair housing law if its *effect* falls disproportionately on a protected group — good intent is a defense to disparate treatment, but it is not a defense to disparate impact.

### Worked Example 1 — A neutral-sounding policy with a real effect (Rancho Cucamonga)

A property manager instructs agents to only show a listing to applicants with a minimum credit score of 750 — well above what is needed to reasonably ensure the tenant can pay rent — with no consideration of alternative proof of ability to pay.

**Analysis.** Even though the policy never mentions a protected class, credit-score requirements set well above genuine business necessity can produce a statistically disparate effect on protected groups, given documented disparities in credit access. This does not mean *no* credit screening is permissible — it means the screening criteria should be tied to a genuine, defensible business necessity (ability to pay rent reliably) rather than set arbitrarily high, and the agent implementing an owner's screening policy should recognize this risk rather than assume "it's neutral, so it's fine."

## 3. Steering, Redlining, and Blockbuking

**Steering** is directing prospective buyers or renters toward or away from particular neighborhoods based on a protected characteristic — even subtly, even with good intentions ("I think you'd be happier in this neighborhood" said to a family of a particular ethnicity, based on assumptions about that neighborhood's demographics, is steering).

**Redlining** is the historical and ongoing practice of denying or limiting financial services (loans, insurance) to residents of certain areas based on the racial or ethnic composition of those areas, rather than individual creditworthiness.

**Blockbuking** is inducing panic selling by suggesting (explicitly or implicitly) that the entry of protected-class residents into a neighborhood will cause property values to decline — a historically devastating practice that fair housing law specifically targets.

Each of these can occur through language that sounds like ordinary real estate talk. "That area has really changed" or "you'd probably want to look somewhere with better schools" said with an unstated assumption about who lives where is exactly the kind of coded language fair housing testing is designed to catch.

## 4. Reasonable Accommodation and Reasonable Modification

Disability protections include two distinct affirmative obligations that agents frequently confuse:

- A **reasonable accommodation** is a change to a *rule, policy, or practice* — for example, permitting an assistance animal despite a no-pets policy, or allowing a reserved accessible parking space.
- A **reasonable modification** is a change to the *physical structure* — for example, installing a wheelchair ramp or grab bars.

**The cost allocation differs**: a landlord generally must provide a reasonable accommodation at the landlord's own expense (subject to genuine undue-burden limits), while a tenant seeking a reasonable modification to a private rental unit generally bears the cost of the modification, though the landlord must permit it if reasonable, and in some cases must restore the unit to its original condition at move-out. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory cost-allocation and restoration rules for reasonable modifications in California rental housing -->

**An assistance/support animal is not a "pet"** for fair housing purposes and cannot be subject to pet deposits, breed restrictions, or pet rent — this remains one of the most frequently litigated and frequently mishandled fair housing issues in rental housing.

### Worked Example 2 — The emotional support animal request (Ontario)

A tenant with documentation from a healthcare provider requests to keep a dog as an emotional support animal in a building with a strict no-pets policy and breed restrictions that would otherwise exclude that dog's breed.

**Analysis.** This is a reasonable accommodation request, not a pet request. Provided the documentation is legitimate and the animal does not pose a direct threat or fundamentally alter the nature of the housing, the landlord generally must grant the accommodation — the no-pets policy and breed restriction do not apply to a qualifying assistance animal. An agent or property manager who denies this request by applying the ordinary pet policy is very likely violating fair housing law, and this is a frequent source of real litigation and settlements.

## 5. Enforcement and Remedies

Fair housing complaints can be pursued through multiple bodies simultaneously or in sequence:

- **HUD** (federal) investigates FHA complaints and can refer cases for administrative or judicial enforcement.
- **California's Civil Rights Department (CRD)** (formerly DFEH) investigates FEHA/Unruh complaints and can pursue administrative or civil action.
- **Private civil lawsuits** are also available directly, without exhausting an administrative process first, under both federal and California law.
- **Fair housing "testers"** — individuals who pose as prospective buyers or renters specifically to detect discriminatory treatment — have **legal standing to sue** even though they never genuinely intended to rent or buy, and their evidence is generally admissible.

**Remedies can include** actual damages, statutory/civil penalties, injunctive relief (changing a policy going forward), and attorney's fees — and for real estate licensees specifically, a fair housing violation also risks license discipline independent of any civil liability.

## 6. Advertising Compliance

Fair housing law reaches marketing and advertising directly: any published statement, limitation, or preference based on a protected class is prohibited, even if the property itself is otherwise available to everyone. This includes photography and phrasing that signal a preference (e.g., describing a property as ideal for a particular family type, or using imagery that consistently excludes certain groups across a marketing campaign) as well as explicit language.

**The practical discipline:** describe the property, not the imagined resident. "Great starter home, walkable to parks" is safe; "perfect for a young family" edges toward familial-status-coded language and should be avoided even though it may feel like harmless marketing copy.

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Disparate treatment** | Intentional discrimination based on a protected characteristic. |
| **Disparate impact** | A facially neutral policy with a statistically disproportionate discriminatory effect, without a defensible business necessity. |
| **Steering** | Directing prospects toward or away from neighborhoods based on a protected characteristic. |
| **Redlining** | Denying or limiting financial services based on an area's racial/ethnic composition. |
| **Blockbuking** | Inducing panic selling by suggesting protected-class residents will lower property values. |
| **Reasonable accommodation** | A required change to a rule or policy for a person with a disability. |
| **Reasonable modification** | A required allowance for a physical structural change for a person with a disability. |
| **Source of income protection** | California's protection against discrimination based on lawful income source, including housing vouchers. |
| **Civil Rights Department (CRD)** | California's state agency enforcing FEHA and Unruh Act housing protections. |

## 8. Exam and Practice Tips

- California's protected class list is broader than federal FHA's — source of income, marital status, and ancestry are commonly tested California-specific additions.
- Disparate impact does not require intent — good intentions are not a defense when a neutral policy has a discriminatory statistical effect.
- Know the three coded practices by name and definition: steering, redlining, blockbuking.
- Reasonable accommodation = policy change (landlord's cost); reasonable modification = physical change (often tenant's cost, landlord must permit).
- Assistance animals are never "pets" for fair housing purposes — no pet deposit, no breed restriction.
- Testers have standing to sue even without genuine intent to rent or buy — this is a frequently tested exam point.
- Advertising language describing an "ideal" resident type, not just the property, is a common compliance trap.
