# Unit 7 — Finance Law: Security Instruments, Foreclosure & Anti-Deficiency Rules

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** the promissory note from the security instrument and explain why both exist.
2. **Compare** judicial and non-judicial foreclosure procedurally and strategically.
3. **Apply** California's anti-deficiency rules to purchase-money and non-purchase-money loans.
4. **Explain** the "one-action rule" and its practical effect on a lender's choices.
5. **Analyze** a fact pattern to determine whether a deficiency judgment is available.
6. **Distinguish** judicial foreclosure's redemption rights from non-judicial foreclosure's lack of them.
7. **Advise** (within license scope) on why a distressed homeowner needs an attorney early, not late.

---

## 1. Two Documents, Two Jobs

Every secured real estate loan involves two separate legal instruments performing two separate jobs (Principles Unit 11 introduced this; this unit goes deeper into the litigation and remedy consequences):

- The **promissory note** is the borrower's personal promise to repay — it creates the debt obligation and is, standing alone, an unsecured personal IOU.
- The **security instrument** (in California, almost always a **deed of trust**, occasionally a mortgage) pledges the real property as collateral for that promise, giving the lender (or its trustee) the right to have the property sold if the borrower defaults.

Because California uses the deed of trust structure overwhelmingly, this unit focuses there. A deed of trust involves three parties: the **trustor** (borrower), the **beneficiary** (lender), and the **trustee** (a neutral third party holding bare legal title as security, with the power to conduct a sale on default).

## 2. Judicial vs. Non-Judicial Foreclosure

**Non-judicial foreclosure** (foreclosure under the power of sale contained in the deed of trust) is the overwhelming default method in California. It does not require filing a lawsuit — the trustee follows a statutory notice and timeline (notice of default, waiting period, notice of trustee's sale, public auction) culminating in a trustee's sale. It is faster and cheaper for the lender than judicial foreclosure. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory notice periods and any temporary state-specific forbearance/notice requirements in effect -->

**Judicial foreclosure** is a lawsuit asking a court to order the property sold to satisfy the debt. It is slower and more expensive, but it comes with two features non-judicial foreclosure does not: the possibility of a **deficiency judgment** in some circumstances, and the borrower's **statutory right of redemption** after the sale (a further period during which the borrower may reclaim the property by paying the full amount owed, plus costs). <!-- VERIFY: current redemption period length, which depends on sale proceeds relative to debt -->

**The strategic trade a lender faces:** non-judicial foreclosure is fast and avoids a deficiency judgment being pursued in that action, but it also means the lender generally waives the right to sue for a deficiency afterward on that same debt. Judicial foreclosure preserves the deficiency option (subject to anti-deficiency rules below) but is slow, costly, and gives the borrower a post-sale redemption period the lender does not want to wait out. Most institutional lenders choose non-judicial foreclosure for exactly this reason — speed and finality, in exchange for giving up the deficiency remedy.

## 3. Anti-Deficiency Rules: California's Borrower Protections

A **deficiency** is the shortfall between what a foreclosure sale recovers and what the borrower still owes. California has some of the strongest anti-deficiency protections in the country, though the rules are not absolute and depend on the type of loan.

**Purchase-money loans** — loans used to purchase an owner-occupied residence of specified size, taken from the seller (seller carryback) or from an institutional lender to fund the purchase — are generally protected from deficiency judgments regardless of foreclosure method. This is the core anti-deficiency rule: **a lender who forecloses on a true purchase-money loan on a qualifying residence generally cannot also sue the borrower personally for any shortfall.** <!-- VERIFY: current statutory definition and unit/size limits for the protected residence category -->

**Non-purchase-money loans** — refinances, home equity lines of credit, and cash-out loans — do **not** automatically carry the same protection. Whether a deficiency judgment is available depends heavily on the foreclosure method chosen and the specific facts. <!-- VERIFY: current treatment of refinance and HELOC deficiency exposure, including any purchase-money-refinance carve-outs -->

**The "one-action rule"** requires a secured lender to exhaust its security (foreclose on the collateral) before or as its sole method of pursuing the debt — a lender generally cannot simply sue the borrower personally on the note while ignoring the security, and cannot pursue multiple simultaneous actions on the same debt. This rule interacts closely with the anti-deficiency protections and is one of the more conceptually dense areas of California real estate finance law.

### Worked Example 1 — Purchase-money protection (Chino)

A buyer purchases a Chino home as their primary residence with an institutional first mortgage used entirely to fund the purchase. Years later, after a job loss, the buyer defaults, and the lender completes a non-judicial foreclosure. The sale proceeds are $60,000 less than the remaining loan balance.

**Analysis.** Because this is a purchase-money loan on a qualifying owner-occupied residence, the lender's remedy is generally limited to the foreclosure itself — the lender cannot pursue the former homeowner personally for the $60,000 shortfall. This is precisely the protection anti-deficiency law exists to provide: a homeowner who loses the property in foreclosure is not also left facing a personal money judgment on top of that loss.

### Worked Example 2 — A refinance changes the analysis (Fontana)

The same type of buyer purchases a home, then two years later refinances the original purchase-money loan into a new loan with a different lender, taking some cash out in the process. After a later default and foreclosure, the sale proceeds fall short of the new loan balance.

**Analysis.** Because the original purchase-money loan was replaced by a refinance — and especially because cash was taken out — the new loan is very likely **not** treated as a purchase-money loan, meaning the automatic anti-deficiency protection may not apply in the same way. Whether a deficiency judgment is available now depends on the foreclosure method used and other facts specific to the loan. This distinction — "the original loan was purchase-money, but a later refinance often is not" — is one of the most commonly misunderstood points among both agents and homeowners, and it is exactly the kind of fact pattern that requires an attorney's analysis, not an agent's assumption.

## 4. Redemption Rights

**The right of redemption** allows a borrower whose property was sold at a judicial foreclosure sale to reclaim it by paying the full sale price (plus interest and costs) within a statutory period after the sale — but **only** when the foreclosure was judicial. Property sold at a **non-judicial** trustee's sale carries **no** post-sale statutory right of redemption for the former owner; once the trustee's sale is complete, it is final. <!-- VERIFY: current redemption period lengths under judicial foreclosure, which vary based on whether the sale proceeds were sufficient to satisfy the debt -->

This is one more reason non-judicial foreclosure dominates: lenders prefer finality, and borrowers who might have used a redemption period to save the property essentially never get that option under the far more common non-judicial process.

## 5. Notice, Timelines, and Where Litigation Enters

Even non-judicial foreclosure, despite not being a lawsuit itself, generates real litigation: borrowers challenge whether the trustee followed statutory notice requirements exactly, whether the party foreclosing actually held the right to enforce the note (a "standing to foreclose" challenge, prominent during and after the 2008 mortgage crisis), and whether required loss-mitigation/loan-modification review occurred before the sale where state or federal rules require it. <!-- VERIFY: current California Homeowner Bill of Rights protections around dual tracking and loan modification review before foreclosure --> A wrongful foreclosure claim, if successful, can unwind a completed trustee's sale — a remedy of last resort, but a real one.

## 6. The Licensee's Role With a Distressed Homeowner

Agents regularly encounter homeowners facing default, and the professional boundary here is sharp: a licensee may discuss listing the property, explain the foreclosure timeline in general terms, and refer the client to resources — but **may not** advise on legal rights, negotiate directly with the lender's legal department on the homeowner's behalf as a matter of legal representation, or promise a specific deficiency or credit outcome. Distressed-homeowner transactions also carry specific California consumer-protection statutes governing equity purchasers and foreclosure consultants that agents must be aware exist, even if the specific compliance duties fall on specialized parties. <!-- VERIFY: current Home Equity Sales Contract Act / foreclosure consultant statute requirements applicable to agents working with distressed sellers --> The single most valuable thing an agent can do for a homeowner facing foreclosure is get them to a housing counselor or attorney **early** — well before the notice of trustee's sale — because options narrow sharply as the timeline runs out.

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Promissory note** | The borrower's personal promise to repay a debt. |
| **Deed of trust** | California's standard security instrument, involving trustor, beneficiary, and trustee. |
| **Non-judicial foreclosure** | Foreclosure under the power of sale, without a lawsuit; the dominant California method. |
| **Judicial foreclosure** | Court-ordered foreclosure, allowing a possible deficiency judgment and a post-sale redemption right. |
| **Deficiency** | The shortfall between foreclosure sale proceeds and the remaining debt owed. |
| **Purchase-money loan** | A loan used to fund the purchase of the security property, generally protected from deficiency judgments on a qualifying residence. |
| **One-action rule** | Requires a secured lender to pursue its security before or as its sole method of collecting the debt. |
| **Right of redemption** | A judicial-foreclosure-only right to reclaim property post-sale by paying the full amount owed within a statutory period. |
| **Wrongful foreclosure** | A claim challenging a foreclosure's validity, potentially unwinding a completed sale. |

## 8. Exam and Practice Tips

- Non-judicial foreclosure is the dominant California method: faster, cheaper, no lawsuit, but the lender generally gives up the deficiency judgment remedy on that debt.
- Judicial foreclosure allows a possible deficiency judgment but grants the borrower a post-sale redemption right that non-judicial sales do not.
- Purchase-money loans on a qualifying owner-occupied residence are generally protected from deficiency judgments — refinances and HELOCs generally are not automatically protected the same way.
- "The original loan was purchase-money, but the refinance often is not" is a frequently tested distinction.
- The one-action rule prevents a lender from suing on the note while ignoring the security, or pursuing multiple actions on the same debt.
- A licensee's role with a distressed homeowner is limited — refer to a housing counselor or attorney early, before options narrow.
