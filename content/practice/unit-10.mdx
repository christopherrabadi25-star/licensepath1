# Unit 10 — Financing the Buyer

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** prequalification, preapproval, underwriting approval, and a clear to close.
2. **Explain** the buyer's loan timeline without acting as a lender, attorney, or tax adviser.
3. **Calculate** basic loan-to-value, down payment, and seller-credit amounts.
4. **Recognize** financing red flags early enough to protect the buyer and transaction.
5. **Coordinate** productively with a lender while preserving the buyer's privacy and choice.
6. **Describe** the purpose of the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure.
7. **Apply** fair-lending and advertising boundaries to financing conversations.

---

## 1. Your Role Is Essential — and Limited

Financing is the source of more transaction anxiety than almost any other subject. A buyer will often ask you, “Can I afford this?” “Is this a good rate?” or “Will this lender get me approved?” Those are understandable questions. They are not permission to become the loan officer.

Your job is to make the financing process visible, help the buyer ask better questions, spot transaction risks, and keep the contract calendar aligned with the loan timeline. The lender's job is to take an application, analyze credit and income, recommend loan products, make required disclosures, and determine approval. The appraiser's job is to develop an independent opinion of value. The underwriter's job is to evaluate the file against the lender's and investor's requirements. The buyer's job is to provide accurate documents, avoid financial changes, and decide whether loan terms are acceptable.

The boundary is practical, not ceremonial. A real estate agent who says “you are definitely approved” can create a devastating reliance problem. A buyer can be well qualified in principle and still lose approval because income cannot be documented, the appraisal is low, a condo project does not meet lender requirements, insurance is unavailable, or the buyer opens a new credit account before closing.

Use careful language:

- “Your lender is the person who can confirm this.”
- “Here are the questions I would ask before we write the offer.”
- “This condition affects the loan timeline; let’s get the lender's written status.”
- “I can help you compare the transaction terms, but I cannot tell you which loan is best for you.”

That language is not evasive. It protects the buyer from false certainty and protects you from practicing outside your competence.

## 2. Four Milestones That Buyers Commonly Confuse

### Prequalification

A prequalification is an early estimate based on information the buyer provides. It may involve a conversation about income, debts, assets, and credit, but the depth of verification varies widely. It can be useful for setting expectations. It is not a promise that the buyer can close.

### Preapproval

A preapproval generally means the lender has reviewed more information, often including a credit report and documents supporting income and assets. But the label is not standardized across every lender. One lender's “preapproval” can be another lender's preliminary screen. Ask what was actually verified, what conditions remain, how long the approval is valid, and whether the loan amount depends on a particular property type or down payment.

### Underwriting approval

After contract acceptance, the loan file moves through more complete underwriting. The lender evaluates the borrower and the property. Conditions can include updated bank statements, explanations of deposits, appraisal review, insurance, title items, or documentation of employment. A buyer can hear “approved with conditions” and feel finished; the transaction is not finished until those conditions are satisfied.

### Clear to close

“Clear to close” means the lender has completed its required conditions and is prepared for closing, subject to final process steps. It is materially stronger than a preapproval. Do not replace the lender's confirmation with an agent's impression. Request status through the buyer and lender in a way that respects authorizations and privacy.

## 3. The Loan Timeline Inside the Transaction Timeline

Financing begins before the offer and continues until funding. A disciplined buyer's agent maps the two timelines together.

| Transaction point | Financing work | Agent's coordination role |
|---|---|---|
| Before touring seriously | Buyer consults lender, reviews funds, understands payment range | Ask what has been verified; help buyer define an offer range |
| Before writing | Lender reviews target price, property type, down payment, credit, and closing-cost assumptions | Confirm lender can contact listing side if buyer authorizes it |
| Acceptance | Formal application and documentation collection begin or deepen | Calendar loan and appraisal milestones immediately |
| First week | Appraisal ordered; lender requests documents and disclosures are delivered | Make access easy; remind buyer that delays have consequences |
| Underwriting | Conditions, appraisal, title, insurance, and final income/asset review | Track status, not private details; escalate timeline risk early |
| Pre-closing | Closing Disclosure, final cash-to-close, signing instructions | Have buyer confirm funds and timing directly with lender/escrow |
| Funding and recording | Lender wires funds; escrow records | Never give wire instructions by unverified email or text |

The key distinction is between *asking for status* and *asking for sensitive documents*. You may need to know whether an appraisal was ordered or whether underwriting has a material timing condition. You do not need a buyer's tax return, account number, or credit score. If a lender shares personal information without a buyer's authorization, stop the conversation and ask the buyer to communicate directly or provide written permission.

## 4. The Numbers You Must Understand

You do not need to price a loan. You do need to understand a few basic calculations so you can recognize whether the buyer's cash assumptions and offer terms fit together.

### Down payment

**Down payment = purchase price − loan amount.**

If a buyer offers $800,000 and borrows $640,000, the down payment is $160,000. That is 20% of the price. But the buyer also needs closing costs, prepaid items, reserves if required, and possibly an appraisal-gap amount. Never describe the down payment as the buyer's total cash requirement.

### Loan-to-value ratio (LTV)

**LTV = loan amount ÷ value (or purchase price, depending on program and appraisal).**

For the $640,000 loan on an $800,000 purchase, LTV is 80%. If the appraisal is $760,000, a lender may calculate against the lower value. The same $640,000 loan becomes approximately 84.2% LTV ($640,000 ÷ $760,000). That can change mortgage-insurance requirements, loan eligibility, or the amount of cash the buyer must bring.

### Seller credit

**Seller credit = negotiated dollar amount, subject to the contract and loan-program limits.**

A seller credit can help with allowable closing costs, prepaids, or rate-related costs depending on the loan. It is not free money and it does not necessarily reduce the down payment. The lender must confirm what is permitted. When writing an offer, make the requested credit explicit and ensure the buyer understands that unused credit may not become cash back at closing.

### Payment is more than principal and interest

Buyers often fixate on the advertised principal-and-interest payment. Their actual housing payment may include principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, homeowners-association dues, and sometimes special assessments. The familiar shorthand **PITI**—principal, interest, taxes, and insurance—is helpful, but not exhaustive.

An agent should not use a payment calculator as an approval determination. Use it to frame questions: “Does this estimate include HOA dues?” “Has the insurance quote reflected the property's fire-zone exposure?” “Did the lender model the requested seller credit?”

## 5. Appraisal Is a Financing Event, Not a Negotiation Tool

The appraisal affects the lender's collateral analysis. It is not a second listing presentation and it is not an opportunity to pressure the appraiser. Provide factual, relevant information if the lender or appraiser requests it: the executed contract, factual comparable-sale data, a list of documented improvements, and access information. Do not advocate through unsupported conclusions or conceal negative data.

When the appraisal is lower than the contract price, the buyer and seller have choices shaped by the contract and loan program. The buyer might increase cash, renegotiate price, alter financing, challenge an appraisal through the lender's process, or cancel if the applicable contingency remains. The seller might reduce price, seek another buyer, or hold firm. Your job is to explain the transaction paths, check the actual contingency language, and keep the parties from treating an appraisal gap as a moral failure by either side.

### Worked Example 1 — The gap is not the buyer's only cash problem

Jasmine contracts at $900,000 with an 80% loan based on the appraised value. She planned a 20% down payment ($180,000) and estimated $18,000 in closing costs. The appraisal comes in at $875,000.

The lender's 80% maximum loan is now $700,000 (80% × $875,000), not $720,000. If Jasmine still pays $900,000, she needs $200,000 for the price difference after the loan, plus estimated costs: roughly $218,000 total before any reserves. The appraisal gap created a **$20,000 additional cash requirement**, not merely a theoretical valuation issue.

The right response is not “just bring the difference.” Ask the lender whether the loan structure remains available, confirm the buyer's verified funds, and discuss the contractual options with the buyer and broker.

## 6. Disclosures and Consumer Protection

Two federal forms are especially important in a typical covered consumer mortgage transaction:

- The **Loan Estimate** is designed to help a borrower understand estimated terms and costs after application. 
- The **Closing Disclosure** is designed to show the final terms and costs before consummation, subject to applicable timing rules. <!-- VERIFY: current timing requirements and exceptions before DRE submission -->

You should encourage buyers to read these forms and ask their lender about changes. Do not tell a buyer that a cost increase is “normal” without understanding it. A change may be legitimate; the lender is the right source for the explanation.

Consumer-protection laws also prohibit discriminatory credit practices. A real estate agent should never direct a buyer to or away from financing based on race, national origin, religion, sex, disability, familial status, source of income, or another protected characteristic. “That lender is better for people like you” is both vague and dangerous. Use neutral, consistent referral criteria such as product availability, language access requested by the buyer, service area, responsiveness, and the lender's ability to work with the buyer's chosen property type.

Avoid referral arrangements that compromise the buyer's choice or violate applicable law. A preferred-lender list is not a mandate. The buyer chooses the lender.

## 7. Financing Red Flags You Should Catch Early

| Red flag | Why it matters | Professional response |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer calls a conversation a “full approval” | Documents or credit may not have been reviewed | Ask what was verified and what conditions remain |
| Buyer changes jobs or compensation structure | Income eligibility can change | Tell buyer to speak with lender before making changes |
| New car, furniture financing, or credit inquiry | Debt ratio and credit can change | Explain the timing risk; have buyer call lender first |
| Large undocumented bank deposit | Underwriting may require source documentation | Ask buyer to respond promptly to lender, not to hide anything |
| Fire-zone or unusual property | Insurance or eligibility can affect closing | Obtain insurance quotes early; consult lender on property eligibility |
| HOA, condominium, or mixed-use property | Project review may be required | Confirm lender experience with the property type before offer |
| Buyer is slow with documents | Underwriting cannot finish | Set a same-day document-response expectation |
| Wire instruction change by email | High wire-fraud risk | Confirm through a known phone number, never email alone |

Do not interrogate the buyer about protected or private information. Focus on timing and process: “Has your lender asked for anything you have not yet delivered?” “Would you like me to coordinate a deadline update with the other side?”

## 8. Lender Communication That Helps Rather Than Harms

At offer stage, a strong lender letter can help a listing agent understand the buyer's position. But a letter should be accurate. It should not imply verification that has not occurred or expose private data unnecessarily.

After acceptance, establish one communication rhythm. With the buyer's permission, ask the lender for milestone status: application received, appraisal ordered, appraisal received, underwriting submitted, conditions outstanding, clear to close, and loan documents scheduled. Do not ask the lender to disclose the buyer's detailed income, credit score, medical information, or account balances.

When a lender misses a milestone, do not announce certainty to the seller. Say what you know: “The lender has indicated underwriting needs an additional document and expects an update tomorrow. We will update you by 3 p.m.” Then follow through. Accurate, timely communication is more credible than optimistic promises.

### Worked Example 2 — A “preapproval” that was only a starting point

Mateo offers on a $650,000 condominium with a letter stating he is preapproved. After acceptance, the lender learns his income includes recently started self-employment and that the condominium project needs additional review. The agent tells the seller, “No issue—he's preapproved.”

That statement is a mistake. The correct statement is that the lender is reviewing income and the project, and the buyer should provide requested documentation immediately. The buyer's agent should evaluate the loan-contingency timeline, ask whether the buyer has another financing path, and communicate factual status without declaring an outcome. Preapproval reduced uncertainty; it did not eliminate it.

### Worked Example 3 — Seller credit and offer strength

Two offers arrive at $750,000. Offer A requests $15,000 in seller credit. Offer B requests no credit. If both buyers finance at similar terms and close on the same date, Offer B may produce a higher seller net by approximately $15,000 before other differences. But the listing agent should still compare deposits, contingencies, appraisal risk, buyer funds, repair expectations, and timing. “Same price” rarely means “same offer.”

Your buyer needs this explained before writing. A credit can make their cash-to-close workable, but it can also make their offer less competitive. That is a business tradeoff for the buyer to decide with complete information.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Prequalification** | An early financing estimate based on information supplied by a prospective borrower; not a commitment to lend. |
| **Preapproval** | A lender's preliminary approval based on a defined level of review; remaining conditions and verification matter. |
| **Underwriting** | The lender's evaluation of borrower, property, and loan documentation against lending requirements. |
| **Clear to close** | The lender's confirmation that required conditions are satisfied and closing may proceed, subject to final process steps. |
| **Loan-to-value ratio (LTV)** | Loan amount divided by the relevant property value; commonly used in underwriting. |
| **Seller credit** | A negotiated seller contribution to allowable buyer closing costs or other permitted items, subject to lender/program limits. |
| **Loan Estimate** | A standardized estimate of loan terms and costs for covered consumer mortgages. |
| **Closing Disclosure** | A standardized disclosure of final loan terms and costs for covered consumer mortgages. |
| **Appraisal gap** | The difference between contract price and appraised value when the appraisal is lower. |
| **PITI** | Principal, interest, taxes, and insurance; a common payment shorthand that may not include all housing costs. |

## 10. Exam and Practice Tips

- A prequalification, preapproval, underwriting approval, and clear to close are **not interchangeable**.
- Calculate LTV using the loan amount divided by the relevant value. When appraisal is lower, the lender may use the lower value.
- A seller credit is subject to loan-program rules and does not automatically become cash back to the buyer.
- Never promise that a borrower is approved or that a loan will fund.
- Treat appraisal, insurance, HOA/project review, and buyer-document delays as early transaction risks.
- Confirm wire instructions through a known, independently verified phone number.
- Your job is coordination and explanation, not loan origination or financial advice.
