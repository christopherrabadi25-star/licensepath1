# Unit 9 — Land Use & Environmental Law

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** the police power basis for zoning from eminent domain and inverse condemnation.
2. **Explain** CEQA's basic review process and why it matters to development timelines.
3. **Apply** the difference between a variance and a nonconforming use.
4. **Identify** major California environmental disclosure and liability regimes affecting real estate.
5. **Analyze** how state housing-production laws override local discretion in specific circumstances.
6. **Recognize** common environmental red flags in a transaction (contamination, hazard zones, wells/septic).
7. **Advise** (within license scope) on when a land-use or environmental question needs specialized counsel.

## 1. Police Power: The Constitutional Basis for Land Use Regulation

Government land-use regulation — zoning, building codes, subdivision requirements — rests on the **police power**: the government's inherent constitutional authority to regulate property for public health, safety, morals, and general welfare, without paying compensation to affected owners. This is fundamentally different from **eminent domain**, where the government actually takes private property for public use and **must** pay just compensation (Principles Unit 14).

**Inverse condemnation** is the mirror image of eminent domain: it is a lawsuit a property owner brings when government action or regulation has effectively taken or damaged their property *without* formally exercising eminent domain and without paying compensation — for example, government-caused flooding, or a regulation so restrictive it deprives the owner of essentially all economically viable use of the land. Courts distinguish a lawful, uncompensated police-power regulation from a regulation so extreme it becomes a compensable "regulatory taking" — a fact-intensive line that is genuinely difficult to draw and is squarely litigated territory, not something a licensee assesses independently.

## 2. Zoning: Variances vs. Nonconforming Uses

**Zoning** divides land into districts with permitted uses, density, height, and setback rules, adopted under police power pursuant to a jurisdiction's general plan. Two related but distinct concepts often confused:

- A **variance** is permission granted to deviate from the zoning ordinance for a *specific parcel*, typically because strict application would create an unusual and unnecessary hardship unique to that property (an oddly shaped lot, for example) — granted prospectively, through an application and hearing process, and generally requires showing the variance will not harm the surrounding area or grant a special privilege inconsistent with other properties in the zone.
- A **legal nonconforming use** is a use that was lawful when established but no longer conforms to a *later-adopted* zoning change — the property is generally allowed to continue that use ("grandfathered"), but the right is typically narrow: it usually cannot be expanded, and it can often be lost if abandoned for a specified period or if the structure is destroyed beyond a specified extent and not promptly rebuilt. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory/local abandonment and rebuild thresholds, which vary by jurisdiction -->

### Worked Example 1 — Variance vs. nonconforming use in the same neighborhood (Claremont)

Two properties on the same street both sit closer to the front property line than the current zoning setback allows. Property A's structure was built decades ago, before the setback rule existed, and has been continuously used the same way since. Property B's owner wants to build a new addition that would sit at the same close-to-street distance, in violation of the current setback.

**Analysis.** Property A is a **legal nonconforming use** — grandfathered because it predates the rule and has been in continuous use; it generally does not need approval to continue as-is, but expanding it further into the setback would likely exceed what grandfathering protects. Property B's owner, by contrast, needs a **variance** — a new, forward-looking exception — and will have to show the setback creates a unique hardship on that specific lot, not just that the addition would be convenient or that the neighboring nonconforming structure looks similar.

## 3. CEQA: California's Environmental Review Framework

The **California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA)** requires state and local government agencies to evaluate and disclose the environmental impacts of discretionary projects they approve, and to adopt feasible mitigation measures or alternatives where significant impacts are identified. It applies broadly to new development, general plan amendments, and many other discretionary government approvals.

**CEQA review, in outline:**

1. An initial determination of whether the project is exempt (many routine or minor projects are).
2. If not exempt, an **Initial Study** to determine whether the project may have a significant environmental effect.
3. If significant effects are possible, either a **Negative Declaration** (project modified to avoid significant impacts) or a full **Environmental Impact Report (EIR)** — a detailed, often lengthy analysis of impacts, alternatives, and mitigation.

**Why agents care:** CEQA review, and especially a required EIR, can add substantial time and cost to a development project and is a frequent target of litigation by opponents of a project (sometimes for genuine environmental concerns, sometimes as a delay tactic in unrelated disputes). An agent representing a buyer of undeveloped or underdeveloped land who is not aware that a planned use may trigger significant CEQA review — and the accompanying timeline risk — has not adequately served that client. <!-- VERIFY: current CEQA streamlining exemptions for housing, which have expanded significantly in recent years -->

## 4. State Housing Law Overriding Local Discretion

In recent years, California has enacted a series of statutes designed to override local government discretion specifically to increase housing production, in response to a severe statewide housing shortage — a significant and evolving area. Broadly, these laws:

- Limit a local jurisdiction's ability to deny or unreasonably condition qualifying housing developments that comply with objective zoning and design standards (the **Housing Accountability Act** framework).
- Allow, by right in many single-family zones, the addition of accessory dwelling units (ADUs) and junior ADUs, subject to state-set standards that cities cannot make more restrictive than the state minimum in most respects.
- Create streamlined, ministerial approval pathways for certain qualifying multifamily and affordable projects that meet objective criteria, bypassing much of the discretionary review process (and, often, CEQA review) that would otherwise apply.

**Why this matters:** a licensee working with a buyer interested in an ADU, a small-lot subdivision, or infill multifamily development needs to know, at minimum, that state law has meaningfully expanded what an owner can do "by right" in many zones — and that local staff resistance to a qualifying project is not necessarily the last word.

## 5. Environmental Liability and Disclosure

Beyond zoning and CEQA, several environmental regimes intersect directly with real estate transactions:

- **Superfund/state cleanup liability** — under federal and California hazardous substance cleanup law, current and prior owners of contaminated property can face liability for cleanup costs, in some cases regardless of who actually caused the contamination — a doctrine with real teeth for buyers of industrial or formerly industrial property.
- **Natural Hazard Disclosure** — flood zones, fire hazard severity zones, seismic hazard zones, and earthquake fault zones must be disclosed (Principles Unit 9); this is distinct from, but related to, broader environmental review.
- **Wells, septic systems, and underground storage tanks** — properties outside sewer/municipal water service, or with a history of fuel storage tanks, carry environmental and disclosure considerations that a standard residential transaction on municipal utilities does not.
- **Mold, asbestos, and lead-based paint** — each carries its own disclosure regime layered onto the general material-fact disclosure duty (Principles Unit 9), with lead-based paint disclosure specifically required for pre-1978 housing under federal law.

### Worked Example 2 — The former dry cleaner site (Pomona)

A buyer is interested in a commercial parcel that operated as a dry-cleaning business for thirty years before closing a decade ago. Standard dry-cleaning solvents are known groundwater contaminants.

**Analysis.** This is exactly the fact pattern where an agent should insist on a **Phase I Environmental Site Assessment** before the buyer removes contingencies — a professional environmental consultant's review of historical use and records to identify potential contamination risk, which can then lead to a Phase II (actual soil/groundwater testing) if the Phase I flags concerns. Given potential owner liability for cleanup regardless of fault, a buyer who skips this step on a site with an obvious contamination history risk profile is taking on exposure that can dwarf the property's purchase price. This is squarely a case for environmental counsel and licensed consultants, not general due diligence alone.

## 6. The Licensee's Role

Land use and environmental law intersect with real estate constantly, but licensees are not land-use attorneys, environmental consultants, or CEQA specialists. The professional standard is **pattern recognition**: know enough to spot a zoning nonconformity, a likely CEQA trigger, a possible ADU opportunity, or an environmental red flag — and route it to the right specialist (land-use attorney, environmental consultant, city planning department) before the client is contractually committed, not after.

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Police power** | Government's constitutional authority to regulate property for public health, safety, and welfare without compensation. |
| **Inverse condemnation** | A property owner's claim that government action effectively took or damaged property without formal eminent domain or compensation. |
| **Variance** | Permission to deviate from zoning for a specific parcel due to unique hardship. |
| **Legal nonconforming use** | A grandfathered use lawful when established, no longer conforming to later zoning. |
| **CEQA** | California's environmental review law requiring disclosure and mitigation of significant project impacts. |
| **Environmental Impact Report (EIR)** | The detailed CEQA document required when significant environmental effects are identified. |
| **Housing Accountability Act** | A framework limiting local denial of qualifying housing developments meeting objective standards. |
| **Phase I Environmental Site Assessment** | A historical-use review identifying potential contamination risk before further testing. |

## 8. Exam and Practice Tips

- Police power regulates without compensation; eminent domain takes with compensation; inverse condemnation is the owner's claim when uncompensated regulation goes too far.
- A variance is a forward-looking, parcel-specific exception; a nonconforming use is a grandfathered pre-existing use under new rules — do not confuse the two.
- CEQA can add real time and cost to development; know the basic Initial Study → Negative Declaration/EIR sequence.
- State housing laws increasingly override local discretion for qualifying projects, especially ADUs and objective-standard housing developments.
- Owner liability for contamination cleanup can attach regardless of fault — a Phase I assessment is the standard first step on a red-flag site.
- The licensee's job is pattern recognition and referral, not land-use or environmental legal analysis.
