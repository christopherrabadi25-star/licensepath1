# Unit 5 — Pricing Property: CMA Methodology & Adjustments

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Select** appropriate comparable sales and justify each choice.
2. **Apply** adjustments in the correct direction and derive adjustment values from market evidence.
3. **Reconcile** adjusted comparables into a defensible price range.
4. **Analyze** active and expired listings as competitive and cautionary evidence.
5. **Calculate** price per square foot correctly and explain its limits.
6. **Interpret** absorption rate and months of inventory to advise on timing.
7. **Distinguish** a CMA from an appraisal and stay within licensed authority.

---

## 1. What a CMA Is and Is Not

A **competitive market analysis** is your opinion of a defensible price range, supported by market evidence. It is a normal, expected part of your job.

It is **not an appraisal.** An appraisal is prepared by a licensed or certified appraiser under USPAP. You may not call your CMA an appraisal, present it as one, or accept a fee contingent on reaching a particular value (Principles Unit 13).

The other distinction that matters in practice: an appraiser answers "what is this worth?" while you are answering a more useful question — **"what price produces the best outcome for this seller given their timeline?"** Those are not always the same number. A seller who must close in 30 days and a seller with a year both own the same house.

## 2. Selecting Comparables

The quality of a CMA is decided at comparable selection, before any arithmetic.

**The hierarchy of evidence:**

1. **Closed sales** — what buyers actually paid. This is the only evidence of value.
2. **Pending sales** — where the market is heading; price is unconfirmed until closing.
3. **Active listings** — your competition, and evidence of what sellers *hope* for.
4. **Expired and withdrawn** — evidence of what the market refused.

**Selection criteria**, in rough order of importance:

- **Recency.** Three to six months is typical; shorter in a fast-moving market. An older sale needs a time adjustment you must be able to justify.
- **Proximity.** Same tract or subdivision is ideal. Crossing a major arterial, a school district boundary, or a city line often crosses a value boundary too.
- **Similarity** in living area, lot size, bedroom and bath count, age, condition, and style.
- **Arm's-length.** Exclude sales between relatives, distress sales, and anything with unusual concessions — those are prices, not evidence of value (Principles Unit 13).

**Use three to five strong comparables.** Six weak ones are worse than three good ones, and the fewer and smaller your adjustments, the more reliable your conclusion.

## 3. Making Adjustments

**The rule that decides everything: adjust the comparable, never the subject.**

- **Comparable Better → Subtract** (CBS)
- **Comparable Inferior → Add** (CIA)

**The order of adjustments** matters when several apply: property rights, financing terms, conditions of sale, expenditures immediately after purchase, **market conditions (time)**, then location, then physical characteristics.

### Deriving adjustment values

New agents invent adjustment values. Competent agents derive them from the market.

**Paired sales analysis** is the cleanest method: find two sales alike in every respect but one, and the price difference is the market's adjustment for that feature.

*Example:* in the same tract, a 3-bed/2-bath sold for $712,000 and a 4-bed/2-bath of similar size and condition sold for $741,000. The market's adjustment for the fourth bedroom is roughly **$29,000** — not the $50,000 you might have guessed.

When paired sales are unavailable, use **cost with depreciation** for improvements (a $60,000 pool rarely contributes $60,000 — Principles Unit 13 on contribution), or a **percentage of value** for condition differences, and say clearly in your presentation how you derived it.

**Guardrails on adjustments:**
- **Net adjustment** — total up and down — ideally within about 15% of the comparable's sale price.
- **Gross adjustment** — the absolute sum regardless of direction — ideally within about 25%.
- A comparable requiring 40% gross adjustment is telling you it is not comparable. Find a better one.

## 4. Price Per Square Foot: Use With Care

Price per square foot is the most abused number in residential real estate.

**It is useful for** a rough sanity check within a tract of similar homes, and for comparing homes of nearly identical size.

**It misleads when** homes differ meaningfully in size, because **cost per square foot declines as size increases.** A 1,200 sq ft home and a 3,000 sq ft home in the same neighborhood will not share a price per square foot, and applying the small home's figure to the large one overstates value badly.

It also ignores lot size, condition, view, garage, and everything else buyers actually pay for.

**Use it as a check, never as a method.** If your adjusted-comparable conclusion and your price-per-square-foot estimate diverge wildly, that is a signal to re-examine your comparables — not to average the two.

## 5. Market Context

Two numbers tell a seller what kind of market they are entering.

**Months of inventory** = active listings ÷ average monthly closed sales.

As a rough convention: under about 3 months favors sellers, 4 to 6 is balanced, and above 6 favors buyers. <!-- VERIFY: treat these bands as conventional guidance, not fixed rules -->

**Absorption rate** = the percentage of inventory selling per month, the same relationship inverted.

**Days on market** for recent sales tells the seller what to expect, and **the ratio of sale price to original list price** tells them the cost of starting high. If homes that sold within 14 days averaged 99% of list while those over 60 days averaged 93%, you have the overpricing argument in one line.

## 6. Presenting the Result

**Give a range, not a point.** A range with stated assumptions is honest and more useful: "$1,150,000 to $1,190,000. The top of that range assumes we address the exterior paint and stage the living areas; the bottom assumes we list as-is."

**Show your work.** Every comparable, every adjustment, and where the adjustment values came from. A seller who can follow your reasoning will trust the conclusion. A seller handed a number will argue with it.

**Separate price from strategy.** What the market supports is one question. Whether to list slightly below to generate competition, or at market, is a separate strategic conversation with the seller.

**Never let a seller believe your CMA is an appraisal**, and never let a lender treat it as one.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Full adjustment grid (Glendora)

Your subject: 3 bed, 2 bath, 1,780 sq ft, 2-car garage, no pool, standard condition.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale price | $812,000 | $779,000 | $845,000 |
| Sold | 8 months ago | 2 months ago | 4 months ago |
| Living area | 1,810 | 1,720 | 1,960 |
| Bedrooms | 4 | 3 | 3 |
| Garage | 1-car | 2-car | 3-car |
| Pool | Yes | No | No |

Market has appreciated about **3% over 8 months**. Derived adjustments: bedroom **$25,000**, living area **$180/sq ft**, garage stall **$12,000**, pool **$18,000**.

**Comp A:**
1. Start: $812,000
2. Time (+3%, 8 months): +$24,360 → $836,360
3. Extra bedroom (comp better): −$25,000 → $811,360
4. Living area: 1,780 − 1,810 = −30 sq ft × $180 = −$5,400 → $805,960
5. Garage (comp has 1, subject 2 — comp inferior): +$12,000 → $817,960
6. Pool (comp better): −$18,000 → **$799,960**
   *Gross adjustment: $84,760 = 10.4% of sale price ✓*

**Comp B:**
1. Start: $779,000
2. Time (2 months, ~0.75%): +$5,843 → $784,843
3. Living area: 1,780 − 1,720 = +60 × $180 = +$10,800 → $795,643
4. Bedrooms, garage, pool: no difference
   *Adjusted: **$795,643**. Gross adjustment: $16,643 = 2.1% — the strongest comparable by far.*

**Comp C:**
1. Start: $845,000
2. Time (4 months, ~1.5%): +$12,675 → $857,675
3. Living area: 1,780 − 1,960 = −180 × $180 = −$32,400 → $825,275
4. Garage (comp has 3, subject 2 — comp better): −$12,000 → **$813,275**
   *Gross adjustment: $57,075 = 6.8% ✓*

**Reconciliation:** $799,960 · $795,643 · $813,275

Do **not** average these. Comp B required only 2.1% total adjustment and is the most recent — it deserves the most weight. Comps A and C bracket it above. A defensible conclusion: **$795,000 to $812,000**, with the recommendation near **$799,000**.

Note what averaging would have produced: $802,959 — close, but arrived at by a method that would collapse the moment a seller asked you to justify it.

### Example 2 — Deriving an adjustment from paired sales (Rancho Cucamonga)

You need a pool adjustment. Four sales in the same tract, similar size and condition:

| Sale | Pool | Price |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No | $698,000 |
| 2 | Yes | $719,000 |
| 3 | No | $705,000 |
| 4 | Yes | $722,000 |

**Walkthrough.**
1. Average without pool: ($698,000 + $705,000) ÷ 2 = **$701,500**
2. Average with pool: ($719,000 + $722,000) ÷ 2 = **$720,500**
3. Indicated pool adjustment: **$19,000**

That is derived, defensible, and roughly a third of what an in-ground pool costs to build — a concrete illustration of the principle of contribution (Principles Unit 13), and a number you can show a seller who spent $60,000 on their pool.

### Example 3 — The price per square foot trap (Claremont)

A seller points to a nearby sale: 1,240 sq ft at $694,000 — **$560/sq ft**. His home is **2,900 sq ft**. He concludes: 2,900 × $560 = **$1,624,000**.

**Analysis.** The arithmetic is right and the conclusion is wrong, because **cost per square foot declines as size increases.** Land, kitchen, systems, and site costs do not scale linearly with living area.

Show him actual larger sales in the area. If 2,800–3,000 sq ft homes are selling at $420–$450/sq ft, the supported range is 2,900 × $420–$450 = **$1,218,000–$1,305,000** — roughly $350,000 below his figure.

The teaching point for the seller: price per square foot is a *result* of a sale, not a method for producing one.

### Example 4 — Market context (Inland Empire)

A submarket has **148 active listings** and averaged **41 closed sales per month** over the last three months. Homes selling within 14 days averaged **99.2%** of original list; homes taking over 60 days averaged **92.8%**.

**Walkthrough.**
1. Months of inventory: 148 ÷ 41 = **3.6 months** — a modestly seller-favorable to balanced market.
2. Absorption rate: 41 ÷ 148 = **27.7% per month**.
3. The list-price ratio is the argument: on an $800,000 home, selling quickly at 99.2% yields **$793,600**; sitting past 60 days at 92.8% yields **$742,400** — a **$51,200** difference, before counting two extra months of carrying costs.

That single comparison does more to move a seller off an inflated price than any amount of persuasion.

## 8. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **CMA** | A licensee's competitive market analysis producing a defensible price range; not an appraisal. |
| **Comparable (comp)** | A recent arm's-length sale of a similar property used as evidence of value. |
| **CBS / CIA** | Comparable Better, Subtract; Comparable Inferior, Add — adjust the comparable, never the subject. |
| **Paired sales analysis** | Deriving an adjustment value by comparing two sales alike except for one feature. |
| **Net adjustment** | The sum of adjustments accounting for direction; a reliability check on a comparable. |
| **Gross adjustment** | The absolute sum of adjustments regardless of direction; the better test of comparability. |
| **Time (market conditions) adjustment** | An adjustment for price movement between the comparable's sale date and the effective date. |
| **Months of inventory** | Active listings divided by average monthly sales; indicates market balance. |
| **Absorption rate** | The share of inventory selling per month. |
| **Sale-to-list ratio** | Sale price as a percentage of original list price; evidence of the cost of overpricing. |
| **Price per square foot** | Sale price divided by living area; a check on a conclusion, never a method for reaching one. |

## 9. Practice Tips

- **Adjust the comparable, never the subject.** CBS/CIA, every line, every time.
- **Derive adjustments from paired sales** where you can, and say how you derived them.
- **Watch gross adjustment.** Above roughly 25% and the property is not comparable.
- **Never average adjusted comparables.** Weight them by reliability and recency, and explain the weighting.
- **Price per square foot declines as size increases.** Never extrapolate a small home's figure onto a large one.
- **Lead with sale-to-list ratios by days on market.** It is the clearest evidence of what overpricing costs.
- **Give a range with stated assumptions**, not a single number.
- **It is a CMA, never an appraisal**, and never take a fee contingent on a value.
