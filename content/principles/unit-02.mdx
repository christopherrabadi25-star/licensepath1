# Unit 2 — Property: Real vs. Personal, Fixtures & Appurtenances

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Define** real property and personal property and explain how each transfers.
2. **Describe** the physical components of land: surface, subsurface, airspace, and what is affixed or appurtenant.
3. **Apply** the MARIA fixture tests to decide whether an item is real or personal property.
4. **Distinguish** ordinary fixtures from trade fixtures and explain a tenant's removal rights.
5. **Explain** appurtenances, including easements, stock in a mutual water company, and mineral rights.
6. **Differentiate** riparian and littoral water rights and California's doctrine of reasonable use.
7. **Classify** crops as natural growth (fructus naturales) or emblements (fructus industriales) and state who owns them.

---

## 1. Two Kinds of Property — and Why the Line Matters

California law divides everything ownable into two categories. **Real property** is land, that which is affixed to land, that which is appurtenant (attached by right) to land, and that which is immovable by law. **Personal property** — also called *chattel* — is everything else: movable things, from patio furniture to a car to money.

Why does the line matter to you, the agent? Because the two kinds of property transfer differently, are financed differently, and are fought over constantly:

- Real property transfers by **deed**; personal property transfers by **bill of sale**.
- When escrow closes on a Whittier house, everything that is real property goes to the buyer automatically — even if the contract never mentions it. Everything that is personal property stays with the seller unless the contract says otherwise.
- Half the "the sellers took the..." disputes you will ever mediate — chandeliers, mounted TVs, garage shelving, the koi in the pond — are really classification questions.

Property can also change categories. Lumber at a Fontana supply yard is personal property; nailed into a deck, it becomes real property (**annexation**). Tear the deck out and haul it away, and it is personal property again (**severance**). A growing orange tree is real property; picked oranges are personal property.

## 2. What "Land" Includes

Land is more than the dirt you stand on. Ownership of a parcel is often pictured as an inverted pyramid running from the center of the earth, through the surface boundaries, and into the sky:

- **Surface rights** — the ground itself and the right to use it.
- **Subsurface (mineral) rights** — oil, gas, coal, and minerals beneath. These can be sold separately: an owner in Signal Hill can deed away mineral rights while keeping the surface. Once severed, minerals are a separate real property interest, and the mineral owner has an implied right of reasonable surface entry to extract them (often negotiated away).
- **Airspace rights** — the reasonable use of air above the parcel. You cannot exclude jets at 35,000 feet, but a neighbor's crane swinging over your lot invades your airspace. Airspace itself can be real property — a condominium is, at its core, ownership of a box of airspace plus a shared interest in common areas.
- **Water rights and support rights** — covered below.

Also part of real property: **improvements** (structures, fences, pipelines — things affixed by people) and **appurtenances** (rights that go with the land).

## 3. Fixtures and the MARIA Tests

A **fixture** is an item that started life as personal property and became real property by attachment to land or a building. The bolted-in wall oven in a San Marino kitchen was personal property in the showroom; installed, it is a fixture and passes with the house.

When classification is disputed, California courts weigh the factors agents memorize as **MARIA**:

- **M — Method of attachment.** Is it nailed, bolted, plumbed, wired, cemented? Items attached so removal damages the property lean real. Items resting by their own weight lean personal (with exceptions — a house resting on its own weight is still real property).
- **A — Adaptability.** Was the item custom-fitted to the property? Wall-to-wall carpet cut to the rooms, a built-in speaker system, custom window shutters — adapted items lean real even if easily removable.
- **R — Relationship of the parties.** Close calls tilt toward buyer over seller, tenant over landlord, and lender over borrower.
- **I — Intention.** The most important test: did the person attaching the item intend it to remain? Intention is judged objectively from conduct and circumstances, not secret thoughts.
- **A — Agreement.** The parties' written agreement controls. A purchase contract can make the "excluded" chandelier personal property or the "included" washer part of the deal. This test beats the others — which is why your job is to *write it down*.

**Practice rule:** never let clients rely on MARIA when a sentence in the contract removes all doubt. List inclusions and exclusions expressly.

## 4. Trade Fixtures and Emblements — the Two Great Exceptions

### Trade fixtures

A **trade fixture** is an item a *business tenant* attaches to leased premises for use in its trade: the espresso machine plumbed into a Claremont café, a dry cleaner's conveyor, a nail salon's stations. Although attached, trade fixtures remain the **tenant's personal property**, and the tenant may remove them before the lease ends, repairing damage caused by removal. Trade fixtures left behind after the tenancy ends generally become the landlord's property by **accession**.

### Emblements

Crops divide the same way. **Fructus naturales** — naturally occurring growth like native trees and pasture grass — are real property. **Fructus industriales**, or **emblements** — annual crops produced by labor, like strawberries in Oxnard or row vegetables in Chino — are treated as the cultivator's **personal property**. A tenant farmer whose lease ends before harvest may re-enter to harvest the crop their labor produced.

## 5. Appurtenances: Rights That Run With the Land

An **appurtenance** is a right or thing that belongs to and passes with the land — "appurtenant" literally means "belonging to." When the land is conveyed, appurtenances transfer automatically, even if the deed never mentions them. Classic examples:

- **Easements appurtenant** benefiting the parcel (the driveway easement over the neighbor's strip — Unit 5 covers easements in depth).
- **Stock in a mutual water company.** In many older SoCal communities, water is delivered by a mutual water company and the shares are appurtenant to the land: they transfer with the parcel and cannot be sold separately.
- **Mineral rights** not previously severed.
- **Covenants that run with the land** benefiting the parcel.

## 6. Water Rights and Support

California water law blends two doctrines, both limited by the state's **reasonable and beneficial use** requirement — no owner has an unlimited right to water:

- **Riparian rights** attach to land bordering a *flowing* watercourse (river, stream). The riparian owner may make reasonable use of the water on the riparian land, sharing correlatively with other riparians. Riparian rights are appurtenant — they cannot be sold apart from the land.
- **Littoral rights** attach to land bordering a *non-flowing* body — a lake or the ocean.
- **Appropriative rights** (a permit-based system administered by the state) let non-riparian users divert water for beneficial use — the backbone of California agriculture. <!-- VERIFY: keep description general; permitting details change -->
- **Percolating groundwater** is subject to the overlying owner's correlative rights and, increasingly, to groundwater sustainability regulation. <!-- VERIFY: SGMA specifics before deepening this section -->

Boundary changes by water have exam-favorite names: **accretion** is the gradual buildup of soil (**alluvium** is the deposited soil itself), which the owner gains; **erosion** is gradual loss; **avulsion** is the sudden tearing away of land (a flood rerouting a channel), which does *not* immediately change boundaries; **reliction** is land exposed by receding water.

Every parcel is also entitled to **lateral support** (from adjoining land) and **subjacent support** (from beneath, when the subsurface is owned by another). An Inland Empire developer who excavates at the lot line and slumps the neighbor's slope has violated the duty of lateral support.

## 7. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — The mounted TV and the mounting bracket (Azusa)

Sellers in Azusa list their home. The living room features a 75-inch TV on an articulating wall mount bolted into studs, wires routed inside the wall. The contract is silent. At the walk-through, the TV and mount are gone; drywall holes remain.

**Analysis.** Run MARIA. The *TV* clips onto the mount and lifts off — method favors personal property, and custom adaptation is minimal: most agents and courts treat the TV as personal. The *mount*, bolted through drywall into framing with in-wall wiring, is attached and adapted — a fixture that should have stayed, and the sellers owe for the damage. The real lesson is the **A** that wins: an agreement. One contract line — "wall mounts remain; TVs excluded" — would have prevented the dispute.

### Example 2 — Trade fixtures at lease end (Rancho Cucamonga bakery)

A bakery tenant's five-year lease in a Rancho Cucamonga center expires June 30. The tenant installed ovens (bolted, vented through the roof), a walk-in refrigerator, and display cases. On June 28 the tenant removes all three, patches the roof penetration, and repairs bolt holes. The landlord demands the equipment back as "fixtures."

**Analysis.** These are trade fixtures — attached by a business tenant for its trade — so they remained the tenant's personal property. Removal *before the lease ended* with repair of removal damage was exactly what the law allows. Had the tenant vacated and returned in August to collect the ovens, the landlord would win: trade fixtures left after the tenancy generally pass to the landlord.

### Example 3 — Classification math at closing (Chino Hills)

A Chino Hills purchase closes at $850,000. The contract includes the washer, dryer, and refrigerator ("no value assigned") and excludes the dining-room chandelier. For the buyer's loan, the lender requires that personal property carry no financed value.

**Walkthrough.**
1. The house, built-ins, and the *replacement* chandelier the seller installs are real property — conveyed by the grant deed, no listing needed.
2. Washer, dryer, refrigerator are personal property — they pass by the contract's inclusion (functionally a bill of sale) at $0 assigned value, keeping the $850,000 price fully attributable to real property for the lender.
3. The original chandelier, though a fixture by MARIA, is excluded by agreement — the seller must remove it and repair any damage, ideally replacing it before the appraisal so the appraiser never photographs a missing fixture.

### Example 4 — Severed mineral rights (Signal Hill)

An investor buys a Signal Hill lot. The preliminary report shows a 1940s deed reserving all oil and mineral rights to a prior owner, with surface-entry rights waived above 500 feet. The investor owns the surface and airspace; the mineral estate below is someone else's real property. Because surface entry was waived, extraction could occur only by slant drilling from other land. Your job as the agent: make sure the buyer reads the preliminary report and understands what was *not* purchased.

## 8. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Real property** | Land, things affixed to it, things appurtenant to it, and things immovable by law; transferred by deed. |
| **Personal property (chattel)** | Movable property — everything that is not real property; transferred by bill of sale. |
| **Improvement** | Anything affixed to land by human effort: buildings, fences, pipelines. |
| **Fixture** | Personal property converted to real property by attachment, judged by the MARIA tests. |
| **MARIA** | Method of attachment, Adaptability, Relationship of the parties, Intention, Agreement — the fixture tests; agreement controls. |
| **Trade fixture** | An item attached by a business tenant for its trade; remains the tenant's personal property, removable before the lease ends. |
| **Appurtenance** | A right that belongs to and passes automatically with the land, such as an easement or mutual water company stock. |
| **Annexation** | Attaching personal property to land so it becomes real property. |
| **Severance** | Detaching real property (crops, fixtures, minerals) so it becomes personal property. |
| **Accession** | Acquiring property by its addition to yours — e.g., improvements or abandoned trade fixtures left on the premises. |
| **Emblements (fructus industriales)** | Annual crops produced by labor; the cultivator's personal property, harvestable even after a tenancy ends. |
| **Fructus naturales** | Naturally occurring growth (native trees, grasses); part of the real property. |
| **Riparian rights** | Water rights of land bordering a flowing watercourse. |
| **Littoral rights** | Water rights of land bordering a lake or the ocean. |
| **Appropriative rights** | Permit-based rights to divert water for beneficial use on non-riparian land. |
| **Accretion / alluvium** | Gradual gain of land by waterborne soil deposits / the soil deposited. |
| **Avulsion** | Sudden removal or shift of land by water; boundaries do not immediately change. |
| **Reliction** | Land exposed by gradually receding water. |
| **Lateral / subjacent support** | The right to have land supported by adjoining land / by the earth beneath it. |

## 9. Exam Tips

- **"Which is real property?"** questions turn on attachment and appurtenance. Growing native trees: real. Harvested crops: personal. Annual crops under cultivation: personal (emblements) — the exam's favorite curveball.
- **MARIA's hierarchy:** intention is the most important court test, but *agreement between the parties* controls over everything. If the fact pattern mentions a contract term, that term is the answer.
- **Trade fixtures flip the default.** Attached by a business tenant = personal property, removable *before* lease end. Left behind = landlord's by accession.
- **Deed vs. bill of sale** is a two-second giveaway: real property → deed; personal property → bill of sale.
- **Water vocabulary is pure memorization:** flowing = riparian; lake/ocean = littoral; gradual gain = accretion; sudden = avulsion (no boundary change); soil itself = alluvium.
