# Unit 12 — Construction, Mechanics' Liens & New-Home Law

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Explain** the legal theory behind a mechanics' lien and who may file one.
2. **Apply** the preliminary notice and lien-filing timeline requirements at a conceptual level.
3. **Distinguish** a mechanics' lien's priority rules from a typical purchase-money mortgage's priority.
4. **Analyze** a fact pattern where an owner paid the general contractor in full but a subcontractor still files a lien.
5. **Explain** the purpose of a release/waiver of lien and why staged releases matter.
6. **Identify** the core statutory protections buyers of new homes receive from builders.
7. **Advise** (within license scope) on why unpermitted work is a distinct risk from a mechanics' lien risk.

## 1. The Legal Theory Behind a Mechanics' Lien

A **mechanics' lien** is a statutory lien available to contractors, subcontractors, laborers, and material suppliers who improve real property and are not paid for that work. The theory is straightforward: someone who added value to a specific property through labor or materials has a claim against *that property itself*, not just against whoever hired them personally — because the property's value increased due to their work, and it is that increased value the lien reaches.

**Who may file:** general contractors, subcontractors at multiple tiers, laborers, and material/equipment suppliers who furnished labor or materials for a work of improvement, provided they follow the required statutory steps.

## 2. Preliminary Notice: The Step Most Owners Never See Coming

Before most lien claimants (other than a general contractor who has a direct contract with the owner, in many cases) can enforce a lien, they must generally serve a **preliminary 20-day notice** on the property owner — a notice given near the start of their work, informing the owner that the claimant is providing labor or materials to the project and preserving the claimant's right to later file a lien if unpaid. <!-- VERIFY: current preliminary notice statutory deadline and any recent legislative changes to timing -->

**This is the step that surprises owners:** an owner can pay their general contractor in full and still see a lien filed by a subcontractor or supplier the owner never directly hired and may not even have known was on the job — because the sub was working for the general contractor, not the owner, but the sub's lien right attaches to the property regardless of who the owner already paid.

## 3. The Owner's Vulnerability: Paid the GC, Liened by the Sub

### Worked Example 1 — The double-payment trap (Rialto)

A homeowner hires a general contractor for a $90,000 kitchen and addition remodel, pays the full contract price in installments as work progresses, and the project completes. Two months later, a drywall subcontractor who was never paid by the general contractor (who has since become unreachable) records a mechanics' lien against the home for $14,000.

**Analysis.** Despite having paid the general contractor in full, the homeowner can be exposed to the subcontractor's lien — the general contractor's failure to pay a sub does not automatically protect the owner, because the sub's lien right runs against the property. This is exactly why **conditional and unconditional lien releases tied to each payment**, and **verifying subcontractors and suppliers are being paid** (sometimes through joint checks or a title company/escrow-style disbursement process on larger jobs), are standard risk-management practices for any significant construction project — practices a homeowner unfamiliar with construction law would never think to insist on without being told. An agent working with a client planning major renovations, or buying a property with recent unpermitted or under-documented construction work, should flag this risk explicitly.

## 4. Lien Releases and Waivers

A **release (or waiver) of lien** is a document in which a contractor, subcontractor, or supplier gives up lien rights, typically in exchange for payment. California statute provides specific, sometimes mandatory forms distinguishing:

- **Conditional vs. unconditional** — a conditional release is effective only once payment actually clears; an unconditional release is effective immediately upon signing, regardless of whether payment later fails (a real risk if a check bounces).
- **Progress payment vs. final payment** — releases tied to interim progress payments versus the final payment closing out the job.

**The practical lesson for anyone managing a renovation:** obtain conditional releases at each payment stage (protecting against a bounced check) and require lien releases from subcontractors and suppliers, not just the general contractor, before making the final payment.

## 5. Mechanics' Lien Priority

Mechanics' lien priority follows unusual and California-specific rules: a mechanics' lien can, under specified circumstances, **relate back to the date work on the project actually commenced** — which can mean a mechanics' lien recorded *after* a construction loan's deed of trust was recorded can still gain priority *ahead of* that loan if the underlying work began before the loan was recorded. <!-- VERIFY: current statutory commencement-of-work priority rules, which are a genuinely complex and frequently litigated area of California lien law --> This is precisely why construction lenders require careful timing coordination (recording their deed of trust before any visible work commences) and title company oversight on construction loans — the ordinary "first recorded, first in priority" intuition does not reliably apply here.

## 6. Filing Deadlines and Enforcement

A mechanics' lien claimant must generally record the lien within a specific statutory period after completion of the work of improvement (or, for a subcontractor, potentially tied to when a notice of completion or cessation is recorded) — and must then file a lawsuit to **foreclose the lien** within a further statutory period after recording, or the lien expires and becomes unenforceable. <!-- VERIFY: current recording deadline and foreclosure-filing deadline periods, both of which are strict statutory limits --> These deadlines are strictly enforced; missing them can extinguish an otherwise valid claim, which is one reason construction disputes generate so much time-sensitive legal activity.

## 7. New-Home Buyer Protections

Buyers of newly constructed homes from a builder receive statutory protections distinct from resale transactions:

- **Statutory functionality/construction standards** — California's Right to Repair Act (and related statutes) establishes specific standards new residential construction must meet and creates a structured pre-litigation process requiring the builder be given notice and an opportunity to inspect and repair claimed defects before the homeowner can sue. <!-- VERIFY: current Right to Repair Act scope, standards list, and pre-litigation notice/repair procedure requirements -->
- **Builder warranties** — many builders provide structured warranty coverage (often with different terms for different systems — e.g., shorter coverage for fixtures and appliances, longer coverage for structural elements) as a matter of standard practice or contract, separate from the statutory functionality standards.
- **Disclosure obligations** parallel to resale disclosure in some respects, though the specific forms and timing differ for new construction sold directly by a builder. <!-- VERIFY: current new-construction-specific disclosure requirements, which differ from the resale TDS/NHD framework -->

### Worked Example 2 — The new-construction defect claim (Fontana)

A buyer purchases a newly built home; eighteen months later, cracks appear in the drywall consistent with a foundation issue.

**Analysis.** Before any lawsuit, the buyer's likely first step under the Right to Repair framework is a **formal notice to the builder**, giving the builder the statutory opportunity to inspect and attempt repair. This pre-litigation process is mandatory in many cases, not optional — a buyer (or their attorney) who sues first without following the required notice procedure can face real procedural obstacles. An agent who sold the home, or who represents the buyer in an unrelated later transaction, is not the one to advise on this process, but recognizing that new-construction defect claims follow a different legal path than a resale disclosure dispute is valuable working knowledge.

## 8. Unpermitted Work: A Related but Distinct Risk

Unpermitted work — construction completed without required building permits and final inspection sign-off — is a **separate risk category** from mechanics' liens, though the two often appear together in older, non-professionally-managed renovation projects. Unpermitted work can affect insurability, financeability, resale disclosure obligations, and in some cases can require an owner to obtain retroactive permits (sometimes involving costly demolition/exposure of the work for inspection) before a sale can close cleanly. Principles Unit 9's material fact disclosure duty covers known unpermitted work directly — sellers and their agents cannot simply stay silent because "it passed informally" or "the neighbor's contractor did it years ago."

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Mechanics' lien** | A statutory lien available to unpaid contractors, subcontractors, laborers, and suppliers against the improved property. |
| **Preliminary 20-day notice** | Early notice most lien claimants must serve to preserve future lien rights. |
| **Conditional release** | A lien release effective only once payment actually clears. |
| **Unconditional release** | A lien release effective immediately upon signing, regardless of later payment failure. |
| **Right to Repair Act** | California's statutory construction-standards and pre-litigation notice framework for new-home defect claims. |
| **Unpermitted work** | Construction completed without required permits and inspection sign-off, a distinct risk from a mechanics' lien. |

## 10. Exam and Practice Tips

- Paying the general contractor in full does not automatically protect an owner from a subcontractor's mechanics' lien — this is one of the most frequently tested and misunderstood points in this area.
- Use conditional releases tied to each payment stage to guard against a bounced check.
- Mechanics' lien priority can relate back to the commencement of work — sometimes beating a later-recorded but earlier-work-preceding construction loan.
- Recording and foreclosure-filing deadlines for mechanics' liens are strict; missing them extinguishes the claim.
- New-home defect claims generally require a pre-litigation notice-and-repair process under the Right to Repair Act before a lawsuit.
- Unpermitted work is a distinct risk from mechanics' liens, affecting insurability, financing, and resale disclosure obligations.
