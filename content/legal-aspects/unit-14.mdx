# Unit 14 — Real Estate Litigation, ADR & License Discipline

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** mediation from arbitration and explain why the choice matters practically.
2. **Apply** the standard purchase-agreement mediation and arbitration clauses conceptually.
3. **Explain** the DRE's investigation and disciplinary process at a high level.
4. **Identify** the range of DRE disciplinary outcomes and what triggers each severity level.
5. **Analyze** common sources of real estate litigation and how better practice habits reduce exposure.
6. **Distinguish** civil liability from license discipline and explain why an agent can face both for the same conduct.
7. **Advise** (within license scope) on documentation practices that protect an agent facing a later dispute.

## 1. Mediation vs. Arbitration: Two Different Tools

**Mediation** is a non-binding process where a neutral third party (the mediator) helps the parties negotiate their own resolution. The mediator has no power to impose an outcome — the parties retain full control, and either side can walk away without a binding result if they cannot agree.

**Arbitration** is fundamentally different: a neutral third party (the arbitrator) hears the dispute and issues a **binding decision**, much like a judge, but outside the court system. Arbitration is generally faster and more private than litigation, but it also generally waives the right to a jury trial and significantly limits the grounds for appealing an unfavorable decision — an arbitrator's factual and legal errors are, in most cases, very difficult to overturn even if a court might have decided differently.

**Why the distinction matters practically:** the standard California residential purchase agreement typically includes a **mediation provision** (often required as a prerequisite before either party can recover attorney's fees in a later dispute) and a **separate, optional arbitration provision** that both parties must specifically initial to activate. A party who does not initial the arbitration clause retains the right to sue in court if mediation does not resolve the dispute; a party who does initial it gives up that right in exchange for arbitration's speed and privacy. <!-- VERIFY: current standard-form mediation/arbitration clause structure and attorney-fee-shifting mechanics, which can be revised by the association that publishes the standard forms -->

### Worked Example 1 — Skipping mediation costs the win (Upland)

A seller sues a buyer directly in small claims court over a disputed repair credit, without first attempting mediation as required by their signed purchase agreement's attorney's-fee clause.

**Analysis.** Even if the seller ultimately prevails on the merits of the dispute itself, skipping the required mediation step can independently forfeit the seller's ability to recover attorney's fees under the contract's fee-shifting provision — a real financial consequence separate from who was actually right about the underlying repair dispute. This is exactly the kind of contract-mechanics detail that makes "read the dispute-resolution clause before doing anything" as important as understanding the underlying facts.

## 2. The DRE's Role and Investigative Process

The California Department of Real Estate (DRE) licenses and disciplines real estate salespersons and brokers, separate from any civil lawsuit a wronged party might file. A DRE investigation can be triggered by a consumer complaint, a referral from another government agency, a criminal conviction, or the DRE's own audit activity.

**Investigation, in outline:** the DRE reviews the complaint, may request records and a written response from the licensee, may conduct interviews, and determines whether grounds for disciplinary action exist. If the DRE finds cause, it can pursue an **accusation** — a formal administrative proceeding, generally heard before an administrative law judge, that can result in license discipline.

## 3. Range of Disciplinary Outcomes

DRE disciplinary outcomes range broadly by severity:

- **Public reproval** — a formal, published warning, without loss of license.
- **Suspension** — temporary loss of licensing authority for a defined period.
- **Restricted license** — continued licensure subject to specific conditions (increased supervision, restricted activities, or a probationary period).
- **Revocation** — permanent loss of licensing authority, though a revoked licensee may in some cases petition for reinstatement after a statutory waiting period, subject to DRE discretion. <!-- VERIFY: current reinstatement eligibility timelines and standards -->

**What triggers discipline** spans a wide range of severity: from technical/administrative violations (failing to properly supervise, inadequate trust fund recordkeeping) through serious ethical violations (misrepresentation, undisclosed dual agency, commingling trust funds) to outright fraud and criminal conduct. The DRE's disciplinary calculus generally weighs the severity and pattern of the conduct, the harm caused, whether it was a first offense, and whether the licensee cooperated with the investigation.

## 4. Civil Liability and License Discipline Are Separate Tracks

**A single piece of misconduct can expose an agent to both civil liability and license discipline simultaneously — these are entirely separate legal tracks with different purposes, different standards, and different consequences.**

- A **civil lawsuit** (brought by the harmed buyer, seller, or other party) seeks to compensate the injured party — damages, rescission, or other private remedies.
- **DRE discipline** is a regulatory action protecting the public generally, independent of whether the specific harmed party ever sues — the DRE can discipline a licensee even if the wronged consumer never files a civil claim, and a civil settlement does not prevent or resolve a separate DRE investigation into the same conduct.

An agent who settles a civil lawsuit confidentially, assuming "the matter is closed," may still face a DRE complaint and investigation arising from the same underlying facts — the two systems do not automatically communicate or resolve each other.

### Worked Example 2 — Two tracks, one set of facts (Ontario)

An agent fails to disclose a known, serious foundation issue to a buyer. The buyer discovers it after closing, sues, and the case settles for repair costs and a confidentiality clause. Separately, the buyer (before the settlement) had already filed a DRE complaint.

**Analysis.** The civil settlement resolves the buyer's private claim for money, but it does **not** resolve the DRE's independent investigation into whether the agent violated their disclosure duties as a licensee — the DRE can proceed with its own investigation and potential discipline regardless of the private settlement, and a confidentiality clause in the civil settlement cannot bind the DRE, which was not a party to it. An agent who believes a quiet settlement "makes the problem go away" is operating on a mistaken assumption.

## 5. Common Sources of Real Estate Litigation

Recurring patterns across the units in this course converge here — most real estate disputes trace back to a handful of recurring failure points:

- **Non-disclosure or misrepresentation** of a material fact (Principles Unit 9; Unit 3 of this course).
- **Agency and fiduciary breaches** — undisclosed dual agency, undisclosed conflicts of interest (Unit 2).
- **Contract disputes** — deposit disputes, contingency-removal disagreements, breach claims (Practice Unit 9; Unit 3 of this course).
- **Trust fund mishandling** — commingling, conversion, or recordkeeping failures (Principles Unit 18).
- **Fair housing violations** — steering, disparate treatment, discriminatory advertising (Unit 10 of this course).
- **Negligent or careless professional conduct** — failing to recommend inspections, giving inaccurate valuation opinions presented as fact, or overstepping into legal/tax advice.

## 6. Documentation: The Best Defense Available to Any Agent

Nearly every recurring source of litigation above is made dramatically less risky — not eliminated, but genuinely reduced — by disciplined documentation practices:

- Confirm material client instructions and representations **in writing**, through the brokerage's approved system, not memory or informal texts.
- Document disclosures given and when they were given.
- Keep a clean, chronological transaction file that a neutral reviewer could reconstruct the relationship and events from.
- When a conflict, ambiguity, or red flag arises, document that it was raised and how it was addressed — not just the final outcome.

This is not paperwork for its own sake. In both a civil lawsuit and a DRE investigation, the agent's contemporaneous written record is frequently the single most persuasive piece of evidence available — far more persuasive than a later recollection of "what I always tell clients."

## 7. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Mediation** | A non-binding process where a neutral third party helps parties negotiate their own resolution. |
| **Arbitration** | A binding process where a neutral third party issues a decision outside the court system, generally waiving jury trial rights. |
| **Accusation (DRE)** | The formal administrative proceeding through which the DRE pursues license discipline. |
| **Public reproval** | A formal, published DRE warning without loss of license. |
| **License revocation** | Permanent loss of licensing authority, with limited possible reinstatement. |
| **Civil liability** | Private legal responsibility to compensate a harmed party, separate from license discipline. |

## 8. Exam and Practice Tips

- Mediation is non-binding; arbitration is binding and generally waives jury trial rights and most appeal grounds — know the distinction cold.
- The standard purchase agreement's arbitration clause typically must be separately initialed to apply; mediation is often a prerequisite to recovering attorney's fees.
- DRE discipline and civil liability are separate tracks — a civil settlement does not resolve a DRE investigation into the same conduct.
- DRE disciplinary outcomes range from public reproval through suspension, restriction, to revocation, scaled to severity and pattern of conduct.
- Most real estate litigation traces back to a small set of recurring failures: non-disclosure, agency breaches, contract disputes, trust fund mishandling, and fair housing violations.
- Disciplined, contemporaneous written documentation is the single most protective habit an agent can build.
