# Unit 1 — Sources of Real Estate Law & the Court System

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

1. **Identify** the major sources of California real estate law.
2. **Distinguish** constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, ordinances, and private agreements.
3. **Explain** how federal, state, and local rules can apply to the same property issue.
4. **Describe** the basic roles of trial courts, appellate courts, and administrative agencies.
5. **Recognize** when a licensee should refer a legal issue instead of interpreting it.
6. **Read** a legal authority for its source, scope, date, and factual limits.
7. **Use** a practical legal-risk workflow in a transaction.

---

## 1. Law Is a Layered System

Real estate is regulated by layers of law. A single purchase of a condominium can involve federal fair-housing law, California statutes, DRE regulations, a city zoning ordinance, a recorded declaration, an HOA rule, a purchase agreement, and a court decision interpreting one of those sources. The sources do not all have equal authority, and they do not all answer the same question.

The first professional habit is to ask: **What kind of rule is this, who made it, and does it apply to this transaction?** That is more useful than repeating a rule you heard in a prior deal. A licensee who confuses a broker policy with a state statute, or an HOA rule with a city ordinance, can give a client an inaccurate and expensive answer.

The practical hierarchy begins with constitutional authority. The United States Constitution and California Constitution set broad limits on government action and allocate power. Statutes are laws enacted by legislatures, such as Congress or the California Legislature. Regulations are rules adopted by agencies under authority delegated by statute. Case law is the body of judicial decisions interpreting and applying legal sources to particular facts. Local governments adopt ordinances in areas delegated to them, often including zoning, building, business licensing, rent rules, and land-use procedure. Finally, private documents—deeds, leases, contracts, CC&Rs, and management agreements—create enforceable obligations among parties when valid and consistent with higher law.

Higher authority normally controls lower authority. A contract cannot authorize discrimination prohibited by law. An HOA rule cannot defeat a valid statute. A city ordinance cannot override a conflicting state rule when the state has preempted the field. But “higher controls lower” is only the start. Whether a conflict exists and whether a source applies can require legal analysis. That is why agents identify, document, and refer issues instead of making definitive legal conclusions.

### Worked Example 1 — The lease clause that cannot do everything

A landlord shows a prospective tenant a lease clause saying “No service animals.” The manager should not assume the printed clause ends the question. Federal and California disability-housing rules may require an accommodation analysis, and current law and broker procedures must guide the response. The agent or manager can say that the request will be processed through the approved accommodation procedure; they should not give a final legal ruling from the clause alone.

## 2. Statutes, Regulations, and Agencies

Statutes are enacted laws. In California real estate, students will encounter statutes in subject areas such as agency, contracts, landlord-tenant relationships, recording, consumer protection, land use, fair housing, and licensing. The California Legislature enacts statutes, and the Governor generally signs them before they become law. Statutes may direct an agency to adopt implementing regulations, define prohibited conduct, create remedies, or authorize local action.

Regulations fill in operational detail. The California Department of Real Estate (DRE), for example, has authority to administer and enforce portions of real estate licensing law and adopt regulations within delegated authority. A regulation can establish a procedure, recordkeeping expectation, advertising standard, or disciplinary framework. Regulations are not suggestions merely because they are not called “statutes.” When validly adopted under authority, they have legal force.

Agency guidance, forms, bulletins, and manuals can be useful, but students must distinguish them from binding statutes or regulations. A handbook may explain the agency’s position or procedure. It may not answer every disputed legal question, and it can be revised. Always confirm the current authority and effective date before treating guidance as a rule.

### A source-check method

When a client asks, “Is that legal?” do not begin by searching a snippet. Use this sequence:

1. identify the issue precisely—agency disclosure, rent increase, land use, accessibility, deposit, title, or something else;
2. identify the jurisdiction—federal, California, county, city, HOA, or contract;
3. identify the source type—statute, regulation, ordinance, recorded document, agreement, or court decision;
4. check whether the source is current and whether facts trigger it;
5. preserve the question and route legal interpretation to the broker, counsel, agency, title professional, or other expert.

This method is not legal research training. It is professional risk control.

## 3. Case Law: Facts Matter

Courts decide disputes by applying legal authority to facts. Their written opinions can interpret ambiguous words, identify duties, announce remedies, or explain how a rule applies to a recurring problem. In a common-law system, a published appellate decision can become precedent that lower courts consider or must follow depending on the court and jurisdiction.

Do not treat a case headline as a universal rule. First, ask which court decided it, what facts mattered, what legal question was before the court, and whether later authority changed the result. A court may decide that a broker had a duty under a particular agency relationship and specific observations. That does not mean every agent has identical facts. The lesson for a licensee is to recognize that duties often arise from context—and to avoid trying to argue precedent with a client.

### Worked Example 2 — The “my friend won that case” argument

A seller says a friend won a lawsuit after refusing a buyer’s request for repairs, so the seller can ignore an inspection issue. The agent should not validate the conclusion. Different contract language, disclosures, timing, property condition, and jurisdiction may matter. The useful response is to identify the present transaction facts, explain the need for broker/counsel guidance on a legal dispute, and keep the seller from treating an anecdote as a legal defense.

## 4. Courts and Administrative Forums

California trial courts, commonly called Superior Courts, hear many civil real estate disputes: contract claims, quiet-title actions, unlawful detainer proceedings, fraud claims, boundary disputes, and damage actions. A trial court determines facts and applies law. Appeals generally focus on claimed legal error rather than conducting a new trial.

The California Courts of Appeal review many trial-court decisions. The California Supreme Court is the state’s highest court and selects a limited number of important cases. Federal courts handle claims arising under federal law, including some civil-rights, lending, bankruptcy, and interstate matters. The federal court structure has trial courts, courts of appeals, and the United States Supreme Court.

Administrative agencies also investigate and enforce in defined areas. A licensing agency may investigate licensee conduct and pursue discipline through administrative procedures. A fair-housing agency may investigate a discrimination complaint. A local planning department may administer zoning applications. These bodies are not interchangeable with civil courts, but their work can overlap with private lawsuits.

For agents, the practical lesson is not to predict where a case will end. It is to identify when a complaint, demand letter, subpoena, regulatory inquiry, or threatened suit must be sent immediately to the responsible broker and counsel. Do not alter records, contact opposing parties directly to “clear it up,” or make admissions outside authorized guidance.

### Worked Example 3 — The DRE inquiry

A brokerage receives a request for records related to an advertisement complaint. A salesperson sees that a listing photo caption was edited after publication and worries it looks bad. The right action is to preserve the original files, notify the responsible broker, follow the brokerage response process, and provide accurate facts. Deleting the old caption or rewriting messages can create a more serious problem than the original error.

## 5. Local Law, Private Rules, and Due Diligence

Local rules often shape a client’s practical question: Can a garage be converted? Is a short-term rental allowed? What setbacks apply? Is a business license required? Are there local tenant protections? The answer can vary by city or unincorporated area and may change. Never infer a legal right from what a neighbor has done or an online listing claims.

Private rules can be equally important. CC&Rs, lease terms, easements, listing agreements, and purchase contracts may allocate rights and duties. A recorded restriction can bind property owners; a lease can govern use and maintenance; an escrow instruction can control a fund release. But private documents must be read in context and can be limited by law. An agent can identify a document, provide it, and recommend appropriate review. The agent should not promise a result such as “the HOA must allow your renovation” or “this clause is unenforceable.”

### Worked Example 4 — The short-term-rental promise

A buyer tells you they plan to rent a Palm Springs home on weekends. Before marketing the investment return or telling the buyer it is allowed, identify the city, HOA, loan, insurance, and property-specific questions. Provide objective resources, encourage specialist review, and avoid presenting projected rental income as a legal conclusion or guarantee. A neighbor’s active listing is not proof of permission.

## 6. The Licensee’s Legal-Risk Workflow

When law enters a transaction, use a calm routine:

- **Notice:** identify the legal-looking issue without minimizing it.
- **Facts:** preserve dates, documents, observations, and exact statements.
- **Boundaries:** state what you can and cannot determine in your role.
- **Referral:** bring in the responsible broker and the right qualified professional.
- **Follow-up:** track the transaction impact without taking over the specialist’s role.
- **Record:** retain material communications in the approved file.

This workflow turns “I am not a lawyer” from a dead end into professional service. It does not prevent a client from getting an answer; it makes sure the answer comes from someone with the authority and expertise to give it.

## 7. Reading Authority Without Overreading It

Legal sources reward careful reading. Start with the date. A statute may have been amended, an ordinance may have an effective date, an agency form may have been replaced, and an appellate decision may have been superseded or limited. Then read the defined terms. A rule that applies to a “broker,” “owner,” “dwelling,” “consumer,” or “residential property” may use a definition different from everyday speech. Finally, read the condition words: “shall,” “may,” “unless,” “except,” “subject to,” and “within” often determine the actual scope of the rule.

Do not pull one sentence out of a longer authority and treat it as the answer. A section may contain exceptions, cross-references, procedure, notice requirements, remedies, or special definitions. If the answer matters to a client decision, the agent should preserve the source and question, then bring the matter to the broker or qualified advisor. You are not expected to litigate the issue; you are expected to recognize that the issue is not safely resolved by a snippet.

### Worked Example 5 — The viral rent-rule post

An owner forwards a viral post claiming that “California landlords can never deny any applicant because of credit.” The post does not identify an effective date, jurisdiction, statute, or exception. The manager should not adopt a new screening rule or send a resident a definitive answer. Identify the claimed issue, preserve the source, check current broker-approved materials, and ask the broker or counsel for guidance. The correct operational response to unreliable legal content is verification, not argument.

### Authority versus evidence

It also helps to distinguish a legal authority from evidence. A recorded deed, email, photograph, inspection report, payment receipt, or witness statement may prove a fact. It does not automatically state the law. Conversely, a statute may state a rule without proving that the facts trigger it. In a dispute, both questions matter: what rule applies, and what evidence supports the relevant facts? Agents should preserve available evidence and avoid changing it. They should not decide which side has “won” a legal question.

This distinction is especially useful in disclosure and agency disputes. A client may produce a screenshot and say it proves fraud. The screenshot may be relevant evidence, but it does not by itself establish the legal conclusion. A licensee can document the report, protect the file, notify the broker, and help the client reach qualified assistance. Accurate process is not the same as legal judgment.

### Why local verification matters

Real estate is intensely local. A statewide rule may coexist with a local ordinance, administrative procedure, or property-specific recorded restriction. A state license does not make an agent an expert in every city’s short-term rental rules, rent controls, grading requirements, historic-district rules, or permit process. The proper professional posture is curious and precise: identify the jurisdiction, direct the consumer to the authoritative local department or qualified professional, and never advertise a use as permitted without verification.

This restraint is not a lack of expertise. It is the practical expression of professional competence. The agent adds value by seeing the issue early, gathering the right documents, preventing a careless promise, and keeping the client’s decision timeline moving while the qualified person answers the legal question. A client can act confidently only when the source, facts, and authority have been matched correctly.

In every unit that follows, apply this same sequence: identify the source, identify the facts, verify currency, recognize the limit of the licensee role, and document the referral or next step.

That disciplined habit supports accurate teaching, safer client service, and a transaction file capable of answering questions long after the closing date.

## 8. Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Constitution** | Foundational legal document that allocates government power and protects rights. |
| **Statute** | Law enacted by a legislature. |
| **Regulation** | Rule adopted by an authorized administrative agency. |
| **Ordinance** | Local law enacted by a city or county. |
| **Case law** | Law developed through judicial decisions. |
| **Precedent** | Earlier judicial decision used as authority in later cases. |
| **Jurisdiction** | A court or government body's authority over a subject, person, or geographic area. |
| **Preemption** | Higher law displacing conflicting or inconsistent lower-level regulation. |

## 9. Exam and Practice Tips

- Statutes are enacted by legislatures; regulations are adopted by agencies under delegated authority.
- A contract or HOA rule cannot authorize conduct prohibited by higher law.
- A case’s facts, court, and date matter; headlines are not legal analysis.
- Trial courts hear facts; appellate courts review claimed legal error.
- Identify local rules and private documents, but route legal interpretation to qualified professionals.
- Preserve records and notify the broker immediately if a regulatory inquiry or legal complaint arises.
- Current authority always beats an old class note or online summary.
