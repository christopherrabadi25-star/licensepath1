# Unit 15 — Fair Housing & Ethics

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **List** the federal protected classes under the Fair Housing Act and **identify** California's additional protected classes.
2. **Define and recognize** steering, blockbusting, redlining, and discriminatory advertising in realistic scenarios.
3. **Explain** disability protections, including reasonable accommodations, reasonable modifications, and service and assistance animals.
4. **Describe** the Unruh Civil Rights Act, FEHA, and the Housing Financial Discrimination Act.
5. **Apply** fair housing rules to advertising language and marketing practice.
6. **Explain** implicit bias and how it produces discriminatory outcomes without discriminatory intent.
7. **Identify** enforcement paths, remedies, and license consequences.
8. **Distinguish** legal duties from ethical obligations and apply an ethical framework to gray-area decisions.

## 1. Why This Unit Is Different

Everything else in this course is about competence. This unit is about that too — but it is also the area where a single careless sentence ends careers, and where the industry's own history is the reason the rules exist.

Real estate professionals were central to housing segregation in America. Racially restrictive covenants (Unit 5) were drafted and enforced by the industry. Redlining maps guided lending for decades. Trade association codes once *required* agents to avoid introducing "members of any race or nationality" whose presence would be "detrimental to property values." Those provisions are gone, but the neighborhoods and wealth gaps they built are still here, visible in any Southern California map of home values by census tract.

You are not responsible for that history. You are responsible for not extending it. That is the frame for this entire unit.

## 2. Federal Fair Housing Law

The **Fair Housing Act** — Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, as amended — prohibits discrimination in residential housing transactions based on **seven federal protected classes**:

1. **Race**
2. **Color**
3. **Religion**
4. **National origin**
5. **Sex** (interpreted to include sexual orientation and gender identity) <!-- VERIFY: current HUD interpretation and rulemaking -->
6. **Disability** (added 1988)
7. **Familial status** (added 1988)

A common mnemonic is **"FRESH CoN"** — Familial status, Race, sEx, Sexual orientation/gender identity, Handicap (disability), Color, Origin (national), and religioN. Use whatever mnemonic sticks; know the seven.

**Prohibited conduct** includes refusing to sell or rent, offering different terms or conditions, discriminatory advertising, falsely representing that housing is unavailable, steering, blockbusting, denying access to an MLS or other service, coercion or retaliation against someone exercising fair housing rights, and discrimination in lending or appraisal.

**Familial status** protects households with children under 18, pregnant persons, and those securing legal custody. The narrow exception is **qualified housing for older persons** meeting strict statutory criteria (commonly 62+, or 55+ with at least 80% of units having a 55+ occupant plus published policies demonstrating intent). <!-- VERIFY: current HOPA criteria --> A community cannot simply declare itself "adults only."

**Disability** protections are the most operationally detailed and are covered in Section 4.

**Federal exemptions** exist but are narrow, and — this matters enormously in California — they are largely **swallowed by state law**. The classic federal exemptions cover certain single-family homes sold or rented by an owner without a broker and without discriminatory advertising, and owner-occupied buildings with four or fewer units. **The exemptions never apply when a real estate licensee is involved, and never permit discriminatory advertising.** Because California's Unruh Act and FEHA reach more broadly, treat every transaction you touch as fully covered.

## 3. California Fair Housing Law

California protects everything federal law protects, **plus substantially more.**

**FEHA (the Fair Employment and Housing Act)** is the state's primary housing discrimination statute. California's protected characteristics include, in addition to the federal seven:

- **Marital status**
- **Ancestry**
- **Source of income** — including housing vouchers, a significant California protection
- **Sexual orientation**, **gender identity**, and **gender expression**
- **Genetic information**
- **Citizenship and immigration status**
- **Primary language**
- **Military or veteran status**
- **Age**
- Any arbitrary characteristic <!-- VERIFY: current complete FEHA list, which is amended frequently -->

The **source of income** protection deserves emphasis because agents and landlords violate it constantly. In California, a landlord generally may not refuse to rent because the applicant will pay with a Section 8 voucher or other public assistance. "We don't take vouchers" is not a policy — it is a violation. Landlords may still apply legitimate, uniformly applied financial criteria, but they must count the voucher as income.

**The Unruh Civil Rights Act** prohibits discrimination by **all business establishments** in California — which includes brokerages, property management companies, and landlords. Its coverage is broad and its list of protected characteristics is not exhaustive; courts have extended it to arbitrary discrimination generally.

**The Housing Financial Discrimination Act (Holden Act)** prohibits discrimination in **mortgage lending** based on the characteristics of the borrower *or* the **neighborhood** — the direct statutory attack on redlining.

**Commissioner's Regulations** separately prohibit discriminatory conduct by licensees and make it grounds for discipline. This means a fair housing violation exposes you to **three separate consequences**: civil liability, administrative fair housing enforcement, and DRE license discipline.

## 4. Disability: Accommodations, Modifications, and Animals

Disability rules require operational knowledge, not just awareness.

**Definition.** A physical or mental impairment substantially limiting one or more major life activities, a record of such an impairment, or being regarded as having one.

**Reasonable accommodation** — a change in **rules, policies, practices, or services** so a person with a disability has equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling. The **housing provider bears the cost**, because a policy change costs nothing to implement.

*Examples:* waiving a no-pets policy for an assistance animal; assigning an accessible parking space; permitting a live-in aide; accepting a co-signer where policy normally forbids one.

**Reasonable modification** — a **physical change** to the premises. Generally the **tenant pays**, and the landlord may require restoration on move-out where reasonable (interior changes; a landlord typically cannot require removal of a grab bar, but may for a wall removed).

*Examples:* installing grab bars, widening a doorway, building a ramp.

**Memory hook:** *Accommodation = policy, provider pays. Modification = physical, tenant pays.*

**Assistance animals** are the single most common flashpoint.

- A service animal or emotional support animal is **not a pet**. No-pet policies, pet rent, and pet deposits **do not apply**.
- A housing provider may ask for **documentation of disability-related need** when the disability is not obvious, but may **not** ask about the nature or severity of the disability, demand medical records, or require specific certification or "registration." Online "registries" selling certificates have no legal force, and California has legislated against fraudulent assistance-animal documentation. <!-- VERIFY: current documentation standards and California statutes -->
- Breed and weight restrictions generally do not apply to assistance animals.
- The tenant remains responsible for **actual damage** the animal causes.

**Design and construction.** Covered multifamily housing built for first occupancy after **March 13, 1991** must meet federal accessibility requirements. California's building standards impose additional requirements. <!-- VERIFY: current accessibility standards -->

## 5. The Named Practices

**Steering** — directing prospects toward or away from particular neighborhoods, buildings, or units based on a protected characteristic. Steering is usually *not* malicious; it typically arrives dressed as helpfulness. "You'd be more comfortable over here," "that neighborhood has a great Persian community," "let me show you where families like yours are buying" — all steering.

**The safe practice:** let clients define their own search criteria. Provide objective, factual information and neutral resources. Never characterize a neighborhood's demographics, and never answer "is this a good area?" with anything other than a redirect to objective data sources — crime statistics, school ratings from published sources, census data — that the client can evaluate themselves.

**Blockbusting (panic selling)** — inducing owners to sell by representing that members of a protected class are entering the neighborhood, with the implication that values will decline. Illegal regardless of whether the statements are true.

**Redlining** — refusing to lend, insure, or provide services in an area based on its demographic composition rather than the applicant's or property's objective qualifications. Prohibited federally and by the Holden Act.

**Discriminatory advertising** — any statement indicating a preference, limitation, or discrimination. This is where agents create liability without any intent, because the rules govern the *listing copy*, not the writer's heart.

**Advertising: describe the property, never the people.**

| Avoid | Why | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Perfect for a young couple" | Familial status, age | "Two-bedroom condominium" |
| "Walking distance to St. Anne's" | Religion | "Near public transit and schools" |
| "Ideal for professionals" | Familial status, source of income | "Home office and fiber internet" |
| "Safe, family neighborhood" | Familial status; also a risky representation | "Cul-de-sac location" |
| "No Section 8" | Source of income (California) | Omit entirely |
| "Master bedroom" | Increasingly avoided industry-wide | "Primary bedroom" |
| "Must speak English" | National origin, primary language | Omit entirely |
| "Adult community" | Familial status unless HOPA-qualified | State the qualifying senior status precisely |

Two further rules: **use the Equal Housing Opportunity logo** in advertising as required, and remember that your **social media is advertising**. A post saying "great starter home for a young family!" is a fair housing violation on Instagram exactly as it is in the MLS.

## 6. Implicit Bias

**Implicit bias** is unconscious association that influences judgment without conscious intent. It matters because fair housing law increasingly examines **outcomes**, not just intentions — and because most modern discrimination is not deliberate.

California requires implicit bias training for licensee renewal, and the Real Estate Practice course must include an implicit bias component with interactive elements (SB 263). <!-- VERIFY: current SB 263 hour and interactivity specifications --> Course 2 Unit 3 develops this at length with participatory exercises; here is the foundation.

**Where implicit bias shows up in practice:**

- **Differential service.** Studies of paired testing have repeatedly found that testers of different races, presenting identical financial qualifications, are shown different numbers of homes and different neighborhoods. Almost none of those agents believed they were discriminating.
- **Assumptions about qualification.** Deciding who "looks like a serious buyer" and adjusting your effort accordingly.
- **Assumptions about preference.** Deciding where someone will "feel comfortable" — the engine of steering.
- **Appraisal and valuation.** Documented disparities in valuations of homes in majority-Black and Latino neighborhoods have prompted federal attention and enforcement.
- **Language and accent** shaping how promptly you return a call.

**Practical countermeasures** — and these are behavioral, not attitudinal:

1. **Standardize your process.** Use the same intake questionnaire, the same buyer consultation, the same follow-up cadence with every client. Consistency defeats bias more effectively than good intentions.
2. **Let clients set criteria.** Ask what *they* want; never infer it.
3. **Use objective data** and point clients to public sources rather than offering neighborhood characterizations.
4. **Audit yourself periodically.** Look at your own client list, showing counts, and response times. Patterns you did not intend will be visible in the data.
5. **Slow down on judgment calls.** Bias operates fastest under time pressure.

## 7. Enforcement, Remedies, and Ethics

**Enforcement paths.** A person may file with **HUD**, with the **California Civil Rights Department** <!-- VERIFY: current department name -->, or file suit directly in state or federal court. HUD complaints generally must be filed within one year of the discriminatory act; court actions carry their own limitation periods. <!-- VERIFY: current filing deadlines -->

**Remedies** include actual damages, injunctive relief, civil penalties, punitive damages, and attorney's fees. Under the Unruh Act, statutory minimum damages are available per violation. Separately, the **DRE may suspend or revoke** a license — and because the DRE proceeding is independent, settling a civil case does not end your exposure.

**Testers** — individuals who pose as prospects to detect discrimination — have standing to sue, and evidence they gather is admissible. Treat every inquiry as though it may be a test, which is simply another way of saying treat every inquiry identically.

### Ethics beyond the law

Law sets the floor. Professional practice asks more.

The **NAR Code of Ethics** binds Realtor® members (Unit 1) and organizes duties toward clients, the public, and other Realtors®. Non-members are not bound by it but are held to the same legal standards.

For gray-area decisions, a workable framework:

1. **Is it legal?** If no, stop.
2. **Is it honest?** Would the other party feel deceived if they learned everything I know?
3. **Is it fair?** Am I treating comparable people comparably?
4. **Is it transparent?** Would I be comfortable if this appeared in the transaction file, or in a news story?
5. **Whose interest does it serve?** If the answer is mine at my client's expense, it is a fiduciary breach (Unit 6).

The honest reality: most agents who lose their licenses over fair housing did not think of themselves as discriminating. They were being helpful, or accommodating a client's stated preference, or repeating language they had heard for years. Vigilance about your own defaults is the whole job.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Helpful steering (San Gabriel Valley)

A buyer relocating from out of state mentions she is Muslim and asks where she should look. Wanting to help, you say: "There's a wonderful community around the mosque in Rowland Heights — I think you'd feel really at home there," and you focus your showings there.

**Analysis.** This is **steering**, notwithstanding entirely good intentions. You directed a buyer toward a neighborhood based on religion and narrowed her options accordingly. The violation is complete regardless of whether she would have liked the area.

**What to do instead:** "I'd rather not steer you toward or away from any neighborhood — that's actually something fair housing law requires of me. Tell me your criteria: price, commute, size, schools, and any specific amenities you want nearby, and I'll show you everything that matches. If there are particular institutions or amenities important to you, you can search for them directly and I'll show you homes anywhere you'd like."

Notice this answer is *better service*, not just compliance. It surfaces her actual criteria instead of your guesses.

### Example 2 — The seller's instruction (Pasadena)

A seller instructs: "I want it to go to a nice family, not investors — and honestly, I'd rather not sell to people who don't speak English. It's my house, I get to choose."

**Analysis.** Two separate things here.

"Not investors" is generally **lawful** — investor status is not a protected characteristic, and sellers may prefer owner-occupant buyers.

The rest is **unlawful**. Screening by primary language and national origin violates federal and California law, and preferring a "family" implicates familial status. The seller's ownership does not create an exemption — and even if a federal exemption theoretically applied, it evaporates the moment a licensee is involved.

**Your response:** explain plainly that you cannot follow that instruction, that it would expose you both to liability, and that you are legally required to present all offers without regard to those characteristics. If the seller insists, **withdraw from the listing.** Obedience covers only lawful instructions (Unit 6), and no commission is worth your license.

### Example 3 — The assistance animal (Ontario rental)

Your property management client has a strict no-pets policy. An applicant with excellent credit discloses she has an emotional support dog — a 70-pound breed the owner's insurance policy "discourages." The owner says: "Tell her no pets, and if she pushes, ask for her medical records."

**Analysis.** Three violations lined up.

1. An assistance animal is **not a pet**; the no-pets policy does not apply, and waiving it is a **reasonable accommodation** the provider must grant.
2. **Breed and weight restrictions** generally do not apply to assistance animals. Insurance concerns do not override the accommodation obligation, though the owner may explore carriers.
3. Demanding **medical records** is prohibited. If the disability is not obvious, the provider may request **documentation of disability-related need** — not diagnosis, not records, not severity.

Also note: **no pet rent and no pet deposit.** The tenant remains liable for actual damage the animal causes, which is the owner's real protection.

### Example 4 — Advertising copy audit (listing)

Draft copy: *"Charming starter home in a quiet, safe family neighborhood. Walking distance to St. Rita's. Perfect for a young couple or first-time buyers. Master suite upstairs. No Section 8."*

**Line-by-line:**

- **"starter home"** — borderline; implies buyer profile. Describe the home instead.
- **"safe"** — a risky factual representation *and* often coded language. Remove.
- **"family neighborhood"** — familial status. Remove.
- **"Walking distance to St. Rita's"** — religion. Naming a nearby school as a landmark is a genuine gray area; the safer construction is a neutral proximity reference.
- **"Perfect for a young couple"** — familial status, age, marital status. Remove.
- **"Master suite"** — increasingly replaced industry-wide with "primary suite."
- **"No Section 8"** — **source of income discrimination in California.** This one is a clear violation.

**Rewritten:** *"Three-bedroom, two-bath home on a quiet cul-de-sac. Updated kitchen with quartz counters, primary suite upstairs with walk-in closet, detached two-car garage, and a covered patio. Close to parks, shopping, and transit. Equal Housing Opportunity."*

The rewrite is also simply **better copy** — it describes what a buyer is purchasing rather than who the writer imagines buying it.

### Example 5 — The pattern you didn't intend (self-audit)

An agent reviews her past year: 34 buyer clients. Comparing her CRM notes, clients with Anglo surnames averaged 11 showings before writing an offer; clients with Latino and Chinese surnames averaged 6. Her average response time to inbound inquiries was 41 minutes for the first group and just under 3 hours for the second.

**Analysis.** She did not decide to provide different service, and she would sincerely deny discriminating. But **the outcome is differential service correlated with national origin**, which is exactly what fair housing enforcement examines — and what paired testing is designed to reveal.

**What to do:** this is why you audit. Standardize the intake process, set a uniform response-time standard (all inquiries within one hour), use the same buyer consultation with everyone, and re-measure in six months. Fixing a pattern you found yourself is vastly better than having HUD find it for you.

| **Fair Housing Act** | The federal law prohibiting housing discrimination based on seven protected classes. |

| **Familial status** | Protection for households with children under 18, pregnant persons, and those securing custody. |
| **Housing for older persons (HOPA)** | The narrow exception permitting age-restricted communities meeting strict statutory criteria. |
| **FEHA** | California's Fair Employment and Housing Act, protecting additional characteristics including source of income, marital status, and immigration status. |
| **Unruh Civil Rights Act** | California law prohibiting discrimination by all business establishments, including brokerages and landlords. |
| **Holden Act** | California's Housing Financial Discrimination Act, prohibiting lending discrimination based on borrower or neighborhood characteristics. |

| **Steering** | Directing prospects toward or away from areas based on a protected characteristic; usually well-intentioned and always illegal. |
| **Blockbusting** | Inducing sales by representing that members of a protected class are moving into a neighborhood. |
| **Redlining** | Denying loans, insurance, or services in an area based on its demographics rather than objective qualifications. |
| **Discriminatory advertising** | Any statement indicating a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on a protected characteristic. |
| **Reasonable accommodation** | A change in rules, policies, or services for a person with a disability; the housing provider bears the cost. |
| **Reasonable modification** | A physical change to the premises for a person with a disability; generally at the tenant's expense. |

## 10. Exam Tips

- **Memorize the seven federal classes**, then remember California adds many more — **source of income, marital status, ancestry, immigration status, primary language, age, veteran status, gender identity and expression, genetic information.**
- **Accommodation = policy change, provider pays. Modification = physical change, tenant pays.** A guaranteed question.
- **Assistance animals are never pets.** No pet rent, no deposit, no breed or weight limits, and no demanding medical records — only documentation of disability-related need when the disability is not obvious.
- **Steering questions describe helpful behavior.** If an agent suggests where someone would "feel comfortable" or "fit in," that is the violation.
- **"No Section 8" is source of income discrimination in California.** Expect it as an answer choice.
- **Federal exemptions never apply when a licensee is involved** and never permit discriminatory advertising.
- **Describe the property, not the people.** Any advertising answer referencing buyer characteristics is wrong.
- **Familial status = children under 18**, with the narrow HOPA senior housing exception. "Adults only" is not a lawful category.
- **Three consequences stack:** civil liability, agency enforcement, and DRE discipline.
- **A seller's unlawful instruction must be refused**, and if the seller persists, withdraw.
