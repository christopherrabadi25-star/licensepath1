# Unit 4 — Real Property Interests & Conveyancing

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** the legal requirements for a valid deed from the practical/customary elements agents expect to see.
2. **Compare** grant, quitclaim, warranty, and trust deeds and identify what each does and does not warrant.
3. **Apply** the requirements for valid execution and delivery of a deed.
4. **Explain** why recording is not what transfers title, and what recording actually accomplishes.
5. **Analyze** legal descriptions and identify red flags in ambiguous or inconsistent descriptions.
6. **Evaluate** methods of involuntary transfer and their effect on title.
7. **Advise** (within license scope) on why vesting choice has legal consequences beyond the sale itself.

---

## 1. Conveyancing Is a Legal Act, Not a Formality

A **conveyance** is the transfer of an interest in real property from one party to another. Agents handle conveyancing documents constantly, which can create a false sense that it is purely administrative. It is not — a deed is a legal instrument, and small defects in it can cloud title for years.

## 2. What Makes a Deed Legally Valid

California law requires specific elements for a deed to be valid:

1. **In writing** — real property conveyances must satisfy the Statute of Frauds.
2. **A competent grantor** — of legal age and sound mind.
3. **An identifiable grantee** — named with reasonable certainty.
4. **A granting/operative clause** — words evidencing present intent to convey (not a future promise to convey).
5. **An adequate legal description** of the property.
6. **Signed by the grantor** (not required to be signed by the grantee).
7. **Delivery and acceptance** — the deed must be delivered by the grantor with present intent to pass title, and accepted by the grantee.

Notice what is *not* required: **consideration is not required** for a deed to be valid (a gift deed is valid), and **recording is not required** for a deed to be valid between the parties.

## 3. Delivery: The Element Agents Most Often Misunderstand

**Delivery** requires the grantor's *present intent* to immediately transfer an interest — physical handover of the paper is evidence of delivery, but is neither necessary nor sufficient by itself. A grantor can retain physical possession of a signed deed and still have legally delivered it if the facts show present intent to convey; conversely, a grantor can hand over a signed deed and there may be no valid delivery if the grantor's intent was that title pass only in the future or upon a condition.

**A deed signed but held in the grantor's own desk drawer with an intent that it take effect only upon the grantor's death is not a valid present conveyance** — that is an attempted testamentary transfer, which must instead follow the requirements for a will. This is a recurring source of family disputes: a parent signs a deed "to be given to my daughter when I die" and keeps it in a drawer. Because there was no present intent to convey and no delivery during the grantor's lifetime, the deed is very likely invalid, and the property passes instead through the parent's estate — often not to the intended daughter alone.

**Escrow delivery** is the common commercial pattern: a grantor delivers a signed deed to a neutral third party (escrow) with instructions to deliver it to the grantee upon satisfaction of specified conditions. This is valid delivery once the conditions are met, and title relates back to the date of the escrow delivery in some circumstances — a doctrine called the **relation-back doctrine**, often relevant when a grantor dies or becomes incompetent between escrow deposit and closing.

### Worked Example 1 — The deed in the drawer (Pomona)

A widowed grandfather signs a grant deed conveying his Pomona duplex "to my grandson," tells no one, and keeps the signed deed in a locked box. He dies two years later; his will leaves his estate equally to his three children. The grandson finds the deed and claims the duplex.

**Analysis.** Without evidence of present intent to convey during the grandfather's lifetime and without delivery, this deed almost certainly failed as a conveyance. The duplex passes through the estate under the will, not to the grandson under the deed. This is exactly the fact pattern licensees should recognize and refer to an attorney and estate counsel — not attempt to resolve informally within the family.

## 4. Types of Deeds and What They Warrant

| Deed type | What it conveys/warrants |
|---|---|
| **Grant deed** | The most common California residential deed. Carries two implied warranties: the grantor has not already conveyed the property to someone else, and the property is free of undisclosed encumbrances made by the grantor — but does **not** warrant against defects arising before the grantor's ownership. |
| **Quitclaim deed** | Conveys whatever interest the grantor has, if any — with **no warranties at all**. Commonly used to clear a cloud on title (e.g., removing an ex-spouse's potential interest) rather than to sell property outright. |
| **Warranty deed** | Rare in California residential practice (more common in other states); the grantor warrants clear title against all claims, including those predating the grantor's ownership. |
| **Trust deed (deed of trust)** | Not a conveyance of ownership — it is a **security instrument** (Principles Unit 11) conveying bare legal title to a trustee as security for a debt, to be reconveyed upon payoff. |
| **Trustee's deed** | Conveys property sold at a non-judicial foreclosure trustee's sale. |
| **Reconveyance deed** | Releases the trust deed's lien once the underlying debt is paid — conveyed by the trustee back toward the trustor. |

### Worked Example 2 — Quitclaim to clear title (San Bernardino)

A couple divorces; the family home was held as community property. As part of the settlement, the ex-wife receives the home. To remove any cloud created by the ex-husband's prior community-property interest, the ex-husband signs a **quitclaim deed** conveying whatever interest he has to the ex-wife.

**Analysis.** A quitclaim is the right tool here — it is not a sale, there is no warranty needed or expected, and the goal is simply to extinguish a specific person's potential claim on title. Using a grant deed instead would not be wrong, but a quitclaim is the customary and simplest instrument for this purpose because it makes no affirmative warranty the grantor cannot back up.

## 5. Recording: What It Does (and Does Not) Do

**Recording does not transfer title.** Title transfers upon valid execution and delivery. Recording accomplishes something different and equally important: it gives **constructive notice** to the world of the interest recorded, and it establishes **priority** among competing claims under California's recording statutes.

**Priority generally follows a "first in time, first in right" principle**, modified by the recording acts: a subsequent purchaser or lienholder who records first, without actual or constructive notice of a prior unrecorded interest, and who pays valuable consideration, can gain priority over an earlier interest that was never recorded. This is why title companies exist and why a buyer's lender insists on a recorded first-priority deed of trust — an unrecorded interest is invisible to later buyers and lenders acting in good faith.

**Constructive notice** means the law treats a recorded document as if everyone knew about it, whether or not they actually looked. **Actual notice** means someone genuinely knew, regardless of recording. Either type of notice can defeat a later claim of "I didn't know."

### Worked Example 3 — The race to record (Fontana)

A financially distressed homeowner sells the same property to two different buyers within the same week, in an attempted fraud. Buyer A's grant deed is delivered first but not recorded for two weeks. Buyer B, with no knowledge of Buyer A's purchase, closes days later and records immediately.

**Analysis.** Under California's recording framework, Buyer B — who paid value and recorded first without notice of Buyer A's unrecorded interest — is likely to prevail in a priority dispute over Buyer A, despite Buyer A's earlier delivery date. Buyer A's remedy is against the fraudulent seller, not necessarily the property itself. This example is exactly why title companies run a full title search and issue title insurance, and why "record it right away" is not bureaucratic caution — it is the mechanism that protects the buyer's priority.

## 6. Legal Descriptions

A valid deed needs a legal description sufficient to identify the property with reasonable certainty — a street address alone is generally not treated as a sufficient legal description for a deed. Common methods:

- **Metes and bounds** — a description by directions, distances, and monuments starting and ending at a fixed point of beginning; common for irregular parcels.
- **Government (rectangular) survey system** — describes land by township, range, and section relative to base lines and meridians; more common in areas surveyed under the federal system.
- **Lot, block, and tract (recorded map/plat)** — references a recorded subdivision map; the most common method for residential lots in developed California subdivisions.

**Ambiguous or inconsistent legal descriptions are a title problem, not a paperwork nuisance.** A description that closes on itself incorrectly, references a superseded map, or conflicts with the assessor's parcel number can create a title exception that must be resolved (often by a licensed surveyor or a quiet title action) before a sale can close cleanly.

## 7. Involuntary Transfer

Not all transfers are voluntary conveyances by deed. Property can transfer by:

- **Intestate succession** — when a person dies without a valid will, state law dictates distribution.
- **Devise** — transfer of real property under a valid will.
- **Adverse possession** — acquiring title through open, notorious, hostile, continuous possession under claim of right for the statutory period, combined with payment of property taxes for that period (Principles Unit 5).
- **Eminent domain** — government taking for public use with just compensation (Principles Unit 14).
- **Execution sale** — court-ordered sale to satisfy a judgment lien.
- **Foreclosure sale** — transfer resulting from default on a secured debt (Principles Unit 11).

Each of these produces a conveyance without the grantor's voluntary participation in a traditional sale, and each has its own procedural requirements that must be satisfied for the resulting title to be valid.

## 8. Vesting Has Legal Consequences Beyond the Sale

Principles Unit 4 covered how title can be held. This unit's point is narrower: **the vesting choice made at closing has consequences the buyer often does not think about until much later** — how the property passes on death, how it can be encumbered or sold by one owner without the other's consent, how creditors of one owner can reach it, and how it is taxed. Licensees cannot give legal or tax advice on which vesting to choose, but a competent agent flags that the choice matters and refers the client to an attorney or CPA before closing — not after, when the vesting is far harder to unwind.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Conveyance** | The transfer of an interest in real property. |
| **Grant deed** | The standard California deed carrying two implied warranties, but not a full warranty against defects predating the grantor. |
| **Quitclaim deed** | Conveys whatever interest the grantor has, with no warranties; commonly used to clear title. |
| **Delivery** | The grantor's present intent to immediately convey, required (with acceptance) for a valid deed. |
| **Relation-back doctrine** | Treats delivery through escrow as effective as of the date of deposit under certain conditions. |
| **Constructive notice** | Legal notice imputed by a recorded document, regardless of actual knowledge. |
| **Actual notice** | Genuine, real knowledge of a fact or interest. |
| **Recording** | Public filing that establishes priority and constructive notice; does not itself transfer title. |
| **Metes and bounds** | Legal description by directions, distances, and monuments from a point of beginning. |
| **Intestate succession** | Distribution of a decedent's property under state law when there is no valid will. |

## 10. Exam and Practice Tips

- Deeds do not require consideration or recording to be valid between the parties — they require writing, a competent grantor, an identifiable grantee, a granting clause, a legal description, the grantor's signature, and delivery/acceptance.
- Recording establishes priority and constructive notice; it does not transfer title.
- A grant deed's implied warranties do not cover defects that predate the grantor's ownership.
- A quitclaim deed makes no warranties at all — know when it is the right tool (clearing title) versus the wrong one (an arm's-length sale).
- "Delivery" is about intent, not physical handover — the deed-in-the-drawer trap is a recurring exam concept.
- A street address alone is not a sufficient legal description for a deed.
