# Unit 13 — Escrow, Title & Closing Procedures

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

1. **Describe** the distinct roles of the agent, escrow holder, title professional, lender, and client.
2. **Read** a preliminary title report as a transaction-planning document without giving title or legal advice.
3. **Track** the major escrow milestones and communicate factual status clearly.
4. **Recognize** title, vesting, payoff, disclosure, and closing red flags.
5. **Coordinate** signing and possession details without promising a closing outcome.
6. **Explain** the difference between closing, recording, funding, and possession.
7. **Preserve** a post-close file that can be understood later.

---

## 1. Closing Is a Coordinated Process, Not a Single Moment

Clients often say, “We close on Friday,” as though one person presses a button at noon. In reality, a California escrow combines contract performance, lender conditions, title review, disclosures, payoff demands, signing, funding, recording, and delivery of possession. Several people do different jobs. The agent adds real value by making the sequence understandable, tracking contract deadlines, and sending the right issue to the right professional. The agent creates risk by giving legal interpretations, title assurances, lender guarantees, or unsupported promises about timing.

Use role clarity from the beginning:

| Participant | Core function |
|---|---|
| **Buyer and seller** | Make decisions, provide information, sign authorized documents, and perform contractual obligations. |
| **Escrow holder** | Holds documents/funds and follows mutually authorized escrow instructions and applicable requirements. |
| **Title professional** | Searches title records and issues title products subject to stated requirements, exceptions, and terms. |
| **Lender** | Evaluates the loan file, issues conditions, funds under its requirements, and provides loan disclosures. |
| **Real estate agent** | Coordinates the transaction, communicates facts, protects the client’s interests within agency duties, and escalates specialized issues. |

None of those roles replaces the others. A title officer is not the buyer’s attorney. A lender’s approval does not mean the agent can assure that the property will record. An agent’s calendar reminder does not change a contractual deadline.

### Worked Example 1 — “Are we definitely closing tomorrow?”

On Thursday afternoon, the buyer calls: “My lender said documents are ready. Are we definitely closing tomorrow?” The agent should not promise yes. A helpful answer is: “Your lender is reporting that loan documents are ready. We still need escrow to confirm the remaining signing, funding, and recording steps. I’ll confirm the factual status with escrow and keep you updated.”

That answer is not evasive. It recognizes that a loan document package, a lender’s funding decision, a county recording, and the contract’s closing date are related but separate facts.

## 2. Escrow Instructions: Authority Lives in the Documents

Escrow is built on instructions. The holder should not act simply because one party calls urgently, an agent believes an outcome is fair, or a text message appears persuasive. In a typical purchase, the contract and later signed escrow documents guide the holder. If instructions conflict, a signature is missing, or the parties dispute a release, the solution is not an agent’s interpretation. The issue should be escalated to the broker, escrow, and—when appropriate—the parties’ legal counsel.

Agents should be careful with verbs. “I will ask escrow whether that document is still needed” is appropriate. “Escrow must release your deposit” is a legal conclusion and may be wrong. “The seller has breached” can be equally risky without authorized legal advice.

### The milestone board

A high-quality transaction file has an understandable milestone board:

1. executed agreement delivered to escrow;
2. escrow opened and contact information confirmed;
3. deposit receipt or verified status recorded;
4. disclosures and reports delivered and tracked;
5. title report received and routed for review;
6. inspections, appraisal, loan, and other contract contingencies tracked;
7. loan and title conditions communicated to the responsible parties;
8. signing, funding, recording, and possession details confirmed;
9. closing documents and post-close records archived.

This list is a communication system, not a substitute for the contract. Use actual dates and names, not vague labels like “title done.”

## 3. Preliminary Title Reports: Read, Route, Do Not Diagnose

A preliminary title report is often misunderstood as a guarantee that title is perfect. It is better understood as a report that identifies the proposed insured, the property, the estate or interest, requirements to be satisfied before a policy may issue, and exceptions or matters affecting title. The report gives the transaction team a chance to ask questions early.

Common items a student should recognize include deeds of trust, recorded easements, CC&Rs, liens, tax matters, probate or trust issues, judgments, leases, boundary or survey matters, and vesting names that do not match the contract. Recognition is not legal interpretation. You can say, “The report lists an easement; title or your attorney can explain its legal effect.” You should not say, “That easement will never affect your use of the yard.”

### A disciplined title-review workflow

**Confirm identity.** Does the legal description and property address match the transaction? Does the proposed insured party reflect the buyer’s intended vesting?

**Identify requirements and exceptions.** Flag unfamiliar items for title, the broker, or counsel rather than trying to explain their legal effect.

**Connect title to transaction facts.** If the property is subject to an HOA, a solar agreement, a lease, a recorded access easement, or a payoff demand, make sure the appropriate reports, disclosures, and professionals are engaged.

**Document delivery and questions.** Preserve when the report was sent, what question was referred, and the factual response received. Do not write a memo declaring what a title exception “means” unless it is a verified quotation from an authorized professional and is attributed as such.

### Worked Example 2 — The driveway easement

The preliminary report for a Glendale home lists a recorded easement across part of the rear driveway. The buyer asks whether they can build a gate there. The agent should not answer based on intuition or a neighborhood observation. The appropriate response is to provide the recorded document or title contact, recommend that the buyer obtain qualified legal and design/permit advice, and preserve the question in the file. The easement may be ordinary, material, or both; its legal scope is not an agent’s call.

## 4. Vesting, Payoffs, and Names: Small Mismatches Can Delay Closing

Vesting describes how title is held. Names in the purchase agreement, loan documents, title report, and identification must align with the buyer’s intended vesting and the lender’s requirements. A married buyer, a buyer using a trust, a buyer acquiring with a partner, or an estate sale can involve documents that deserve early specialist review. The agent’s job is to surface the mismatch promptly—not to choose a legal form of ownership.

Payoffs also require care. A seller’s existing loan, lien, HOA balance, tax obligation, or recorded judgment may need a demand and clear instructions before closing. Never assure a seller that “the sale proceeds will cover everything” unless the authorized settlement statement and escrow professional confirm it. A shortfall may require a seller contribution, lender approval, or another solution beyond the agent’s authority.

**Worked Example 3 — The surprise payoff shortfall.** Two days before scheduled closing, escrow reports that the seller’s payoff and costs exceed projected proceeds by $8,400. The agent should immediately notify the responsible broker, ensure the seller receives factual escrow information, and avoid suggesting that escrow simply “take it from the buyer’s deposit.” The seller and qualified professionals must determine the lawful and authorized solution. The agent’s role is prompt coordination and accurate documentation.

## 5. Contingencies and Closing Readiness

An agent should maintain a contract calendar, but it must be a real calendar. Identify the exact event, deadline, person responsible, and current evidence. Do not mark an inspection contingency “removed” because the buyer said they feel good about the house; look for the applicable written action. Do not mark a loan condition “done” because a lender conversation sounded positive; obtain factual written status where appropriate.

Keep the parties informed without exposing unnecessary private information. A listing side may need to know whether the buyer is on track, but it does not need the buyer’s tax returns or full credit history. “Lender reports the appraisal has been received and underwriting conditions remain” can be sufficient. If a deadline requires an extension or other contract action, route the request promptly through the parties and broker; do not promise that either side will agree.

## 6. Signing, Funding, Recording, and Possession

These terms are often used loosely, so teach them precisely:

- **Signing**: parties execute documents required for the transaction.
- **Funding**: the lender disburses loan proceeds under its process.
- **Recording**: the appropriate deed or instrument is accepted for recording by the county recorder.
- **Closing**: the transaction reaches the contractual/escrow completion point described by the agreement and closing process.
- **Possession**: the buyer is entitled to occupy or control the property under the agreement; it may be linked to closing or separately negotiated.

The exact sequence varies. Do not hand over keys until the agreement, escrow instructions, and broker procedure support it. A verbal “we should be good” from one participant is not the same as confirmation that possession is authorized.

### Worked Example 4 — The early-key request

The buyer has movers scheduled for 9 a.m. but recording has not been confirmed. The seller casually says, “They can have the keys; it will record today.” The agent should not make an informal handoff. Contact the broker and escrow, confirm the parties’ written authority and risk allocation, and communicate the factual status. Early possession can create insurance, damage, access, and dispute issues. Convenience is not authority.

## 7. Communication That Moves Escrow Forward

Escrow friction often comes from a missing answer, not a hard legal issue. The agent can reduce friction by making each request concrete. Instead of writing “Any update on title?” ask “Has title confirmed whether the recorded solar agreement must be assumed, paid, or separately reviewed? If a document is needed from the seller, please identify it and the deadline.” Instead of forwarding a lender email with no context, say which contract milestone may be affected and what factual response is needed.

Copy only people who need the information. A large email chain can expose private financial data, create conflicting instructions, and make the file harder to follow. Separate client-facing explanations from broker consultations and preserve the final material communication in the transaction system. If a client wants the agent to forward a sensitive document, use the approved secure channel and verify the recipient.

Do not confuse responsiveness with authority. An agent may prompt an overdue report, coordinate a signing appointment, and make a deadline visible. An agent may not alter escrow instructions, tell a title officer to disregard a lien, or decide what a contract means when parties disagree. When a message raises an issue outside your role, make the handoff visible: identify the question, identify who is qualified to answer it, set a follow-up time, and tell the client what you will do next.

### Worked Example 5 — The unsigned addendum

At 4:30 p.m. on a contingency deadline, the listing agent says both sides “agreed” to extend, but the buyer has not yet signed the written extension. The buyer’s agent should not mark the deadline extended based on the conversation. They should immediately obtain the current written status, notify their broker if there is uncertainty, keep the buyer informed of the actual deadline, and avoid telling anyone that the extension is effective until the authorized document supports that statement. A polite verbal understanding may lead to a signed agreement; it is not automatically the agreement itself.

### Closing-day communication checklist

On the scheduled closing day, confirm rather than assume:

1. Which outstanding item belongs to escrow, title, lender, buyer, seller, or broker?
2. Has signing occurred, and are any signatures or corrections still required?
3. Has the lender confirmed funding status through the proper channel?
4. Has recording been confirmed by escrow/title when recording is the relevant event?
5. What does the agreement say about possession, keys, remotes, and access codes?
6. Has the client been told only facts that have actually been confirmed?

This checklist does not change a contract. It makes sure the agent knows what to ask before communicating a result.

One final habit is to distinguish an update from an instruction. An update reports a confirmed fact: “Escrow reports recording is pending.” An instruction attempts to direct action: “Release the keys now.” Agents should be especially cautious when a client turns an update into an instruction. Confirm the written agreement, the authorized decision-maker, and the broker’s procedure before acting. This distinction prevents an avoidable number of closing-day errors.

The same discipline improves client trust. When an answer is not yet known, say so plainly, identify the person who can verify it, and give a specific follow-up commitment. A calm factual update is stronger than a prediction that later proves wrong.

## 8. Post-Close Discipline

After closing, send the client the documents and contacts the brokerage is authorized to provide, confirm how keys or access devices were handled, and preserve the transaction record under brokerage policy. Do not discard files because a commission was paid. A post-close question about a disclosure, payment, possession, or recorded matter may arise months later.

Use a simple closing checklist: final settlement/closing documents received as appropriate; receipt of recording or closing confirmation; client delivery documented; keys, remotes, and access credentials accounted for; referral or vendor information handled according to policy; and any unresolved issue assigned to a responsible person. No checklist eliminates judgment, but it prevents avoidable omissions.

## 9. Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Escrow** | A process in which a neutral holder follows authorized instructions concerning funds and documents. |
| **Preliminary title report** | A title document identifying proposed coverage, requirements, and exceptions before a policy may issue. |
| **Vesting** | The manner in which legal title is held. |
| **Exception** | A matter not covered by a title policy or subject to stated limitations. |
| **Payoff demand** | A statement of an amount required to satisfy an existing obligation. |
| **Recording** | Acceptance of an instrument for entry in public county records. |
| **Funding** | Disbursement of loan proceeds under the lender's process. |
| **Possession** | Contractual right to occupy or control property. |

## 10. Exam and Practice Tips

- A preliminary title report is not an agent’s permission to give legal advice.
- Identify title issues, route them to qualified professionals, and document the referral.
- Signing, funding, recording, closing, and possession are different events.
- Never promise a closing or hand over keys based on an assumption.
- Use actual calendar dates and written transaction evidence.
- Protect buyer privacy while giving the other side factual status updates.
- Treat a disputed instruction or payoff shortfall as an escalation event.
